Before you start

The last domain. This topic opens Earth and space science, the lightest domain on the test (about twenty percent) and yet the one that leans most on what you have already studied. Almost nothing here is truly new: these are known mechanisms at a different scale.

The convection of Lesson 6.3, which explained boiling water in a pot, will explain the motion of whole continents. The density of 5.2 decides which plate sinks under which. And the scientific notation of 2.1 is essential again, because Earth’s age is measured in billions of years and numbers like that are written no other way.

How to study this lesson: in section 2, read the table of evidence before the theory, and ask yourself which clue convinces you most and why. The conclusion should be something you reach from the evidence, not something you are simply told.

1What lies beneath: Earth’s layers and convection

Earth is not a solid, uniform ball. It is built in layers of different makeup and state, and we know about those layers not from seeing them but mostly from how earthquake waves travel through the inside, a direct use of the waves from Lesson 6.3.

Those currents in the mantle are exactly the same mechanism that made hot water rise in a pot. Deep material heats up, its density goes down, and it rises; near the crust it cools, becomes denser, and sinks. The only difference from the pot is the scale: here one loop takes millions of years and drags whole continents.

Solid does not always mean rigid

The mantle is solid rock and yet it flows, so slowly that on a human scale you cannot notice it: a few centimeters a year (about an inch or two), roughly as fast as your fingernails grow. That slowness is why plate tectonics took so long to be accepted: no one can watch it happen directly.

2Evidence first, theory second

In the early 1900s, Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined and had drifted apart. The idea was rejected for decades, and it helps to understand why: his evidence was good, but it lacked a mechanism. No one knew what force could move a continent.

The four kinds of evidence for continental drift, what each shows, and how strong it is as proof
EvidenceWhat it showsStrength as proof
Continents fit togetherThe coasts of Africa and South America fit like puzzle piecesSuggestive, but on its own it could be chance
Shared fossilsFossils of the same land species appear on both sides of the oceanStrong: those species could not cross an ocean
Matching mountain rangesRock formations of the same age and type continue from one continent to anotherStrong: the match includes makeup and age
Where earthquakes and volcanoes areThey are not scattered at random: they line up in narrow, well-defined beltsDecisive: those belts trace the edges of the plates

Here is the skill the test checks, already practiced in Lesson 4.3 with evolution: several independent lines of evidence pointing to the same conclusion are much stronger than one alone. The fit of the continents proved nothing by itself. Together with the fossils, the rocks, and the earthquakes, the conclusion becomes hard to avoid.

The three kinds of plate boundary

Subduction deserves attention because it ties back to Topic 5. When an ocean plate and a continental plate collide, the ocean plate always sinks, because it is denser. It is the same rule as in Lesson 5.2: the denser material goes to the bottom. The material and the scale change, not the principle.

Ask whether magma is involved

The three boundaries do not cause the same events, and the test asks in exactly those terms. A transform boundary causes earthquakes but not volcanoes, because no material is rising. A convergent boundary causes both, plus mountain ranges. A divergent boundary makes new crust and is usually on the ocean floor, where it is hardly noticed. When a question describes an event and asks for the kind of boundary, the useful question is whether magma is involved.

3The rock cycle

Every rock belongs to one of three types, defined by how it formed, not by how it looks or how hard it is.

The three rock types by how they form, with examples and a feature you can recognize
TypeHow it formsExamplesRecognizable feature
IgneousMagma or lava cools and hardensGranite, basaltCrystals; no layers or fossils
SedimentarySediments pile up and are pressed together, layer on layerSandstone, limestoneVisible layers; the only type with fossils
MetamorphicAnother rock is changed by heat and pressure without meltingMarble, slateBands or sheets; squeezed crystals

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

What matters is not the classification itself but that the three types turn into one another through processes that never stop: erosion wears down any rock and makes sediment; burial presses it together; heat and pressure change it; and if the temperature keeps rising, everything melts and starts over as magma.

The rock cycle is a closed loop with no starting point

The test does not ask for the sequence from memory: it asks about specific changes (“a sedimentary rock put under heat and pressure becomes…”) or asks the question backward. Just keep in mind which process makes each type: melt and cool gives igneous, pile up and press gives sedimentary, heat and squeeze without melting gives metamorphic.

Only sedimentary rocks hold fossils

The reason is in the definition of the other two: in an igneous rock everything was once melted, and in a metamorphic rock heat and pressure destroy any remains. That single fact answers several test items and is the base of the next section: if fossils are in the sedimentary layers, those layers are the record of the past.

4Reading time in the layers

Sedimentary rocks are laid down in layers, one on top of another, and that gives the principle that orders all of Earth’s history: the principle of superposition. In a stack of layers that has not been disturbed, the lower layers are older than the upper ones, because they were laid down first.

A second rule, just as simple and very useful on the test, goes with superposition: anything that cuts across a layer is younger than that layer. If an igneous intrusion cuts through layers A, B, and C but stops below D, the conclusion is immediate: it formed after C and before D.

Index fossils

Some species lived for a short time but spread over wide areas. When their fossil shows up in two places far apart, it shows that both layers were laid down at the same time. These are index fossils, and they work like a time stamp that lets geologists match rock columns on different continents.

The geologic time scale

The numbers in this lesson go far beyond everyday experience, so they are written in scientific notation, as in Lesson 2.1:

Approximate geologic time scale with four milestones in scientific notation of years
EventAbout how long ago
Earth forms4.6 × 10⁹ years
First forms of life3.5 × 10⁹ years
Extinction of the non-bird dinosaurs6.6 × 10⁷ years
First modern humans3 × 10⁵ years

Comparing exponents is enough

Between 10⁹ and 10⁵ there are four orders of magnitude, a factor of ten thousand. Put another way: if Earth’s history were squeezed into one year, modern humans would show up in the last minutes of December 31. The test does not ask you to remember the numbers, but it does ask you to order and compare them.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Tectonic comes from the Greek tektonikós, about building, from téktōn, builder; it names the processes that build the structure of the crust. Igneous comes from the Latin ignis, fire, the root of ignition. Sediment, from sedere, to settle or sit, as in sedentary: what settles at the bottom. Metamorphic is made of meta-, change, and morphé, shape: changed in shape, like metamorphosis. Stratigraphy joins stratum, layer, with graphía, writing: the writing of the layers, which is exactly what you learned to read here. Subduction, from sub-, under, and ducere, to lead: the plate is led under the other. And convergent and divergent share vergere, to lean or turn, with the prefixes con-, together, and di-, apart.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what turned a rejected idea into an accepted theory.

📖 The ocean floor had the answer

When Alfred Wegener proposed in 1912 that the continents had moved, he presented a set of clues: the fit of the coasts, identical fossils separated by oceans, and rock formations that continued from one continent to another. Most geologists rejected the idea. Their main objection was not that the clues were false, but that Wegener could not explain what force could move a continent.

Things changed starting in the 1950s, when mapping of the ocean floor revealed a continuous underwater mountain range running down the middle of the Atlantic. When the ages of the rocks on the floor were measured, a regular pattern appeared: the youngest are next to that ridge, and they get steadily older toward the continents, on both sides and symmetrically.

The magnetic record gave a second check. Rocks on the ocean floor keep the direction of Earth’s magnetic field at the moment they harden, and that field has flipped several times. The bands of alternating direction lie symmetrically on both sides of the ridge, like mirror images of one pattern.

With these data, the idea became part of the theory of plate tectonics. Wegener’s clues were still the same; what was added was the missing mechanism and an independent check that no one had been looking for.

📘Key words

Mantle
Earth’s thickest layer: hot solid rock that flows very slowly.
Plate tectonics
The theory that Earth’s crust is broken into plates moved by convection in the mantle.
Subduction
One plate sinking under another at a convergent boundary; the denser plate sinks.
Igneous rock
Rock formed when magma or lava cools.
Sedimentary rock
Rock formed from piled-up, pressed sediment; the only type with fossils.
Metamorphic rock
Rock changed by heat and pressure without melting.
Superposition
In undisturbed layers, lower layers are older.
Index fossil
A fossil from a short-lived, widespread species, used to match layers of the same age.
