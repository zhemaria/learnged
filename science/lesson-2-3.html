Before you start

Lesson 2.1 taught you to write sizes and Lesson 2.2 to read them on an axis. This lesson teaches you to summarize them: what to do when instead of one value there are thirty, and you need to say in one number what they are like.

How to study this lesson: work small data sets (eight or ten values) by hand first, and only then use the calculator for bigger ones. Working by hand fixes the meaning of each measure; the calculator only saves time. Math Level 2 (mean, median, probability) and Math Level 3, Topic 7 (quartiles and box plots) are a good bridge.

1Four ways to summarize a data set

The first three measures tell where the center of the data is; the fourth, how spread out they are. None of them replaces the others, and the test often asks about the one you least expect.

The four measures that summarize a data set, how to find each one, and when it fits best
MeasureHow to find itWhen to use it
Mean (average)Add all the values and divide by how many there are.When the data are even, with no extreme values. It uses all the information, but it is the most fragile.
MedianPut the data in order and take the middle value. With an even count, average the two middle values.When there is an outlier. The median is not pulled by it.
ModeThe value that appears most often. There may be more than one, or none.With data that are not numbers (flower color, blood type) and with measurements that repeat a lot.
RangeSubtract the smallest value from the largest.To show at a glance how much the data vary. It says nothing about how the values in between are spread.

Example 1 · The four measures on one data set

A group measured the length of the same leaf ten times, in millimeters: 84, 86, 87, 87, 87, 89, 89, 90, 91, and 95.

1Mean: the sum is 885 and there are 10 values, so the mean is 88.5 mm.

2Median: the data are in order; the two middle values are 87 and 89, and their average is 88 mm.

3Mode: 87 appears three times, more than any other: 87 mm.

4Range: 95 − 84 = 11 mm.

✓What the comparison shows: the mean is larger than the median. That is no accident: the value 95, far from the rest, pulls the mean up without moving the median. If that measurement was a mistake, the median represents the real leaf better.

The outlier and which measure to choose

When one value is far from the others, the test often asks which measure “best represents” the data. The answer is almost always the median, and you should be able to say why: the mean uses the value of every data point, so one huge number moves it; the median only looks at the middle position, so it holds steady.

2Shape, center, and spread: the histogram

A histogram groups the data into intervals and shows how many values fall in each one. The height of a bar is not a value but a frequency (how many). What you read in a histogram is, above all, the shape of the data.

Shell length of 40 sea turtles on a nesting beach

The three things to read in a histogram, what you see, and what it means in an experiment
ElementWhat you look atWhat it means in an experiment
ShapeWhether it is symmetric, has a tail to one side, or has two peaks.Two peaks often mean two different groups were mixed and should be analyzed separately.
CenterWhere the data pile up; around which value.The most likely value of what was measured.
SpreadWhether the bars are in a few intervals or spread across many.How precise the procedure is: the narrower, the more repeatable.

3The five-number summary and the box plot

A box plot sums up a whole data set in five numbers: the minimum, the first quartile (Q1), the median, the third quartile (Q3), and the maximum. The box holds the middle half of the data; the whiskers, the ends.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Quart-, as in quarter and quartet, means four: the three quartiles split the ordered data into four parts with the same number of values each. The second quartile is the median itself. In the same family are percentile (one hundred parts) and decile (ten).

The five numbers for the ten measurements of the leaf, with their value and meaning
NumberValueWhat it tells you
Minimum84 mmThe smallest measurement.
First quartile (Q1)87 mmOne quarter of the data are below it. It is the median of the lower half: 84, 86, 87, 87, 87.
Median88 mmHalf of the data are below it and half above.
Third quartile (Q3)90 mmThree quarters of the data are below it. It is the median of the upper half: 89, 89, 90, 91, 95.
Maximum95 mmThe largest measurement.

What a long whisker gives away

Here the box is only 3 mm wide, while the right whisker stretches 5 mm. So the middle half of the measurements was very consistent, and one value moved away from the group. A whisker much longer than the other is the visual sign of an outlier. The first question a researcher should ask: is it a real effect or a measuring error?

4Spread as a measure of experimental error

Here we close a circle opened in Topic 1. There you studied the sources of error in a design; now you have the tools to measure them. The spread of repeated measurements is not a flaw of the experiment: it is the experiment telling you about itself.

Precision and accuracy in a set of measurements, and what each case reveals
CaseWhat the data look likeWhat it says about the experiment
Precise and accurateSmall range, with the center close to the true value.The procedure is repeatable and the instrument is well calibrated.
Precise but not accurateSmall range, but the center is always off from the true value.A systematic error: usually an instrument that is not calibrated and repeats the same shift every time.
Not preciseLarge range, data very spread out.Random error: the technique is not controlled, or the instrument is not sensitive enough.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Repeating does not fix a systematic error

More measurements reduce random error, because the ups and downs tend to cancel out. But if the scale always reads two grams too high, a thousand measurements give a mean that is still two grams too high. Repeating improves precision, not accuracy; calibration fixes accuracy.

5Counting possibilities

The last tool of the topic answers a different question: not what the data look like, but how many different outcomes are possible. In biology it shows up all the time, because it is the base of genetic calculations.

The counting principle

If one choice can be made in m ways and another, independent choice in n ways, both together can be made in m × n ways. The rule extends to as many steps as you need: multiply them all.

Permutations and combinations

When you choose some items from a group, everything depends on one question: does the order matter?

Permutation and combination: the question that tells them apart and a science example of each
IdeaDoes the order matter?Science example
PermutationYes. A different order gives a different result.The steps of a lab procedure: staining then covering is not the same as covering then staining. Five steps can be put in 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120 orders, and only one is right.
CombinationNo. Only which items were chosen matters.Choosing 3 samples out of 8 to analyze: it does not matter which one is picked first. There are (8 × 7 × 6) ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 56 possible choices.

Example 2 · Counting in genetics

How many different gametes (sex cells) can a living thing make if it has three genes with two alleles each?

1Each gene gives a choice with two possibilities: the dominant allele or the recessive one.

2The three choices are independent, so the counting principle applies.

32 × 2 × 2 = 8 different gametes, the eight shown by the tree.

✓The general rule: with n genes of two alleles, the number of different gametes is 2 to the power n. Four genes give 16; five give 32. This count comes back in Topic 4 with genetic crosses and the Punnett square.

🧮 nPr and nCr on the TI-30XS

The calculator counts for you with the prb key: choose nPr (order matters) or nCr (order does not matter). For 3 samples out of 8: type 8, prb, choose nCr, 3, enter → 56. Many students mix up P and C: P = permutation (order matters).

6Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how the authors decide which measure to report.

📖 Ten measurements of the same piece

A team of students found the density of an aluminum block by repeating the measurement ten times with the same procedure. The values, in grams per cubic centimeter, were: 2.68, 2.71, 2.69, 2.70, 2.72, 2.70, 2.69, 2.71, 2.70, and 3.15.

Nine of the ten were packed closely between 2.68 and 2.72. The tenth, 3.15, stood out sharply. Checking the lab notebook, the team found that this time the block had not been fully dried after being put in water, so the water stuck to it added mass without adding volume.

The team found both measures of center. The mean of all ten was 2.745 g/cm³; the median, 2.70 g/cm³. Leaving out the faulty measurement and averaging the other nine gave exactly 2.70 g/cm³.

The report gave the median as the result, with a note that recorded the discarded measurement and why it was discarded. The team argued that removing a value without a record would have been manipulation, while removing it with a written reason is part of the scientific procedure.

📘Key words

Mean
The sum of the values divided by how many there are.
Median
The middle value of the ordered data.
Mode
The value that appears most often.
Range
The largest value minus the smallest.
Outlier
A value far from the rest of the data.
Quartile
One of the three values that split ordered data into four equal parts.
Precision
How close repeated measurements are to each other.
Accuracy
How close a measurement is to the true value.
Permutation / combination
A choice where the order matters / where it does not.
