Lesson 7.2 · The atmosphere, weather, and climate

Earth’s envelope of air: its layers, the water cycle, the difference between weather and climate, the greenhouse effect, and how to read a long data series.

Before you start

From the inside out. Lesson 7.1 studied Earth from the inside. This one comes up to the surface and looks at the thin layer of gases around it, where everything we call weather and climate happens.

Several earlier lessons pay off here. The radiation of Lesson 6.3 explains the greenhouse effect. The convection that moved the mantle in 7.1 explains the winds. And above all, the positive feedback of Lesson 3.3 comes back: the loop that amplifies instead of balancing, taught with clotting and the forest, is the central mechanism of this lesson. It does not need explaining again, only recognizing.

1The atmosphere and its layers

Note on the data: the numbers in this lesson are examples to explain how to read the data, not reference values. Current climate data and the CO₂ and temperature records can be found at NOAA Climate.gov and from the National Weather Service, which has data for each city.

The atmosphere is a layer of gases held in place by gravity. Its makeup is worth knowing, because the test asks about it and it often surprises people: nitrogen is by far the most common gas, about 78 %, followed by oxygen at 21 %. Everything else (argon, carbon dioxide, water vapor) fits in the last 1 %.

That 1 % runs the climate

Carbon dioxide is a tiny share of the atmosphere and yet it decides much of the planet’s temperature. A small ingredient can have a huge effect: the same logic as Lesson 4.1, where changing a single DNA base changed a whole protein.

The four main layers of the atmosphere, from lowest to highest, with what sets each one apart
LayerPositionWhat happens there
TroposphereLowest, touching the surfaceHolds almost all the water vapor. Weather happens here: clouds, rain, wind
StratosphereAbove the troposphereHolds the ozone layer, which absorbs much of the ultraviolet radiation
MesosphereAbove the stratosphereWhere most meteors burn up
ThermosphereThe outermost of the fourVery thin air. The northern and southern lights (auroras) happen here

The ozone layer ties straight back to the spectrum in Lesson 6.3: it absorbs ultraviolet radiation, the first region of the spectrum with enough energy to damage cells. Without that layer, all of it would reach the surface.

2The water cycle

The planet’s water is not used up or made: it circulates. It has been the same water for billions of years, changing state and place, and every process that moves it is one of the changes of state studied in Lesson 5.2.

The processes of the water cycle with the change of state each involves and where the water goes
ProcessChange of stateWhere the water goes
EvaporationLiquid to gasFrom the surface into the air
TranspirationLiquid to gasFrom plants into the air
CondensationGas to liquidForms clouds
PrecipitationNone, or gas to solid if it snowsFrom clouds to the ground
RunoffNoneOver the surface, back to the ocean
InfiltrationNoneDown into the ground

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The Sun drives the cycle

That is the answer to a common test item. The energy that evaporates ocean water is sunlight; without it the cycle would stop. Notice also that evaporation and transpiration are the same change of state, told apart only by where the water comes from: water and soil surfaces for the first, plants for the second.

3Weather and climate are not the same

The difference is one of time scale, and only that. First think of the temperatures where you live, which you know from experience; then read the table.

Weather and climate compared by time scale, what each describes, how predictable it is, and examples
CriterionWeatherClimate
Time scaleHours, days, a week at mostAverages over thirty years or more
What it describesThe state of the air at a given momentA region’s usual pattern over the long term
How predictableReliable a few days aheadDescribed with trends, not daily forecasts
Example“Tomorrow in Chicago: storms and 54 °F (12 °C)”“Winters in Chicago are cold and snowy”

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The example that clears up the most common mix-up

An unusually cold winter in Chicago does not contradict a global warming trend, because the two statements are on different scales: one describes a single data point and the other an average over decades for the whole planet. Mixing them up is like saying a city’s climate has changed because it is raining today. The useful comparison is from Lesson 2.3: a single value is not the mean, and a mean does not predict tomorrow’s value.

4The greenhouse effect

This mechanism can be explained entirely with the radiation of Lesson 6.3. Follow it step by step, because each step depends on the one before.

The mechanism, step by step

1Sunlight arrives. Much of it is visible light, with short wavelengths, which passes through the atmosphere easily.

2The surface absorbs it and warms up.

3Earth gives that energy off again, but no longer as visible light: it gives it off as infrared radiation, with longer wavelengths, which we feel as heat.

4This is the key. Certain gases in the air (carbon dioxide, methane, water vapor) let visible light through but absorb infrared. They hold that energy and send it out again in all directions, including back down.

5Result. Part of the heat that would have escaped to space stays in the system, and the planet’s average temperature is higher than it would be with no atmosphere.

The greenhouse effect is natural and essential

Without it, the planet’s average temperature would be far below freezing and life as we know it would not be possible. The test carefully separates the effect itself from its intensification: what changes the climate is not that the effect exists, but that the amount of the gases that cause it has gone up. An answer choice that calls the greenhouse effect harmful in itself is usually the wrong one.

The loop that amplifies

Lesson 3.3 covered two kinds of feedback loop: negative, which reverses a change and keeps things steady (body temperature, blood sugar), and positive, which amplifies it (blood clotting, childbirth). We said then that a positive loop needs an outside event to stop it, because it does not stop by itself.

The climate is full of positive loops, and that is why it is a concern. The clearest case is ice: icy surfaces are light-colored and reflect much of the sunlight that hits them. If the ice shrinks, it uncovers ocean, which is dark and absorbs much more sunlight; absorbing more, it warms more; warming more, it melts more ice. Each turn of the loop strengthens the next. It has exactly the shape of the clotting loop, with different content. And of the forest studied with the ecosystem simulator: if the temperature passes a certain threshold, the forest dies and releases the carbon it stored, which raises the temperature even more.

Recognizing the shape of the loop is the skill being tested

Not memorizing climate examples. For any description there is only one question: does the system’s response reverse the first change or strengthen it? If it reverses it, the loop is negative. If it strengthens it, it is positive. “Negative” does not mean harmful, as Lesson 3.3 warned.

The currents that spread heat around

Heat does not stay where it arrives. Ocean currents and winds carry it from the equator, which gets the most direct sunlight, toward the poles. Both work by convection: water or air warms up, becomes less dense, rises or moves away, cools, and comes back. It is the same engine that moved the mantle in 7.1 and boiled water in a pot in 6.3, now on a planetary scale.

That leads to a fact the test likes to use: two cities at the same latitude can have very different climates if one is washed by a warm current and the other is not. Latitude does not decide everything.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Atmosphere comes from the Greek atmós, vapor, and sphaira, sphere: the sphere of vapor around the planet. Troposphere has tropos, turn or change, because it is the layer where the air mixes and changes; stratosphere, stratum, layer, the same root as stratigraphy in Lesson 7.1, because the air there lies in steady layers; thermosphere, thermós, heat, seen in Topic 5. Evaporation comes from vapor, and transpiration from trans-, through, and spirare, to breathe: water leaves through the plant. Precipitation, from praeceps, falling headlong. And climate comes from the Greek klíma, slope or tilt, referring to the angle at which the Sun’s rays reach each region, which is exactly what sets its usual temperature.

5Reading a long time series

This is the most tested section of the lesson: five of the twelve quiz questions come from here. What you practice is not climate science but reading trend graphs, a skill that works just as well for a sales or population graph.

Three rules for reading a long time series, what each says, and the mistake it prevents
RuleWhat it saysMistake it prevents
Trend versus single valueNo single point sets the direction of the wholeDeciding the trend has reversed because one year went down
Choose the span carefullyA short span can suggest the opposite of the full seriesDrawing conclusions from five years when one hundred fifty are available
Correlation versus causeTwo series rising together does not by itself prove one causes the otherClaiming cause when the data only show that they match

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

About the third rule, with care

Two series matching is not enough to claim cause: that is true, and checking it is what practice SP.4 requires. But the opposite does not follow either: when the mechanism behind the relationship is also known (and for the greenhouse effect it is; it was described in the previous section), the conclusion no longer rests on the match alone. It is the same lesson as 7.1: Wegener’s clues needed a mechanism to become a theory.

Extrapolating is the costliest mistake in this section

Extending the line into the future in your head seems reasonable, but saying what value the series will have in thirty years goes beyond what the graph shows. The right answer is usually worded carefully: if the trend continued, you would expect such and such. Be wary of options that state the future without an “if.”

6Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what the record allows us to say and what it does not.

📖 Air trapped in ice

In Antarctica and Greenland, the snow that falls each year never fully melts. It piles up and is pressed under the weight of the layers above until it turns into ice, and in that process tiny bubbles of air are sealed in. Each bubble keeps a sample of the atmosphere from the moment it was sealed.

By drilling into the ice and pulling out long, continuous cylinders (ice cores), research teams get an ordered sequence: the upper layers are recent years and the lower ones are older. The principle that lets them order the layers is the same one used for layers of sedimentary rock.

By analyzing the air in the bubbles, scientists have rebuilt the carbon dioxide level over hundreds of thousands of years. The records show wide swings, with periods of high and low levels that alternate with the ice-age cycles. In the last decades of the record, the level is above the highest values reached in those earlier cycles, and the rise has happened over a much shorter time.

The teams themselves point out the method’s limits. Dating the deepest layers has a margin of error that grows with age, the air takes some time to be sealed in, and the drill sites are few. That is why the results are checked against other independent records, such as ocean sediments and tree rings.

📘Key words

Troposphere
The lowest layer of the atmosphere, where weather happens.
Ozone layer
Part of the stratosphere that absorbs much of the Sun’s ultraviolet radiation.
Water cycle
The closed loop of evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff, and infiltration, driven by the Sun.
Weather
The state of the atmosphere over hours or days.
Climate
A region’s usual pattern, averaged over thirty years or more.
Greenhouse effect
Gases that let sunlight in but absorb infrared keep part of Earth’s heat; it is natural and essential.
Positive feedback
A loop in which the response strengthens the first change.
Extrapolate
Extend a trend beyond the data; a careful statement says “if the trend continued.”
