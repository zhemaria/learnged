Lesson 3.1 · Cell theory, organelles, and the cell membrane

Every living thing is made of cells: what is inside one, how it divides, and what its membrane lets through.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.7 · Scientific theoriesSP.1 · Understanding science texts

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • State the three statements of cell theory and the evidence behind each one.
  • Explain how mitosis makes the third statement come true, and why the DNA is copied before it is shared.
  • Find the main organelles in a diagram and match each one with its job.
  • Tell apart animal and plant cells, and prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
  • Explain diffusion, osmosis, and active transport in terms of the concentration gradient.
  • Predict what happens to a cell placed in a hypotonic, isotonic, or hypertonic solution.

1Cell theory and the evidence behind it

Cell theory is one of the clearest examples of what the test means by a scientific theory: not a guess, but an explanation built on two centuries of observations and supported today by all of them.

The three statements of cell theory, the observations behind each one, and what follows from it
StatementWhat observation supports itWhat follows
All living things are made of cells.As microscopes got better in the 1800s, every tissue that was examined, from plants or animals, turned out to be made of cells.The cell is the building block shared by all life.
The cell is the basic unit of structure and function.Each cell carries out the functions of life by itself: it gets energy, responds to its surroundings, and reproduces.To explain a living thing, you first have to explain its cells.
Every cell comes from another cell that already existed.No controlled experiment has ever produced cells from nonliving matter.Life does not appear out of nothing (no “spontaneous generation”). Life is continuous.

Why this is a theory and not a hypothesis

The test keeps the two words apart. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation that still has to be tested; a theory is an explanation that has already passed repeated tests and ties together a whole body of observations. In everyday speech, “theory” suggests doubt; in science it means just the opposite.

2Cell division: how the third statement comes true

The third statement says that every cell comes from another cell, but it does not say how. The answer is cell division. Without it, cell theory would be incomplete: a claim about where cells come from, with no mechanism behind it.

Cells divide for three reasons: so the body can grow, to repair damaged tissue, and to replace cells that wear out. Skin and blood are renewed all through life.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Chromosome: from chromo-, color, and -some, body. The name comes from the fact that these structures soaked up the dyes of the 1800s and so became visible under the microscope. A chromosome is DNA packed tightly so it can be shared out without tangling. The same root -some appears in ribosome, and chromo- in chromatic and monochrome. A human cell has 46 chromosomes, in 23 pairs.

Before it divides, the cell copies all of its DNA. That step makes sure each new cell gets a full set of instructions and not half of them. Only then is the copy shared out.

The stages of cell division by mitosis, what happens in each one, and why it is needed
StageWhat happensWhy it is needed
InterphaseThe cell grows, does its jobs, and copies all of its DNA.Without the copy, there would not be two full sets to share.
ProphaseThe copied DNA packs tightly and the chromosomes become visible. The covering of the nucleus breaks down.A long, loose thread would tangle when shared; packed tight, it can be moved.
MetaphaseThe copied chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell.Lining up makes sure the sharing is exact and not random.
AnaphaseThe copies of each chromosome pull apart and move to opposite ends.This is the sharing itself: one full set goes to each side.
Telophase and cytokinesisTwo nuclei form again and the cytoplasm splits into two cells.It ends the process: one cell has become two separate cells.

Example 1 · Counting chromosomes before and after

A human skin cell, with 46 chromosomes, divides by mitosis. How many chromosomes will each daughter cell have?

1During interphase, the 46 chromosomes are copied. The cell now has two copies of each one.

2In anaphase, one copy of each chromosome goes to each end.

3Each daughter cell gets a full set: 46 chromosomes, the same as the original cell.

✓What to remember: mitosis makes two cells that are genetically identical to the original, with the same number of chromosomes. No information is lost or gained. That is why it works for growth and repair, where what you need is more of the same.

The copy comes first, not after

The most common mistake is to picture the cell splitting in two and then each half filling in what it is missing. That is not what happens. First all the DNA is copied, and only then is it shared out. If the order were reversed, each daughter cell would get half the instructions and could not work.

A second way to divide comes in Topic 4

Egg and sperm cells cannot form by mitosis: if each carried 46 chromosomes, together they would give 92, and in the next generation 184. Meiosis solves that problem by making cells with half, 23 chromosomes. That is also why, in a Punnett square, each parent gives only one allele of each pair.

3Organelles and their jobs

An organelle is a structure inside the cell that does one special job. The word itself says it: a small organ. Comparing the cell to a factory helps you remember the jobs, as long as you remember it is only a comparison, not a description.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Almost every name can be taken apart. Mitochondrion: from mito-, thread, and -chondrion, grain, for how it looks under the microscope. Chloroplast: from chloro-, green, and -plast, formed body: the green body. Endoplasmic reticulum: reticulum is a small net, endo- is inside, and plasm is the content of the cell: a net inside the cytoplasm. Ribosome: ribo-, for the ribonucleic acid (RNA) it is made of, and -some, body. Cytoplasm: cyto- is cell. Five roots solve five names.

The main organelles, their job in the cell, and the matching part of a factory
OrganelleJobFactory comparison
NucleusHolds the DNA and directs the cell’s activity.The main office, where the plans are kept.
MitochondrionCarries out cellular respiration and releases the energy in glucose.The power plant.
RibosomeBuilds proteins following the instructions in the DNA.The assembly line.
Endoplasmic reticulumCarries the substances the cell makes.The hallways and conveyor belts.
ChloroplastCarries out photosynthesis. Only in plant cells and algae.The solar panel that powers the factory.
Cell membraneControls what enters and leaves the cell.The door with access control.
Cell wallGives the cell stiffness and shape. Only in plants, fungi, and many bacteria.The outer wall of the building.
Central vacuoleStores water and other substances, and keeps the pressure that holds the plant up.The water tank.

4Animal, plant, and prokaryotic cells

The differences are not decoration: each one fits a different way of living. A plant cell makes its own food and does not move, and that explains its three special structures.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells compared on five features
FeatureProkaryoteEukaryote
NucleusNone. The DNA floats free in the cytoplasm.Yes, with its own covering that keeps the DNA apart from the rest.
Organelles with a membraneNone.Mitochondria, reticulum, and chloroplasts in plant cells.
SizeSmall: about 1 × 10⁻⁶ m.Larger: between 1 × 10⁻⁵ and 1 × 10⁻⁴ m.
ExamplesBacteria and archaea.Animals, plants, fungi, and protists.
OrganizationAlmost always one-celled.One-celled or many-celled.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The root solves the pair

-karyote comes from the Greek word for kernel, the nucleus. Pro- means before: a prokaryote is “before the nucleus,” a cell that does not have one yet. Eu- means true: a eukaryote has a “true nucleus.” If you know the pair pro- / eu-, you will not mix them up again.

5The membrane and the concentration gradient

The cell membrane is semipermeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Because of that, the cell can keep its inside different from the outside, which, in the end, is what being alive means.

The key word of this section is gradient: the difference in concentration between two areas. Substances tend to move on their own from where there is a lot to where there is little, until both sides are even. That movement costs no energy; moving the other way does.

The three ways substances cross the membrane, what moves and in which direction, and whether energy is needed
MechanismWhat moves and whereDoes it need energy?
DiffusionAny dissolved substance, from where it is more concentrated to where it is less.No. It happens on its own.
OsmosisOnly water, across the membrane, toward the side with more solute.No. It is the diffusion of water.
Active transportSubstances the cell needs to collect, from where there is little to where there is already a lot.Yes. The cell spends ATP.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Water follows the salt

Osmosis is often remembered backward. Water does not move toward where there is less water “looking for balance”; it moves toward the side with more solute. Both statements say the same thing (more solute means less water), but the test words its questions in terms of solute concentration. Always reason that way: water follows the salt.

6Predicting what a solution does to a cell

This is the level the test checks: not recognizing a definition, but predicting a result. The three possible cases come from comparing the solute concentration outside the cell with the one inside.

Example 2 · A prediction, step by step

Red blood cells are placed in distilled water, which has no salt at all. What will happen?

1Compare the solute: inside the blood cell there are salts; outside, none. The outside is hypotonic.

2Water follows the salt, so it will go into the blood cell.

3The blood cell will swell. Since it has no cell wall to hold it, it can burst.

✓The comparison that completes the idea: a plant cell in the same distilled water also takes in water, but its cell wall holds against the pressure, and the cell becomes firm instead of bursting. That is exactly what keeps a well-watered plant standing, and what is lost when it wilts.

The prefixes give the answer before the reasoning

hypo- means below, hyper- above, and iso- equal. A hypotonic solution has less solute than the cell; a hypertonic one, more. Once you know where the solute is, water moves toward it. The same prefixes appear in hypothermia, hypertension, and isothermal.

7Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how it explains an everyday practice with a cell mechanism.

📖 Why salt keeps food from spoiling

Salting meat or fish to keep it is thousands of years old, much older than any knowledge of cells. Even so, why it works is fully explained by osmosis.

The microorganisms that spoil food, bacteria and fungi, are cells surrounded by a semipermeable membrane. When the food is covered with salt, the liquid around those cells has a much higher solute concentration than their inside: it becomes hypertonic.

Under those conditions, water leaves the microbe cells. As they lose water, their inside becomes more concentrated, the reactions that keep them alive stop, and the microorganism stops multiplying. You do not need to destroy it: taking away the water it needs to work is enough.

The same idea explains why sugar keeps jam from spoiling, why a salad gives off liquid when you salt it, and why seawater does not quench thirst: drinking it puts more salt into the body than the body can get rid of, and as a result the cells give up water instead of getting it.

📘Key words

Cell theory
All living things are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; every cell comes from another cell.
Organelle
A structure inside the cell with a special job.
Chromosome
DNA packed tightly so it can be shared when the cell divides.
Mitosis
Cell division that makes two cells identical to the original.
Prokaryote / eukaryote
A cell without a true nucleus / with a true nucleus.
Semipermeable
Letting only some substances through.
Gradient
A difference in concentration between two areas.
Osmosis
The movement of water across a membrane toward the side with more solute.
Active transport
Moving a substance against its gradient, using ATP.
Hypotonic / isotonic / hypertonic
A solution with less / the same / more solute than the cell.
