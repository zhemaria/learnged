Lesson 3.2 · Photosynthesis and cellular respiration: how energy flows

The chloroplast stores energy and the mitochondrion releases it: two equations, one cycle, and a limit.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.6 · Expressing science informationSP.3 · Reasoning from data

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Write and read the overall equations of photosynthesis and cellular respiration.
  • Name the reactants and products of each process and where each one happens.
  • Explain how the two processes depend on each other and why neither would be enough alone.
  • Trace a carbon atom around the cycle, from the air back to the air.
  • Read a graph of the rate of photosynthesis against light and extend it past the saturation point.

Before you start

Lesson 3.1 introduced the chloroplast and the mitochondrion as two names on a list of organelles. This lesson puts them to work: they are the two rooms where energy enters life and where it is released.

There is a reason the equations appear here and not in a chemistry topic. A chemical equation is, as practice SP.6 says, a way of expressing a process with numbers and symbols: the same skill that in Topic 2 took you from a table to a graph now takes you from a biological process to an expression with numbers. You do not memorize it: you read it.

How to study this lesson: build the equations with cards before writing them. Moving the pieces to one side of the arrow or the other fixes the difference between reactant and product much better than copying the finished equation.

1The two overall equations

A chemical equation is read from left to right. To the left of the arrow are the reactants, what is used up; to the right, the products, what you get. The large numbers in front of each formula tell how many molecules take part.

Photosynthesis and cellular respiration compared: reactants, products, where they happen, and the role of energy
FeaturePhotosynthesisCellular respiration
ReactantsCarbon dioxide, water, and light.Glucose and oxygen.
ProductsGlucose and oxygen.Carbon dioxide, water, and usable energy (ATP).
Where it happensIn the chloroplast. Only in plants, algae, and some bacteria.In the mitochondrion. In plants and in animals, every one of them.
EnergyTakes it in and stores it in the bonds of glucose.Releases it so the cell can use it.
When it happensOnly when there is light.All the time, day and night.

The most common mistake on the test

Believing that plants “do photosynthesis” and animals “breathe,” as if they were two alternatives. They are not. Plants do both: they make glucose in the chloroplast during the day and burn it in the mitochondrion at all hours, just like any animal. Photosynthesis is what plants do in addition, not instead.

A check that saves memorizing

The two equations are the same one read in opposite directions. If you remember one, you get the other by swapping reactants and products. One more clue: photosynthesis needs light, so light goes on the left; respiration gives energy, so energy goes on the right.

2Follow the carbon

A carbon atom is neither made nor destroyed: it travels. The same atom that today is part of the carbon dioxide in the air may tomorrow be part of a leaf, then of a muscle, and later go back to the air. That trip is the carbon cycle, and the test checks it by asking about one specific stage.

Example 1 · The trip of one atom

Follow a carbon atom from a CO₂ molecule in the air until it goes back to the air.

1The leaf takes in the CO₂ and, through photosynthesis, builds that carbon into a glucose molecule.

2A plant eater eats the leaf. The carbon becomes part of its body, or is released right away if the animal burns that glucose.

3When the animal dies, decomposers use its remains as food.

4As they respire, the decomposers give the carbon back to the air as CO₂, and the cycle starts again.

✓There are shortcuts, too: carbon does not always make the full trip. The plant itself respires, so part of the carbon it just took in goes back to the air without passing through any animal. And another part can be locked away for millions of years if remains turn into coal or oil. Burning those fuels puts back, all at once, carbon that had been out of the cycle since before humans existed.

3The rate of photosynthesis and its limit

If light is a reactant, you would expect more light to give more photosynthesis. That is what happens, but only up to a point. The graph below is one of the most common on the test, and you read it with the same five steps of Lesson 2.2.

Rate of photosynthesis against light intensity

Example 2 · An extrapolation question

If, starting at 8 units of light, the intensity were doubled to 16, what would happen to the rate of photosynthesis?

1Look at the shape of the curve after 8: it has leveled off. Between 8 and 12 the rate barely changes.

2Light is no longer what limits the process. Another reactant (the CO₂ available) or the temperature is now the bottleneck.

3So you can expect the rate to stay about the same, not to double.

✓How to word the answer: “If the trend shown continues, the rate will stay near 50.” Keep the condition, as in Lesson 2.2, because there are no measurements past 12.

The limiting factor

When a process depends on several reactants, its speed is always set by the one in shortest supply. Adding more of the others does nothing. It is the same logic as a factory with plenty of materials but only one worker: hiring more suppliers does not raise production.

4Science reading

A short science text written for this level. It has numbers: read it with the checklist from Lesson 2.2.

📖 A greenhouse test

A farm research team wanted to find out what limited the growth of its tomato plants in winter. It set up four identical greenhouses and changed only one condition in each, keeping everything else the same.

In the first greenhouse, the control, the plants grew with natural winter light and the normal CO₂ level of the air. In the second, the light was doubled with lamps. In the third, the light stayed natural but the CO₂ level was raised. In the fourth, both were raised at once.

After six weeks, the dry mass of the plants was measured. The control group reached 42 grams per plant. The greenhouse with extra light reached 61 grams. The one with extra CO₂, 46. And the one with both, 78 grams.

The team concluded that in winter the main limiting factor was light, since raising it alone gave a large improvement, while raising only the CO₂ barely changed the result. It warned, however, that the conclusion applies only to the winter conditions tested, and that the experiment cannot predict what would happen in summer.

📘Key words

Reactant / product
What a reaction uses up / what it makes.
Photosynthesis
Making glucose and oxygen from carbon dioxide, water, and light, in the chloroplast.
Cellular respiration
Burning glucose with oxygen to release usable energy (ATP), in the mitochondrion.
ATP
The molecule that carries usable energy inside the cell.
Carbon cycle
The path carbon follows between the air, living things, the soil, and the oceans.
Decomposer
A living thing, such as a fungus or bacterium, that feeds on dead remains.
Saturation point
Where more of a factor no longer raises the rate.
Limiting factor
The factor in shortest supply, which sets the speed of a process.
