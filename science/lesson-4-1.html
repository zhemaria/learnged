Before you start

The second half of life science starts here. Topic 3 answered what a cell is and how it gets energy. Topic 4 answers how information passes from one generation to the next, how populations change over time, and how living things are organized in an ecosystem.

This lesson starts at the smallest level, the molecule; 4.2 moves up to the individual, and 4.3 to the population. Keep that order in mind: molecule, individual, population. Each step explains the next one.

How to study this lesson: work with the model before the formal explanation. Pair the base cards and discover for yourself that only two pairs are possible; the rule will be obvious once you have checked it yourself.

1The structure of DNA and base pairing

DNA is a ladder twisted around itself: that is the double helix. The two sides of the ladder are made of sugar and phosphate; each rung is two bases facing each other.

There are only four bases (adenine, thymine, cytosine, and guanine), and they do not pair just any way. The rule has no exceptions:

The base-pairing rule

Adenine always pairs with thymine, and cytosine always with guanine: A with T and C with G. That gives you something very useful: if you know one strand, the other one is completely set. You do not memorize it; you work it out.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

DNA stands for deoxyribonucleic acid. The three pieces say what it is: nucleic, from the nucleus, where it was found; ribo-, for ribose, the sugar in the sides of the ladder; and deoxy-, from de-, without, plus oxygen, because that ribose is missing one oxygen. RNA is the same name without the deoxy-: ribonucleic acid, with the full ribose. The difference between the two names is one atom, and the name tells you which.

2DNA and RNA: two similar molecules with different jobs

DNA stores the information and never leaves the nucleus. RNA is a working copy that does leave: it carries the message to the ribosome and then breaks down. Think of an office file: the original is kept locked up, and what goes around the office is a photocopy.

DNA and RNA compared: strands, sugar, bases, location, and job
FeatureDNARNA
Number of strandsTwo, wound into a double helix.One.
SugarDeoxyribose.Ribose.
BasesA, T, C, G.A, U, C, G. Uracil takes the place of thymine.
Where it isIn the nucleus, which it does not leave.Made in the nucleus; goes out to the cytoplasm.
JobStore the information permanently.Carry the message to the ribosome to build the protein.

The only letter that changes

Going from DNA to RNA, thymine is replaced by uracil. Where DNA says T, RNA says U. It is the most common mistake in transcription exercises: you copy the T out of habit, and everything after it is wrong. Always check the finished sequence for any T that slipped through.

3From gene to protein

The information in DNA is not used directly: it is copied and then translated. These are two steps with precise names and different places.

Messenger RNA is read three letters at a time. Each group of three bases is called a codon and stands for one specific amino acid, or for the order to stop. The amino acids linked together make the protein.

Example 1 · From a DNA strand to a protein

Start with the DNA template strand: TAC · GGA · CTC · ATC

1Transcription. Write the matching sequence, with uracil instead of thymine: A with U, T with A, C with G, G with C.

2The messenger RNA is: AUG · CCU · GAG · UAG

3Translation. Look up each codon in the table: AUG is methionine, CCU is proline, GAG is glutamic acid, and UAG is the order to stop.

✓Result: a chain of three amino acids (methionine, proline, and glutamic acid), and the reading ends there. The stop codon adds no amino acid: it only says where to stop.

Read in threes, without shifting

Codons are counted from the start and always in threes, without skipping or repeating any base. If a letter is lost while copying, every codon after it shifts and the protein comes out nothing like the right one. Separate the groups of three with a dot before translating, as in the example.

4Point mutations

A point mutation is a change in a single base. It seems small, and sometimes it is, but it can change the protein completely.

Three possible effects of a point mutation on the protein
Possible effectWhat happensWhat it does to the protein
No effectThe codon changes, but the new one stands for the same amino acid. Several codons code for the same amino acid.The protein is identical. The mutation goes unnoticed.
One amino acid changesThe new codon stands for a different amino acid, as when GAG becomes GUG.The protein is complete, but it may work worse, the same, or even better.
Early stopThe new codon turns out to be a stop order where there was none.The protein is cut short and almost always stops working.

A mutation is not “good” or “bad” by itself

The test checks this idea often, and it goes against intuition. The effect of a mutation depends on the environment the living thing lives in. The same variant can be harmful in one place and helpful in another, as you will see in this lesson’s reading and, above all, in Lesson 4.3 with natural selection.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice that the final conclusion depends on the environment, not only on the molecule.

📖 One letter that changes a life

Hemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The gene for it has, in most people, a codon that stands for glutamic acid at one particular place in the chain.

In some people that codon has one different base, and the amino acid built in is no longer glutamic acid but valine. The change affects one link in a chain of hundreds, but that is enough to change how the whole molecule behaves: when it gives up its oxygen, this hemoglobin tends to stick to itself and form stiff fibers.

The red blood cells that contain it lose their round shape and become long and curved, like a sickle, which gives the disease its name: sickle cell anemia. These cells move through the body with difficulty, block the smallest vessels, and are destroyed too soon, which causes pain and anemia.

For years, the puzzling question was why such a harmful variant had not disappeared. The answer came from looking at where it is found: the variant is more common in regions where malaria is common. A person who inherits the allele from only one parent does not develop the disease and is also less likely to get sick from malaria. In those regions, carrying one copy is an advantage; in regions without malaria, that advantage disappears.

📘Key words

DNA
Deoxyribonucleic acid: the double-stranded molecule that stores genetic information.
RNA
Ribonucleic acid: a single-stranded working copy that carries the message to the ribosome.
Base
One of the “letters” of DNA (A, T, C, G) or RNA (A, U, C, G).
Complementary
Matching by the pairing rule: A with T (or U), C with G.
Transcription
Copying DNA into messenger RNA, in the nucleus.
Translation
Reading messenger RNA to build a protein, at the ribosome.
Codon
Three RNA bases that stand for one amino acid or a stop.
Amino acid
One of the building blocks that link up to make a protein.
Point mutation
A change in a single base.
