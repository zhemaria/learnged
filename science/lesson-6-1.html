Before you start

The second half of physical science. Topic 5 covered what matter is made of and how it changes. This topic is about what happens to it when something pushes it: force, motion, energy, and waves.

The main tool is again the one from Lesson 5.2. There, you rearranged the density formula; here you rearrange F = m · a, and in 6.2 the formulas for work and energy. The method is the same and you already know it: what changes is the physical quantity and, above all, the unit the result is given in.

How to study this lesson: each law comes with a real situation before its formal statement. First recognize the event in something you have lived, and only then read the law that explains it.

1Force and net force

A force is a push or a pull on an object. You cannot see it, but you know it by its effect: it can start something moving, stop it, change its direction, or bend it. It is measured in newtons (N); one newton is about the weight of a small apple, roughly 0.22 pound.

Almost always several forces act on an object at once, and what decides its behavior is not each one alone but the net force: the result of combining them all. Direction is essential here, because forces in the same direction add and opposite forces subtract.

Zero net force does not mean at rest

It means the motion does not change. A car moving at a steady speed on a highway has zero net force: the engine’s push exactly equals air resistance and friction. It is moving, and yet no force wins over the others. This point is the key to the first law and one of the most common mix-ups on the test.

2Newton’s three laws

Each law is shown with an everyday situation first, then the statement.

First law · The situation

A bus brakes hard and the standing passengers lurch forward. Nobody pushed them. They were moving with the bus and kept going when it stopped. The same explains the seat belt: it does not hold the car, it holds your body, which tends to keep moving.

The statement. An object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed, unless a net force acts on it. That tendency to keep its state of motion is called inertia, and it depends on mass: the more mass, the more inertia and the harder it is to change the motion.

Second law · The situation

Pushing an empty shopping cart with some force makes it speed up a lot. Pushing it full with the same force makes it speed up much less. And if you push the full cart twice as hard, it speeds up as before.

The statement. An object’s acceleration is directly proportional to the net force on it and inversely proportional to its mass. It is written F = m · a, the formula worked on in the next section.

Third law · The situation

When you jump from a small boat to the dock, the boat moves away the other way. When you fire a pressure hose, you feel a push backward. When you walk, your foot pushes the ground back and the ground pushes you forward.

The statement. For every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. The two forces of the pair act on different objects, which is why they do not cancel each other.

First (inertia)With no net force, motion does not changeIt talks about staying the same, resisting change, or zero net forceThe passenger who lurches forward when the bus brakes
Second (F = m · a)Net force causes acceleration, smaller the more mass there isThere are amounts: force, mass, or accelerationThe full cart speeds up less than the empty one with the same push

The third-law mix-up that costs the most

Thinking that action and reaction cancel out. They do not, because they do not act on the same object. The foot pushes the ground and the ground pushes the foot: two different bodies, each feeling its own force. Only forces acting on the same object cancel, which is what the net force calculates.

3F = m · a and its three forms

The second law is handled just like density in Lesson 5.2: one relationship written three ways, depending on which of the three quantities is missing.

The formula and its three forms
  • To find the force: F = m × a
  • To find the mass: m = F ÷ a
  • To find the acceleration: a = F ÷ m

The units are not optional and go together: force in newtons (N), mass in kilograms (kg) (1 kg is about 2.2 pounds), and acceleration in meters per second squared (m/s²). In fact, a newton is defined exactly this way: 1 N = 1 kg · m/s², the force that makes one kilogram speed up by one meter per second every second.

Example 1 · Finding the force

A 12 kg cart speeds up at 3 m/s². What net force acts on it?

1What is missing. Mass and acceleration are given; force is asked: F = m × a.

2Substitute. F = 12 kg × 3 m/s².

3Calculate. F = 36 N. The unit comes from multiplying kilograms by meters per second squared, which is the definition of a newton.

Example 2 · Finding the acceleration

A net force of 200 N acts on a 50 kg object. What is its acceleration?

1What is missing. Force and mass are given; acceleration is asked: a = F ÷ m.

2Substitute and calculate. a = 200 N ÷ 50 kg = 4 m/s².

3Common-sense check. If the mass were doubled, the acceleration should be halved. With 100 kg you get 2 m/s², which fits the second law.

Example 3 · Finding the mass

A net force of 90 N gives an object an acceleration of 6 m/s². What is its mass?

1What is missing. Force and acceleration are given; mass is asked: m = F ÷ a.

2Substitute and calculate. m = 90 N ÷ 6 m/s² = 15 kg.

3Check. Multiply back: 15 kg × 6 m/s² = 90 N. Always check a division with the opposite multiplication.

Use the net force

Not just any force in the question. If someone pushes a box with 100 N and friction pushes back with 40 N, what goes in the formula is 60 N, not 100. Find the net force first and only then use F = m · a: it is the step most often forgotten.

4Friction

Friction is the force that appears when two surfaces touch and one tends to slide over the other. It always acts against the motion, so its usual effect is to slow things down.

It is also why the first law feels strange. A box pushed across the floor ends up stopping, which seems to go against the idea that a moving object keeps moving. It does not: the box stops because friction acts on it. With no friction at all, it would keep going forever.

Role of frictionSituationResult
Gets in the wayEngine parts rubbing against each otherEnergy is lost as heat and the parts wear out: that is why they are oiled
Gets in the wayAir against a moving vehicleMore fuel is needed to keep the speed
EssentialA car’s brakesWith no friction between pad and disc there would be no way to stop
EssentialWalking, or a tire on pavementWith no friction your foot would slip: that is what happens on ice

A detail the test uses: the energy friction “takes” from motion does not disappear. It turns into heat, which you can check by rubbing your hands together. This idea comes back in 6.2 with conservation of energy and in 6.3 with heat transfer.

The velocity-time graph

The effect of a force on motion can be read straight off a graph, with no calculation. Just look at the slope of the line.

Careful with the flat part

A flat line on a velocity-time graph does not mean the object is stopped: it means it moves at constant speed. It would be stopped only if that flat line were at zero on the vertical axis. It is the same care the heating-curve plateaus needed in Lesson 5.2: flat is not stopped.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Inertia comes from the Latin iners, inactive, made of in-, not, and ars, skill; it describes a body’s inability to change its state of motion by itself. Friction comes from fricare, to rub. Acceleration comes from celer, fast; in physics it does not mean “going fast” but changing speed, so slowing down is also accelerating, with the opposite sign. Newton is named after a person, Isaac Newton, which is why its symbol is a capital N even though the unit name is written in lowercase. The same rule applies to the joule and the watt in Lesson 6.2.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what the study compares and which variables it does not control.

📖 Braking distance on wet pavement

The distance a car needs to stop has two parts in a row. The first is the distance it covers during the driver’s reaction time, before the foot reaches the brake. The second is the distance it covers while braking, and there the friction between tires and pavement is the force working against the motion.

A road safety agency measured the braking distance of one car model on a test track at 50 kilometers per hour (about 31 miles per hour), in two conditions: dry pavement and pavement wet by sprinklers. New tires and an experienced driver were used, and each condition was repeated twenty times.

On dry pavement, the average braking distance was 14 meters (about 46 feet). On wet pavement, 21 meters (about 69 feet). The people running the test put the difference down to less friction being available: the film of water partly separates the tire from the road.

The report warns that the numbers belong to those specific conditions and should not be stretched to others. With worn tires, another kind of pavement, or higher speed, the distances get longer, and the test did not measure those cases.

📘Key words

Force
A push or pull, measured in newtons (N).
Net force
The result of combining all the forces on one object, with their directions.
Inertia
The tendency of an object to keep its state of motion; it grows with mass.
Acceleration
A change in velocity, in m/s². Slowing down is also acceleration.
F = m · a
Newton’s second law: net force equals mass times acceleration.
Action and reaction
Equal and opposite forces between two objects, each acting on a different one.
Friction
A force between surfaces that acts against motion and turns energy into heat.
