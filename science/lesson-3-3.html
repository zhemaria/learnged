Lesson 3.3 · Human body systems, homeostasis, and health

From the cell to the whole body: five systems that work together, a loop that keeps you steady, and data that must be read with care.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.1 · Understanding science textsSP.3 · Reasoning from data

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the job of the circulatory, respiratory, digestive, nervous, and immune systems.
  • Explain at least one specific interaction between two of those systems.
  • Draw a negative feedback loop and name its five parts.
  • Recognize that the same mechanism controls body temperature and blood glucose, with different players.
  • Tell apart negative feedback, which steadies, from positive feedback, which amplifies.
  • Read public health data in tables and graphs, telling what the data support from what they do not.

Before you start

The two earlier lessons worked at the scale of the cell: what is inside a cell and how it gets its energy. This one moves up to the whole body. The question that organizes it is how billions of cells coordinate to act as one body.

A text you already know comes back here. The reading in Lesson 1.3, about how the body keeps its temperature, was studied then as reading practice. Now it is studied as content: that text described, without naming it, a negative feedback loop.

How to study this lesson: data from a real community connect the content with real life. If you live in Chicago, you can compare the examples with the numbers for your own neighborhood; if you live in another city, look for the health data your local health department publishes.

1The five systems and what each one does

A system is a group of organs that work together on one general job. None works alone, and the test asks more often about how systems interact than about each one by itself.

The five main systems of the human body, their general job, and what each one gives the cells
SystemGeneral jobWhat it gives the cell
CirculatoryCarries substances all over the body in the blood, pumped by the heart.Brings it oxygen and nutrients and takes away its wastes.
RespiratoryExchanges gases with the air in the alveoli of the lungs.Supplies its oxygen and removes the carbon dioxide it makes.
DigestiveBreaks food down into molecules the body can absorb.Supplies glucose and other nutrients.
NervousDetects changes, processes the information, and orders fast responses.Coordinates the work of all the other systems.
ImmuneFinds and neutralizes things foreign to the body.Protects it from infections that would destroy tissue.

One interaction up close: the path of oxygen

The teamwork between the respiratory and circulatory systems is the one the test asks about most, because it closes the circuit opened in Lesson 3.2: the oxygen that reaches the mitochondrion and the carbon dioxide that leaves it have to travel somehow.

How to answer an interaction question

Naming the two systems is not enough. A complete answer says what passes from one to the other and where. “The respiratory and circulatory systems work together” is worth little; “oxygen passes from the air in the alveoli into the blood in the lungs, and carbon dioxide goes the other way” is the answer the test is looking for.

2Homeostasis and feedback loops

Homeostasis is keeping inside conditions steady even when the outside changes. It is not resting: it costs energy all the time. And the mechanism behind it always has the same shape.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Homeostasis: from homeo-, similar or same, and -stasis, standing still or steady state. Literally, staying the same. The root -stasis also appears in static. And the word negative in “negative feedback” does not mean harmful: it means the response goes in the opposite direction to the change detected. If the temperature goes up, the response brings it down. That opposite sign is what makes things steady.

The remarkable thing about the mechanism is that it does not change even when what is being controlled changes completely. The players are different; the plan is the same.

The same negative feedback loop applied to body temperature and to blood glucose
Part of the loopBody temperatureBlood glucose
Set pointAbout 37 °C (98.6 °F).A narrow range of glucose in the blood.
Change detectedTemperature rises from heat or exercise.Glucose rises after a meal.
ReceptorNerve endings in the skin and inside the body.Cells in the pancreas that sense glucose.
Control centerThe hypothalamus, in the brain.The pancreas itself.
Effector and responseBlood vessels in the skin widen and sweat glands make sweat: the body loses heat.Insulin is released, which makes the cells take glucose out of the blood.

When the loop fails

In terms of this plan, diabetes is a broken loop. Either the pancreas does not make enough insulin, or the cells stop responding to it. In both cases the receptor and the control center detect the change, but the response does not happen, and glucose stays high. Understanding the mechanism lets you understand the disease without memorizing it as a list of symptoms.

The other loop: positive feedback

Not every loop keeps things steady. In a second kind, the response amplifies the change instead of reversing it. It is called positive feedback, and here “positive” does not mean “good” either: it means the effect adds to the change instead of subtracting from it.

The body uses it when what it needs is not a steady value but to finish a process as fast as possible. When there is a wound, the first platelets to arrive release substances that attract more platelets, which attract more, until the clot is complete. If that loop were negative, the bleeding would never stop.

Negative and positive feedback compared: direction of the response, result, use, and how each one ends
FeatureNegative feedbackPositive feedback
Direction of the responseOpposite to the change detected.The same way as the change detected.
ResultThe value goes back to the set point. Stability.The change speeds up more and more. Amplification.
What it is forKeeping temperature, glucose, or blood pH steady.Finishing quickly a process that has to end: clotting, giving birth.
How it endsIt stops on its own when the set point is reached.It needs an outside event to cut it off: the finished clot, the birth.

When amplification has no brake

A positive loop with nothing to stop it becomes a vicious circle. The example that shows up most on the test is about climate: as temperature rises, part of a forest dies; the dead forest releases the carbon it stored; that extra carbon warms the air more, which kills more forest. Each turn of the cycle feeds the next. Topic 7 comes back to this when it studies climate; here, just recognize its shape: the same as the body-temperature loop with the sign flipped.

3Reading public health data

The test often gives health data in tables and graphs. The skill is the one from Topic 2 (the five steps, reading values, telling what the data show from what they only suggest), now used on content with real consequences.

A note about the data

The numbers in the graph below are illustrative: they follow the structure and size of real reports, but they are not official data. If you live in Chicago, you can look up the current data for your own neighborhood in the Chicago Health Atlas (indicator: emergency room visits for asthma per 10,000 people). In other cities, the local health department usually publishes similar data.

Emergency room visits for asthma (per 10,000 people) · illustrative data, four community areas

Example 1 · What the data support and what they do not

With the graph above, separate three kinds of statement.

1What the data show: area D has a rate of 119 per 10,000 people, more than four times area A’s. That is a direct reading.

2What the data suggest: that something different between those areas affects asthma. The graph points to it, but does not say what.

3What the data do not support: that the cause is air pollution, or housing, or access to medical care. Any of those ideas is reasonable, and none is proved by this graph.

✓What data would be needed: to test the pollution idea, you would need air quality measurements in the same areas over the same period. Without that second variable there is no way to test it, and this links directly to correlation and cause in Lesson 2.2.

The kind of answer the test rewards

On a conclusion question, the right option is usually the most careful one that still fits the data. “Area D has a higher rate” can be supported. “The air in area D causes asthma” cannot, even though it might turn out to be true. A conclusion that goes beyond the data is wrong even if it happens to be right.

4Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how the author stops right where the data end.

📖 A difference between neighborhoods

A public health team compared the rates of emergency room visits for asthma in four community areas of the same city for one full year. It found large differences: two of the areas had rates more than twice as high as the other two.

The team proposed three hypotheses to explain the difference. The first blamed air quality, since the two affected areas are next to industrial corridors and busy highways. The second pointed to the condition of the housing, since dampness and mold are known asthma triggers. The third pointed to unequal access to medical care: people without a regular doctor go to the emergency room for attacks that could otherwise have been controlled earlier.

In its report, the team warned that the available data could not tell the three hypotheses apart, and that all three could be true at once and reinforce one another. To separate them, the rates would have to be compared with air quality measurements, housing inspections, and health insurance data in the same areas.

The report concluded that the inequality was real and large, but that blaming a single cause without that extra data would go beyond what the evidence supports.

📘Key words

System
A group of organs that work together on one general job.
Alveoli
Tiny air sacs in the lungs where gases pass to and from the blood.
Homeostasis
Keeping inside conditions steady even when the outside changes.
Set point
The value the body tries to keep, such as 37 °C.
Receptor / control center / effector
Detects the change / decides / carries out the response.
Negative feedback
A response that reverses the change and keeps things steady.
Positive feedback
A response that amplifies the change until an outside event stops it.
Insulin
A substance made by the pancreas that makes cells take glucose out of the blood.
Rate per 10,000 people
A count adjusted for population, so areas of different sizes can be compared.
