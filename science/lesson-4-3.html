1How natural selection works

Natural selection is not a force pushing species toward perfection. It is the unavoidable result of three facts that, one by one, are not mysterious at all.

The idea the test is hunting for

Saying that living things “adapt” or “develop” a trait because they need it. That is not how it works. The variation was already in the population before the pressure appeared, and it comes from the chance of mutations and meiosis. The environment does not create the variant: it only decides which of the ones already there is helpful. A giraffe did not stretch its neck: the ones that already had longer necks left more offspring.

Example 1 · Bacteria that resist antibiotics

A culture of bacteria is treated with an antibiotic and almost all of them die. A few days later, the population has recovered and the same antibiotic no longer affects it.

1Variation: the first culture already had a few bacteria with a mutation that made them resistant. It did not appear because of the antibiotic: it was there before.

2Environmental pressure: the antibiotic kills the sensitive ones and leaves the resistant ones alive.

3Reproductive success: the survivors multiply with no competition, and the recovered population is made almost entirely of their offspring.

✓What this case shows and what it does not: it shows that the resistant variant became more common. It does not show that the antibiotic caused the mutation. That is exactly the difference between correlation and cause from Lesson 2.2, and it is also why stopping a treatment early makes the problem worse: it leaves alive precisely the most resistant bacteria.

Sickle cell, now with the whole mechanism

In regions with malaria, a person with one copy of the allele resists the disease better and leaves more offspring. The environmental pressure is malaria; the favored variant is the carrier. That is why the allele stays around even though two copies cause a serious disease. Where there is no malaria, that advantage disappears and the allele becomes much less common.

2Evidence for common ancestry and the cladogram

The claim that today’s species come from common ancestors does not rest on one kind of evidence, but on several independent lines that agree. That they agree while coming from different fields is exactly what makes them convincing.

Kinds of evidence for common ancestry, what each one shows, and an example
Kind of evidenceWhat it showsExample
FossilsIn-between forms of today’s groups, put in time order by the rock layer where they are found.Fossils with features of both reptiles and birds.
Comparing body parts: homologous structuresThe same bone plan with different jobs, a sign of a common ancestor.The human arm, the whale’s flipper, and the bat’s wing have the same bones.
Comparing body parts: analogous structuresThe same job with different plans, a sign that there is no close relationship.An insect’s wing and a bird’s wing do the same job but share no structure.
Molecular evidenceHow alike the DNA and protein sequences are, in step with how related the species are.The closer two species are, the more alike their genetic sequences.
Embryo developmentSimilar early stages in groups that are very different as adults.Vertebrate embryos that are hard to tell apart in their first stages.

Homologous and analogous point in opposite directions

It is the costliest mix-up of this part. Homologous means same structure, different job, and it is evidence for a relationship. Analogous means same job, different structure, and it is evidence against: two similar solutions reached by separate paths. The practical test is simple: if you must look at the bones inside to see the likeness, it is homologous; if the likeness is only in what it does, it is analogous.

The cladogram: how to read a family tree of species

When all those lines of evidence are put together, the result is summed up in a branching diagram called a cladogram. It is not a new proof or a sixth kind of evidence: it is the way to draw the conclusion the other five lead to. The test checks it directly and almost always asks the same thing: looking at the diagram, which two species are most closely related?

A cladogram is built from a table of traits. The rule is that once a new trait appears, all the descendants inherit it; that is why the groups fit inside each other, like boxes inside boxes.

Read the table by columns. All five species have a backbone, so it appeared before any split between them. Four have jaws: that trait appeared after the lamprey went its own way. Four limbs, three; the amniotic egg, two; hair, only one. Each column marks a smaller group than the one before, and that nesting is exactly what the cladogram draws.

The four reading rules of a cladogram: what each part of the diagram stands for
Part of the diagramWhat it stands for
The tipsThe species being compared. All are alive today: none comes from another tip of the diagram.
The nodes (branching points)The most recent common ancestor of everything that comes out of that point. It is a species that no longer exists.
The rootThe common ancestor of all the species in the diagram, the oldest of all.
The marks on the branchesNew (derived) traits: they appear there and are inherited by every species above.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The rule that solves almost every question

Two species are more closely related the more recent the node that joins them, that is, the closer it is to the tips. You do not count traits or measure distances: just follow the two branches down and see where they meet.

Example 2 · Three questions about the same cladogram

1Which species is most closely related to the lizard? The mouse. The node joining the lizard and the mouse is the amniotic egg node, the most recent in the diagram. Every other species joins the lizard at an earlier node.

2Is the frog more closely related to the shark or to the mouse? To the mouse. The frog and the mouse join at the four-limbs node; the frog and the shark, at the earlier jaws node. The result is surprising, because the frog and the shark look more alike, but outward looks are not the rule.

3Which is the most distant relative of all? The lamprey, which splits off at the node closest to the root. That is why it shares with the others only the oldest trait: the backbone.

The three mistakes the test hunts for in cladograms
  • Reading closeness on the paper. Two species drawn side by side are not necessarily close relatives. Only the node that joins them decides.
  • Reading it as a ladder of progress. The mouse is not “more evolved” than the lamprey. All five species are alive today and have been evolving for exactly the same time since the root; only the traits that appeared along the way differ.
  • Thinking one tip comes from another. The frog does not come from the shark. Both come from a common ancestor that is neither of them and no longer exists.

The first of those mistakes falls apart with a simple check: the same cladogram can be drawn in many ways. The branches can turn on their nodes, the way a mobile hanging from the ceiling turns, without changing the relationships at all.

In version 2, species A ends up next to D, but it still joins the others at the oldest node: it is still the most distant relative of the three. The nodes are the same, so the relationships are the same. Anyone who reads by the position of the tips will give different answers for two drawings that say exactly the same thing.

3Food webs and the flow of energy

An ecosystem is organized in trophic levels, by where each species gets its energy. The arrow in a food web does not point at who eats whom in the everyday sense: it points where the energy goes.

Only one tenth passes to the next level

Of the energy a trophic level holds, only about 10% reaches the next one. The rest is spent on moving, keeping warm, and staying alive, and is lost as heat. Two things follow that the test asks about: food chains rarely go beyond four or five levels, and there is always much more mass of plants than of plant eaters, and of plant eaters than of predators.

Example 3 · Remove one species and follow the cascade

Suppose a disease wipes out the rabbits in this web. What can you predict?

1Upward: the fox loses its food and its population goes down. The hawk, which eats foxes and frogs, will depend more on frogs.

2Downward: the grass is no longer eaten by rabbits and increases.

3Sideways: with more grass, the grasshopper population can grow, which in turn helps the frog.

✓The general principle: removing a species does not affect only whatever ate it. The effect spreads up, down, and sideways, because the levels are connected. That spreading is called a cascade effect.

Carrying capacity

No population grows forever. Resources (food, water, space) are limited, and there comes a point where the environment cannot hold more individuals. That sustainable maximum is called the carrying capacity.

You read the shape of this curve with the five steps of Lesson 2.2, and its last part is a plateau, exactly like the saturation point in the photosynthesis graph of Lesson 3.2. In both cases, a limiting factor no longer lets the process keep growing.

4Relationships between species

The arrows in a food web only describe who eats whom. The species in an ecosystem also have other close, lasting relationships, sorted by one simple test: who benefits and who is harmed.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Symbiosis: from sym-, together or with, and bio-, life. Literally, living together. The root sym- appears in symmetry and sympathy, and bio- was covered in Lesson 1.3. Within symbiosis, mutualism shares a root with mutual, both ways; commensalism comes from the Latin com-mensa, sharing a table, and describes one who eats what another leaves without bothering it; and parasitism comes from the Greek pará-sitos, one who eats beside another, and came to mean one who lives at the host’s expense.

The four kinds of relationship between species, by who benefits and who is harmed, with an example of each
RelationshipSpecies ASpecies BExample
MutualismBenefitsBenefitsThe bee gets nectar, and the flower gets its pollen carried.
CommensalismBenefitsNeither helped nor harmedA bird nesting in a tree: it gets shelter, and the tree is not affected.
ParasitismBenefitsHarmed, but usually does not die right awayA tick feeds on a dog’s blood.
PredationBenefitsDiesThe fox catches the rabbit.

Parasite or predator?

They differ in the outcome and the time. A predator kills its prey and the relationship ends right there. A parasite lives on or inside its host for a long time and needs it to stay alive, because it is its food. A parasite that kills its host quickly does not do well, and that is why many parasites evolve toward milder forms.

Careful with commensalism

It requires the second species to stay exactly the same, no better and no worse. In practice that is hard to check, and many cases once called commensalism turned out to be mutualism or mild parasitism when studied closely. On the test, choose commensalism only when the question says outright that the second species is not affected.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what the author claims and where he stops.

📖 The wolves and the aspens

For almost seventy years there were no wolves in a large national park in the western United States called Yellowstone. Without them, the elk population grew a lot, and the elk spent long periods feeding along the riverbanks, where young aspen and willow shoots are easiest to reach.

In 1995, wolves were brought back to the park. In the years that followed, researchers recorded fewer elk and also a change in their behavior: the elk avoided staying long on the riverbanks, where they were exposed. The aspen and willow shoots, eaten less, grew taller.

Several studies described later effects: more plant cover on the banks, more birds that live in those plants, and more activity by beavers, which use willows to build. Some authors said that the beavers’ dams in turn changed the course of small streams.

The sequence is often cited as an example of a cascade effect. However, other researchers have warned that in the same period the rainfall, the park’s management, and the numbers of other large plant eaters also changed. They argue that giving the wolves credit for the whole change oversimplifies a system where several factors acted at once, and that separating the effect of each one would require data that were not collected.

📘Key words

Natural selection
Variants that survive and reproduce better become more common over generations.
Homologous structures
Same structure, different job: evidence of a common ancestor.
Analogous structures
Same job, different structure: no close relationship.
Cladogram
A branching diagram of how closely species are related.
Node
A branching point: the most recent common ancestor of what comes out of it.
Trophic level
A step in a food web: producer, primary consumer, and so on.
Cascade effect
A change in one species that spreads through the food web.
Carrying capacity
The largest population the environment’s resources can support.
Symbiosis
A close, lasting relationship between two species: mutualism, commensalism, or parasitism.
