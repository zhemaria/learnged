Three particles, one table that predicts, and two ways for atoms to join.

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify protons, neutrons, and electrons, with the charge and place of each.
  • Calculate the atomic number, the mass number, and the number of neutrons.
  • Tell an isotope from an ion and say which particle changes in each.
  • Find an element in the periodic table and predict its properties from its group and period.
  • Predict whether two elements will form an ionic or covalent bond, and justify it.

Before you start

A new area starts. The four earlier topics covered life science. This one opens the second big area of the test, physical science, and it starts where it should: with the smallest unit that keeps the identity of a substance.

The content changes, but not the tools. You will still need the scientific notation of Lesson 2.1, the table reading of 2.2 (the periodic table is first of all a data table), and the habit from 4.3 of separating what a piece of data supports from what it does not.

How to study this lesson: use the periodic table as a document to look things up in, not as a list to memorize. You do not need to learn symbols or atomic masses by heart: what you practice is getting information from a table that you will always have in front of you.

1The atom and its three particles

All matter is made of atoms, and every atom has the same design: a central nucleus, very small and very dense, and a much larger outer region where the electrons are. The differences between one element and another are not in the design, but in the number of particles.

The three subatomic particles with their electric charge, where they are in the atom, and their relative mass
ParticleChargeWhere it isRelative mass
ProtonPositive (+)In the nucleus1 unit
NeutronNo charge (0)In the nucleus1 unit
ElectronNegative (−)Outside the nucleus, in energy levelsAbout 1,800 times smaller

The test often uses something that follows from that table: since the electron has almost no mass, nearly all the atom’s mass is in the nucleus. Gaining or losing an electron does not change the mass noticeably; gaining or losing a neutron does.

The two numbers that describe an atom

Atomic number and mass number, what each counts, and how to find it
NumberWhat it countsHow to find it
Atomic number (Z)The protons in the nucleusThe periodic table gives it. It identifies the element.
Mass number (A)Protons plus neutronsA = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The atomic number is the element’s identity

No exceptions. An atom with 6 protons is carbon, whatever its neutrons or electrons. If the number of protons changes, it is no longer carbon. So for any question like “which element is it?”, the only number to look for is the protons.

Example 1 · Protons, neutrons, and electrons

Aluminum has atomic number 13 and mass number 27. Find its protons, neutrons, and electrons, if the atom is neutral.

1Protons. The atomic number is the number of protons: 13 protons.

2Electrons. The atom is neutral, so positive and negative charges balance: as many electrons as protons, 13 electrons.

3Neutrons. Subtract: 27 − 13 = 14 neutrons.

4Check. 13 protons + 14 neutrons = 27, exactly the mass number. Always check a subtraction with the opposite addition.

The atomic mass in the table is not the mass number

In the periodic table, under the element’s symbol, there is usually a number with decimals: chlorine’s is 35.45. That value is an average of all the element’s isotopes as they are found in nature, which is why it is not a whole number. The mass number is always a whole number and belongs to one specific atom. When an exercise asks for neutrons from the table’s atomic mass, round to the nearest whole number.

2Isotopes and ions: two different changes

An atom can differ from the basic description in two different ways, and the test often sets them side by side because they are easy to confuse. One question settles it: which particle changed?

Isotope and ion compared: the particle that changes, the effect on mass and charge, and an example
TestIsotopeIon
Particle that changesThe number of neutronsThe number of electrons
Effect on massThe mass number changesAlmost no change
Effect on chargeNone: still neutralIt gets a positive or negative charge
Still the same element?Yes, because the protons do not changeYes, because the protons do not change
ExampleCarbon-12 and carbon-14: both have 6 protons, with 6 and 8 neutronsNa⁺: sodium has lost one electron; Cl⁻: chlorine has gained one

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Keep carbon-14 in mind, because it comes back in Topic 7. It is an unstable isotope, and that instability is what lets us date ancient remains: we know how fast it decays, so the share left in a sample tells how much time has passed. But chemically, carbon-14 behaves just like carbon-12, because electrons decide chemical behavior and both have six.

Cations and anions

When an atom loses electrons, it has more positive charges than negative ones, and the result is a cation, with a positive charge. When it gains electrons, there are extra negative charges, and the result is an anion. The logic is arithmetic: the charge of the ion is the difference between protons and electrons.

Example 2 · Electrons in an ion

The magnesium ion is written Mg²⁺. Magnesium has atomic number 12. How many electrons does this ion have?

1Protons. The atomic number never changes: 12 protons, that is, 12 positive charges.

2Read the sign. 2+ means there are two extra positive charges. That can only happen if two electrons are missing.

3Calculate. 12 − 2 = 10 electrons.

4Check. 12 positive charges minus 10 negative ones leave 2 positive, exactly what the symbol says.

The most common mistake here

Thinking that the plus sign means the atom gained something. It is the other way around. A positive ion is positive because it lost electrons, which are the negative particles. Remember it in a phrase: lose negative, end up positive.

3The periodic table as a tool for prediction

The periodic table is not a list of names to memorize. It is an organized data table, and its organization is not arbitrary: an element’s place predicts how it will behave. If you can read it, you do not need to remember the properties of calcium, because you can work them out from where it is.

The two coordinates: group and period

What group and period mean in the periodic table and what each one predicts
CoordinateWhat it isWhat it predicts
Group (column)Each of the 18 vertical columnsElements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, so they behave alike. Sodium and potassium react the same way because both are in group 1.
Period (row)Each of the 7 horizontal rowsThe period number tells how many energy levels the atom has. An element in period 3 has three levels in use.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Valence electrons are the ones in the outermost level, and they are the only ones that take part in bonds. The lithium in the first diagram had only one in its outer level: that is what makes lithium a group 1 element. For groups 1, 2, and 13 to 18 there is a handy shortcut: groups 1 and 2 have one and two valence electrons; in groups 13 to 18, subtract 10 from the group number, so group 17 has seven and group 18 has eight.

The question the test really asks

Items rarely ask just “how many valence electrons does sulfur have?” They usually give part of the table or the element’s data in the question and ask which element will behave like another. Always look for the answer in the same column, not the same row.

Example 3 · An unknown element

An unknown element is in group 2 and period 4. What can you say about it without knowing its name?

1From the group. Group 2 means two valence electrons. It is an alkaline earth metal and will tend to lose those two electrons, forming an ion with a 2+ charge.

2From the period. Period 4 means four energy levels in use.

3From its general position. It is on the left of the table, so it is a metal: it conducts electricity and reacts with nonmetals.

4Predict. It will behave like magnesium and calcium, which are in its column. None of this needed you to know the element is calcium.

4Why atoms join: the chemical bond

The noble gases in group 18 hardly react with anything, and that is where the whole explanation of bonding starts. Their outer level is full, with eight electrons, and in that state an atom is stable and needs nothing. Other elements are not so lucky: their outer level is incomplete, and all their chemical behavior is about getting that full arrangement. That tendency is called the octet rule.

There are two ways to get it, and they give the two kinds of bond the test checks.

The three kinds of chemical bond: what happens to the electrons, which elements form it, and the properties of the result
Kind of bondWhat happens to the electronsBetween which elementsProperties of the result
IonicThey move from one atom to the otherMetal + nonmetalHard, brittle solids with high melting points. Dissolved in water, they conduct electricity.
CovalentThey are shared by the two atomsNonmetal + nonmetalLower melting points. Usually they do not conduct electricity.
MetallicThey move freely among all the atomsMetal + metalConduct heat and electricity well. Malleable: they bend without breaking.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The practical rule

Predicting the kind of bond takes no calculation: just find both elements in the table. If one is on the left (metal) and the other on the right (nonmetal), the bond is ionic. If both are on the right, covalent. If both are on the left, metallic. Position decides.

Sharing does not mean sharing equally

In many covalent bonds one atom pulls the shared electrons harder than the other, and the molecule ends up with one side a little more negative and the other a little more positive. That happens in water, and it explains properties studied in Lesson 5.2: why water dissolves so many substances and why its boiling point is so high for such a small molecule. Here, just recognize the bond as covalent.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Atom: from the Greek á-tomos, “uncut,” because it was thought to be the piece of matter that could not be split. The root tom-, cut, appears again in anatomy and tomography. Isotope: from iso-, equal, and tópos, place: “in the same place,” because all the isotopes of an element share one box of the table. The root iso- already appeared in isotonic. Ion comes from the Greek ión, “going,” for how it moves toward the electrodes; hence cation, going to the cathode, and anion, going to the anode. Valence comes from the Latin valere, to be strong or worth, and means an atom’s ability to combine. Periodic does not refer to time but to regular repetition: properties come back every so many elements.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what made the table convince the scientific community.

📖 An empty box and an element that did not yet exist

Around 1869, the Russian chemist Dmitri Mendeleev put the known elements in order of increasing atomic mass and noticed that certain properties came back at regular intervals. When he placed elements with similar properties in the same column, the arrangement left several boxes empty.

Mendeleev argued that those gaps belonged to elements not yet discovered. He went further: from the position of each empty box, he predicted the approximate atomic mass, density, and chemical behavior of the element that should fill it. For one of them, below aluminum, he predicted a mass close to 68 and a density near 5.9 grams per cubic centimeter.

In 1875, a French chemist isolated a new element and named it gallium. Its mass turned out to be 69.7. The density he measured at first, 4.7, did not match the prediction; after purifying the sample better, the measurement was corrected to 5.9. The match with what had been predicted six years earlier greatly strengthened acceptance of the table.

Mendeleev’s system did have problems: in a few cases, the order by mass had to be changed so that properties fit the right column. The explanation came decades later, when Henry Moseley showed that the ordering rule was not atomic mass but the number of protons. With the atomic number as the rule, the problems disappeared. Note that the table was useful and predictive for almost half a century before anyone knew why it worked.

📘Key words

Proton / neutron / electron
Positive particle in the nucleus / neutral particle in the nucleus / negative particle outside it.
Atomic number (Z)
The number of protons; it identifies the element.
Mass number (A)
Protons plus neutrons.
Isotope
An atom of the same element with a different number of neutrons.
Ion (cation / anion)
An atom that has lost electrons (positive) or gained them (negative).
Group / period
Column / row of the periodic table.
Valence electrons
The electrons in the outer level, which take part in bonds.
Octet rule
Atoms tend to reach eight electrons in their outer level.
Ionic / covalent / metallic bond
Electrons transferred / shared / moving freely among metal atoms.
