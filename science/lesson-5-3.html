Before you start

Where we are: 5.1 explained why atoms join; 5.2 worked on what happens when they come closer or move apart without breaking. This lesson crosses the line: here bonds break and new ones form, and substances appear that did not exist before.

And one principle rules everything that follows, as simple to state as it is demanding to apply: in a chemical reaction, matter is neither made nor destroyed. The same atoms that go in come out, only rearranged. That is why equations must be balanced, the central skill of this lesson and one of the most tested in the area.

How to study this lesson: balance by counting atoms on both sides, first with chips and then with a pencil. What matters is seeing the atoms rearrange, not solving a math equation.

1Physical change and chemical change

Lesson 5.2 ended with a distinction that now needs to be made formal. When water boils, it is still water: its molecules move apart but none breaks, and cooling it brings back the start. When paper burns, there is no way to get it back: the molecules have broken and their atoms have recombined into different substances.

Physical and chemical changes compared: bonds, new substances, reversibility, and examples
TestPhysical changeChemical change
What happens to the bondsThey do not break: molecules only move closer or farther apartSome break and new ones form
Do new substances appear?No: the substance stays the sameYes: the products are different from the reactants
Can it be reversed?Usually easily: just add or remove energyHard or impossible by simple means
ExamplesMelting ice, boiling water, dissolving salt, crushing a rockBurning, rusting, digesting food, photosynthesis

Signs that a reaction has happened

A chemical change rarely announces itself. You notice it by clues, and the test usually describes one or more of them in the question instead of naming the reaction:

  • A gas is given off: bubbles appear where there were none.
  • A precipitate forms: mixing two clear liquids makes a solid.
  • A lasting color change that is not just a mix of colors.
  • A temperature change with no outside source: the mixture warms up or cools down on its own.
  • Light is given off, as in a flame.
No single clue settles it

That is the trap. Boiling water also makes bubbles, and yet it is a physical change: the gas is still water. The deciding question is not what you see, but whether the resulting substance is different from the starting one. Use that test before answering, especially when the question leans hard on one clue.

2How to read a chemical equation

A chemical equation is a short way of counting what happens in a reaction. It is read from left to right: the reactants are what goes in, the arrow means “turns into,” and the products are what comes out.

The difference between a coefficient and a subscript is the only truly tricky rule of the lesson. Fix it with an example: 2 H₂O is two molecules of water, while H₄O₂ is not water at all. It is a substance that does not exist. That is why, when balancing, you only touch the large numbers in front.

Many equations also give the state of each substance in parentheses: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) dissolved in water, that is, in an aqueous solution. That last symbol links directly to the solutions of Lesson 5.2.

3Balancing: the same atoms out as in

Balancing an equation means adjusting the coefficients until there is the same number of atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow. It is not a calculation: it is a count, checked element by element.

Example 1 · Making water

Balance: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

1Count as it is. Left: 2 H and 2 O. Right: 2 H and 1 O. Hydrogen matches; oxygen does not.

2Fix the element that is off. To have 2 O on the right you need two water molecules: H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.

3Count again. That change pulled the hydrogen along: now there are 4 H on the right and only 2 on the left.

4Fix again. 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. Final count: 4 H and 2 O on each side. Balanced.

✓Remember step 3: fixing one element often throws off another. That is why the method goes around in a loop (fix, count again, fix) and does not end until a full count comes out clean.

Example 2 · Burning methane

Balance: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

1Count as it is. Left: 1 C, 4 H, 2 O. Right: 1 C, 2 H, 3 O. Carbon matches.

2Start with hydrogen, which is in only one substance on each side. For 4 H on the right: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O.

3Leave oxygen for last, because it is in two products. Now there are 2 O in the CO₂ plus 2 O in the two waters: 4 O on the right.

4Fix the oxygen going in. CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Final count: 1 C, 4 H, and 4 O on each side. Balanced.

Two habits that save time on the test

First: leave for last the elements that appear in more than one substance on the same side (almost always oxygen and hydrogen), because fixing them early makes you redo everything. Second: check your answer by counting, not by rereading. If each element’s count is the same on both sides, the equation is balanced even if the coefficients do not match the answer key.

The absolute rule: never touch the subscripts

If you are tempted to turn H₂O into H₂O₂ to make the numbers work, the answer is no. Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, is another substance with other properties. Balancing means adjusting how many molecules take part, never what they are made of inside.

4The four types of reaction

The test does not ask you to memorize mechanisms, but to recognize a shape. The four types it checks are told apart by how many substances go in and come out, and how they rearrange.

The four basic types of chemical reaction: general form, how to recognize them, and an example
TypeGeneral formHow to recognize itExample
SynthesisA + B → ABTwo or more substances go in and one comes out2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O
DecompositionAB → A + BOne substance goes in and two or more come out2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂
ReplacementA + BC → AC + BOne element takes the place of another in a compoundZn + 2 HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
CombustionFuel + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂OIt reacts with oxygen, gives off heat, and usually makes carbon dioxide and waterCH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O

The shortcut

Count substances before reading formulas. If there is only one on the left of the arrow, it is decomposition. If there is only one on the right, synthesis. If O₂ is among the reactants and CO₂ and water among the products, combustion. Only when none of those three signs appears do you need to look closely at whether one element has replaced another.

Notice that photosynthesis and cellular respiration from Lesson 3.2 are, in this vocabulary, reverse reactions of each other: the products of one are the reactants of the other. It is the same pair studied then, now written in the language of equations.

5Thermochemistry: reactions that give heat and reactions that take it

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming them releases energy. The balance between the two decides whether a reaction, overall, gives off heat to its surroundings or takes heat in.

Reading the diagram comes down to comparing two heights: if the products end up below the reactants, the reaction released heat and is exothermic; if they are above, it absorbed heat and is endothermic. All the energy that is “missing” or “extra” came from the surroundings or went into them.

Touching the containerFeels hotFeels cold
ExamplesBurning a fuel, iron rusting, cement settingAn instant cold pack, cooking food, photosynthesis

The vocabulary mistake that costs the most

Thinking “endothermic” means the reaction is hot, because the word sounds like “thermal.” It is the other way around. In an endothermic reaction heat goes into the reaction, so the surroundings (and the hand holding the container) get cold. The roots leave no doubt: endo-, inward; exo-, outward.

The instant cold pack

It is the questions’ favorite example, because it joins 5.2 with this lesson. When you break the inner bag, a salt dissolves in water and the process absorbs heat: the solution takes energy from the surroundings and the pack gets cold with no freezer. It is the same kind of solution as in Lesson 5.2, now read in terms of energy.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Reactant and reaction share the Latin re-agere, to act back or respond. Product comes from producere, to bring forward. Exothermic and endothermic differ only by the Greek prefix: exo-, outward, as in exit and exodus, against endo-, inward, as in endoscopy; both with thermós, heat, as in thermometer. Synthesis comes from syn-, together, and thesis, placing: putting several things together, which is just what that reaction does. Decomposition is its opposite. Combustion comes from the Latin comburere, to burn up completely. And coefficient comes from co-, with, and efficere, to produce: the number that goes with a formula without being part of it.

6Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how a law that seems to go against what we see was tested.

📖 The candle that weighed less

For centuries it seemed obvious that when something burned, part of the matter was destroyed. A lit candle gets smaller until it disappears; a log leaves only ashes, much lighter than the log. The observation seemed conclusive.

In the 1700s, Antoine Lavoisier put that idea to a different test. Instead of weighing only the solid, he carried out the burning in sealed containers and weighed the whole container, with the air inside, before and after the reaction. The total mass did not change.

The explanation was that the products of burning include gases (mainly carbon dioxide and water vapor) that escape unweighed from an open container. Once trapped, the scale registered them. Lavoisier also checked the reverse: when certain metals were heated in air, the resulting solid weighed more than the starting metal, because it had taken in oxygen.

From these and other experiments came the statement of the conservation of matter. Note that the law was not drawn from direct observation, which pointed the opposite way, but from a change in how things were measured: counting what cannot be seen as well.

📘Key words

Physical / chemical change
Same substance, rearranged / new substances formed.
Reactant / product
What goes into a reaction / what comes out.
Coefficient
The large number in front of a formula: how many molecules.
Subscript
The small number after a symbol: how many atoms in the molecule.
Conservation of matter
In a reaction, atoms are neither made nor destroyed.
Synthesis / decomposition
Several substances become one / one becomes several.
Replacement / combustion
One element takes another’s place / a fuel reacts with oxygen and gives off heat.
Exothermic / endothermic
Gives off heat (feels warm) / takes in heat (feels cold).
