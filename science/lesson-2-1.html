Before you start

Topic 1 gave you three tools: rebuild an investigation, audit its design, and read a science text. Topic 2 builds the other support the test needs: numbers. Neither replaces the other: science is read, but it is also measured.

The GED® Science test often shows sizes as small as an atom or as large as the distance between stars. Writing them with all their digits is awkward and leads to mistakes: one extra zero makes you wrong by a factor of ten. How to study this lesson: convert by hand first, and then check with the calculator, never the other way around.

1What scientific notation is and how it is built

Scientific notation writes any number as the product of two pieces: a coefficient and a power of ten. One rule defines it, with no exceptions:

3.6coefficient: 1 ≤ a < 10 × 108power of ten: the exponent is the scale
The coefficient rule

The coefficient must be at least 1 and less than 10: exactly one nonzero digit in front of the decimal point. If the coefficient is 12.5 or 0.6, the number is not in scientific notation yet, even if its value is right.

The exponent is not decoration: it is the scale of the number. Two numbers with the same coefficient and different exponents are nothing alike. Between 3.6 × 10⁸3.6 times 10 to the power of 8 and 3.6 × 10¹¹3.6 times 10 to the power of 11 there is a factor of one thousand.

2Converting both ways

Converting is all about moving the decimal point and counting how many places it moved. The sign of the exponent tells you which way.

How to convert between decimal and scientific notation, depending on the size of the number
SituationWhat to doExample
Large number to scientific notationMove the point to the left until one digit is left in front. The exponent is positive and equals the number of places moved.149,600,000 = 1.496 × 10⁸1.496 times 10 to the power of 8
Small number to scientific notationMove the point to the right until it passes the first nonzero digit. The exponent is negative.0.00047 = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴4.7 times 10 to the power of negative 4
Positive exponent to decimalMove the point to the right as many places as the exponent says, filling with zeros.2.5 × 10⁶2.5 times 10 to the power of 6 = 2,500,000
Negative exponent to decimalMove the point to the left as many places as the exponent says, filling with zeros.5.2 × 10⁻⁶5.2 times 10 to the power of negative 6 = 0.0000052

Example 1 · From decimal to scientific

The average distance from Earth to the Sun is 149,600,000 km (about 93 million miles).

1Find where the decimal point is now: at the end, 149,600,000.

2Move it to the left until one digit is left in front: 1.496.

3Count the places: eight. The number was large, so the exponent is positive.

✓Result: 1.496 × 10⁸1.496 times 10 to the power of 8 km.

Example 2 · From scientific to decimal

The charge of an electron is 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹1.6 times 10 to the power of negative 19 coulombs.

1The exponent is negative, so the number is less than 1 and the point moves to the left.

2Move it nineteen places, filling with zeros: 0.00000000000000000016.

✓Note: this is exactly why scientific notation exists. Nobody counts eighteen zeros without a mistake, and the test does not ask you to: it asks you to understand the exponent.

The direction mistake

Moving the point the wrong way flips the sign of the exponent and turns a tiny number into a giant one. A quick check always catches it: if the original number is less than 1, the exponent must be negative; if it is greater than 10, it must be positive. Check this before you write the answer.

3Multiplying and dividing in scientific notation

Multiplying and dividing in scientific notation is easier than with the full numbers, because the two pieces are handled separately. Coefficients are multiplied or divided; exponents are added or subtracted.

How to multiply and divide in scientific notation
OperationCoefficientsExponentsExample
Multiplymultiply themadd them(4 × 10³4 times 10 to the power of 3) · (2 × 10⁶2 times 10 to the power of 6) = 8 × 10⁹8 times 10 to the power of 9
Dividedivide themsubtract them(9 × 10⁸9 times 10 to the power of 8) ÷ (3 × 10²3 times 10 to the power of 2) = 3 × 10⁶3 times 10 to the power of 6

Example 3 · When the coefficient goes out of range

Multiply (5 × 10⁴5 times 10 to the power of 4) · (4 × 10³4 times 10 to the power of 3).

1Coefficients: 5 × 4 = 20. Exponents: 4 + 3 = 7. The first result is 20 × 10⁷20 times 10 to the power of 7.

2The value is right, but 20 breaks the coefficient rule: it is not between 1 and 10.

3Rewrite 20 as 2 × 10¹2 times 10 to the power of 1 and add that power to the one you had: 1 + 7 = 8.

✓Result: 2 × 10⁸2 times 10 to the power of 8. This adjusting step costs the most points on the test, because the first result looks finished.

A ratio becomes a simple number

To compare the sizes of two objects, divide. A red blood cell of 8 × 10⁻⁶8 times 10 to the power of negative 6 m against a bacterium of 2 × 10⁻⁶2 times 10 to the power of negative 6 m: the coefficients give 8 ÷ 2 = 4, and the exponents cancel, −6 − (−6) = 0. The blood cell is 4 times larger. Without scientific notation, the same comparison means counting zeros.

4Units, prefixes, and the scale of the universe

The same value can be written with many units, but usually only one makes the number easy to read. The metric prefixes are powers of ten with a name. On the GED® Science test, as in all science, measurements are in the metric system (1 meter is about 3.3 feet; 1 kilogram is about 2.2 pounds).

Metric prefixes common on the test, the power of ten for each one, and a typical size
PrefixPower of 10Typical size
giga- (G)10⁹Distances between planets; computer memory.
mega- (M)10⁶Radius of Earth: about 6.4 megameters.
kilo- (k)10³Distances on a map; body mass in kilograms.
centi- (c)10⁻²Length of a sheet of paper in centimeters.
milli- (m)10⁻³Thickness of a coin; rainfall in millimeters.
micro- (µ)10⁻⁶Size of a cell: 10 to 100 micrometers.
nano- (n)10⁻⁹Wavelength of visible light: 400 to 700 nanometers.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

The prefixes come from Greek and Latin, and their literal meaning helps you remember them. Micro- means “small” (microscope, microorganism). Nano- means “dwarf.” Mega- means “great.” Kilo- means “thousand.” Giga- comes from the Greek word for “giant.” If you know these five pieces, you can work out the size even if you forget the exact exponent.

The whole scale, from one end to the other

This line puts seven objects on the same line of powers of ten. Each step on the line means multiplying by the same amount, not adding: that is why a proton and a galaxy fit in the same picture.

A line that fools your intuition

On this line, halfway between 1 meter and 10¹⁸ meters is not 500,000,000,000,000,000 meters, but 10⁹: one billion. Scales like this (logarithmic scales) appear on the test in graphs of earthquakes, acidity (pH), and sound, always for the same reason: the data cover so many powers of ten that no ordinary scale could show them together.

5The TI-30XS MultiView™ calculator

The TI-30XS MultiView™ is the calculator used on the GED® test, on the screen and at the test center. In this lesson you use it only to check conversions and operations you already did by hand.

TI-30XS MultiView keys for scientific notation, how to use them, and a warning for each one
Key or functionHow to use itWarning
The ×10ⁿ keyEnters the power of ten directly. For 3.6 × 10⁸3.6 times 10 to the power of 8, type the coefficient, then this key, then the exponent.Do not type “× 10 ^ 8”: the result is the same, but it is longer and easy to get wrong with parentheses.
The sign key (−)Enters negative exponents, as in 4.7 × 10⁻⁴4.7 times 10 to the power of negative 4.It is not the subtraction key. Using subtraction gives an error or a wrong answer.
The mode menuLets you choose NORM, SCI, or ENG. In SCI, every answer is shown in scientific notation.Go back to NORM when you finish, or later answers will look strange.
Clear firstBefore you start, clear the screen and any stored values from an earlier problem.A value left over from before is a common cause of strange answers.

Example 4 · First by hand, then the calculator

How many hydrogen atoms, each 1 × 10⁻¹⁰1 times 10 to the power of negative 10 m wide, fit in a row along one millimeter?

1Write the millimeter in scientific notation: 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³1 times 10 to the power of negative 3 m.

2Set up the division: (1 × 10⁻³1 times 10 to the power of negative 3) ÷ (1 × 10⁻¹⁰1 times 10 to the power of negative 10).

3Coefficients: 1 ÷ 1 = 1. Exponents: −3 − (−10) = 7.

4Estimate by hand: 1 × 10⁷1 times 10 to the power of 7, ten million atoms.

5Check on the calculator with the ×10ⁿ key and the sign key for the negative exponents. It should show 10,000,000.

✓Why in that order: if you estimated first, you will notice right away if the screen shows nonsense. If you type without estimating, you will accept anything.

🧮 Try it on the calculator

Open the calculator and check Example 4: type 1, ×10ⁿ, (−), 3, then ÷, then 1, ×10ⁿ, (−), 10, and enter. The screen should show 10000000.

The calculator does not choose the size for you

If you enter an exponent with the wrong sign, the calculator returns an answer that is perfectly calculated and completely wrong. No error message warns you. Your only protection is the estimate you made by hand first.

6Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice that every size is in scientific notation, as on the test.

📖 Why you cannot see a virus with a light microscope

A light microscope cannot tell apart two points that are too close together. The problem is not the quality of the lenses: it is light itself. Visible light has wavelengths between 4 × 10⁻⁷4 times 10 to the power of negative 7 m and 7 × 10⁻⁷7 times 10 to the power of negative 7 m, and no instrument that uses light can separate details much smaller than that. In practice, the limit is about 2 × 10⁻⁷2 times 10 to the power of negative 7 m.

A common bacterium is between 1 × 10⁻⁶1 times 10 to the power of negative 6 m and 5 × 10⁻⁶5 times 10 to the power of negative 6 m long, several times that limit. That is why bacteria are easy to see with a school microscope; they were first described in the 1600s with very simple instruments.

A flu virus, in contrast, is about 1 × 10⁻⁷1 times 10 to the power of negative 7 m: half the limit. More magnification does not help. The image gets bigger, but no new detail appears, because the information was never formed. For decades, scientists knew viruses existed without anyone having seen one.

The solution was to replace light with electrons, whose wavelength is around 1 × 10⁻¹²1 times 10 to the power of negative 12 m, millions of times smaller. The electron microscope, built in the 1930s, gave the first pictures of a virus at the end of that decade.

📘Key words

Scientific notation
A number written as a coefficient (at least 1 and less than 10) times a power of ten.
Coefficient
The first piece of a number in scientific notation.
Exponent
The small number on the 10. It tells how many places the point moves, and which way.
Order of magnitude
The size of a number given by its power of ten.
Prefix
A word part that stands for a power of ten: kilo- (10³), milli- (10⁻³), micro- (10⁻⁶), nano- (10⁻⁹).
Logarithmic scale
A scale where each equal step means multiplying by the same number.
