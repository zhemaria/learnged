Before you start

Lesson 4.1 worked at the molecular level: a gene is transcribed and translated into a protein. This one moves up to the individual and asks a different question: if each person has two copies of each gene, one from each parent, which one shows, and how likely is it to be passed on?

And there is a debt from Topic 3. Lesson 3.1 explained mitosis and said that there was a second kind of cell division. That debt is paid here, and not as a curiosity: without meiosis, you can fill in a Punnett square from memory, but you will not understand it.

How to study this lesson: it builds on percents from Math (Level 2) and on the counting principle of Lesson 2.3. If they are not fresh, review them first: the sex cells of a two-gene cross are exactly the 2 × 2 of that tree diagram.

1Meiosis: where sex cells come from

Start with a math problem. A human cell has 46 chromosomes. If the egg and the sperm each carried all 46, together they would have 92. The children of that generation would make sex cells with 92, and their grandchildren would have 184. In a few generations the number would be absurd.

The solution is meiosis: a division that makes cells with half the chromosomes. Human sex cells have 23. When two of them join, fertilization brings the number back to 46, and it stays the same generation after generation.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Meiosis comes from the Greek meíosis, making smaller: the name tells you the result, a division that reduces. Mitosis comes from mitos, thread, for how the chromosomes look as they divide, and says nothing about the number, because it does not change it. Gamete (sex cell) comes from the Greek for “husband or wife”: the cell meant to join another. And diploid and haploid share the root -ploid, fold or set: di- is two sets of chromosomes, 46 in humans; haplo- is one set, 23.

2Genotype, phenotype, and alleles

Each gene can come in different versions called alleles. A person has two alleles of each gene: one from the father and one from the mother. They are written with letters: a capital letter for the dominant allele and a small letter for the recessive one.

Basic genetics terms, what they mean, and an example with flower color
TermWhat it meansExample
GenotypeThe two alleles the individual has.AA, Aa, or aa.
PhenotypeThe trait that shows and can be observed.Purple flower or white flower.
Dominant alleleShows with just one copy. Written as a capital letter.A, the purple-flower allele.
Recessive alleleShows only if both copies are present. Small letter.a, the white-flower allele.
HomozygousThe two alleles are the same.AA or aa.
HeterozygousThe two alleles are different. Also called a carrier.Aa: purple flower, but it carries the white allele.

Different genotypes, same phenotype

A flower with genotype AA and one with Aa are both purple. Looking at them, you cannot tell them apart, and yet they pass different things to their offspring. The test uses this all the time: it asks for the genotype when the phenotype is not enough to work it out, and that is where the family tree comes in.

3The one-gene Punnett square

A Punnett square crosses the possible sex cells of one parent with those of the other. Each box is one possible combination in the offspring, and all the boxes are equally likely.

Example 1 · Crossing two heterozygous plants

Two purple-flowered heterozygous plants are crossed: Aa × Aa. By meiosis, each one makes sex cells with A and sex cells with a.

Punnett square for Aa × Aa. The sex cells of one parent head the columns and those of the other, the rows. The four results are AA, Aa, Aa, and aa.
Sex cellsAa
AAAAa
aAaaa

Example 2 · Reading the square on two levels

1Genotype ratio: one AA box, two Aa boxes, and one aa box. That is 1 : 2 : 1.

2Phenotype ratio: AA and Aa look the same: both are purple. Only aa is white. So 3 purple : 1 white.

3The probability of a white flower is 1 box out of 4.

The most missed distinction

The genotype and phenotype ratios of the same square are different: 1:2:1 against 3:1. Before answering, read whether the question says “genotype” or “phenotype,” or names a trait you can see, such as color.

4Four ways to write the same result

The test can ask for the same result in four different ways. They are not four answers: they are four ways of writing the same one, and you should be able to go from one to another without stopping.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A ratio is not a fraction

It is the most common mistake in this part. A fraction compares one part with the whole: 3/4 means three out of every four. A ratio compares one part with another part: 3:1 means three purple for every white. Same data, two different comparisons. Writing “3/4” where the ratio was asked for, or “3:1” where the fraction was asked for, counts as wrong just like a math error.

Probability is not certainty

A probability of 1/4 for a white flower does not mean that exactly one out of every four seeds will be white. There may be none, or three. The ratio holds approximately when there are many offspring, exactly as with tossing a coin. Remember what Lesson 2.3 said about spread: real results cluster around the expected value; they do not match it exactly.

5The two-gene square

When you follow two genes at once, each parent can make four kinds of sex cell. You do not have to memorize that number: it is the counting principle of Lesson 2.3. Two choices for the first gene times two for the second give 2 × 2 = 4 sex cells, and the square grows to 4 × 4 = 16 boxes.

Two individuals AaBb × AaBb are crossed. The possible sex cells of each are AB, Ab, aB, and ab.

Example 3 · Counting phenotypes in the two-gene square

To count phenotypes, ask in each box whether there is at least one capital letter for each gene.

1Boxes with A and B together, both traits dominant: 9.

2Boxes with A but bb, first trait dominant and second recessive: 3.

3Boxes with aa but B, first trait recessive and second dominant: 3.

4Boxes aabb, both recessive: 1.

✓Result: the ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, the best-known ratio of classical genetics. Always check that the boxes add up to 16: if they do not, a box was miscounted.

6Sex-linked inheritance

The crosses above assumed that both parents have the same possibilities. With certain genes that is no longer true, and the reason is one particular pair of chromosomes.

Of the 23 human pairs, one decides sex. Women have two X chromosomes; men have one X and one Y. And here is the key detail: the Y chromosome is much smaller and does not carry a copy of most of the genes on the X.

The consequence, in one sentence

For a gene on the X chromosome, a woman has two alleles and a man has only one. A man with a single recessive allele shows the trait, because there is no second allele to cover it up. A woman would need both. That is why recessive X-linked traits, such as color blindness or hemophilia, are much more common in men.

In the notation, the allele is written above the X. A woman who carries color blindness is XAXa: she has the allele, but sees colors normally. A color-blind man is XaY.

Example 4 · Carrier mother, father not color-blind

A carrier woman, XAXa, has children with a man who sees colors normally, XAY.

Punnett square for a carrier mother and a father who is not color-blind. The mother’s sex cells head the rows and the father’s, the columns. Results: a daughter who is not a carrier, a son who is not color-blind, a carrier daughter, and a color-blind son.
Sex cellsXA (father)Y (father)
XA (mother)XAXA · daughter, not a carrierXAY · son, not color-blind
Xa (mother)XAXa · carrier daughterXaY · color-blind son

Read the result separately for each sex, and that is the difference from everything before. Among the daughters, none is color-blind and half are carriers. Among the sons, half are color-blind. One cross, two different results depending on sex.

The mistake the test is looking for

Answering “25% of the children will be color-blind” and stopping there. It is true of all the children, but the question almost always says “of the sons” or “of the daughters,” and then the right answer is 50% or 0%. In a sex-linked cross, read carefully which group the question is about.

7Reading a family tree

A family tree, or pedigree, follows a trait through several generations. It is used to work out genotypes that cannot be seen: who is a carrier even though nothing shows.

Example 5 · Working out a genotype you cannot see

The trait in the tree is recessive. What can you say about II-2, the woman in the second generation, who does not show it?

1Her son III-1 shows the trait, so he is homozygous recessive: he has two recessive alleles.

2One of those two alleles must come from her, because each parent gives one.

3Since she does not show the trait, she cannot be homozygous recessive. So she has one dominant and one recessive allele.

✓Conclusion: II-2 is a carrier, even though nothing about her shows it. That is exactly the kind of reasoning the test asks for: a trait that skips a generation means that someone in between carried it without showing it.

8Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how the author tells the expected ratio apart from the observed result.

📖 The peas of a monastery

In the mid-1800s, a monk named Gregor Mendel grew peas in his monastery garden and kept careful records of his crosses. He chose seven traits that came in two clearly different versions, among them seed color, yellow or green, and seed shape, smooth or wrinkled.

When he crossed yellow-seed plants with green-seed plants, all the offspring were yellow. Green seemed to have disappeared. But when he crossed those yellow plants with each other, green came back in part of the next generation. Of 8,023 seeds counted in one of his experiments, 6,022 were yellow and 2,001 green.

Mendel worked out the ratio between the two numbers and got about three to one. The same ratio came up in all seven traits he studied. From that he concluded that each plant must carry two factors for each trait, that one could hide the other, and that the two factors separate when the reproductive cells form.

It is remarkable that he reached that conclusion without knowing about chromosomes, which would be described decades later, or DNA. What he did was count a great deal and trust the ratio. He also noticed that his numbers were not exact: 6,022 is not exactly three times 2,001, and he argued that the difference was what you would expect in any count where chance plays a part.

📘Key words

Meiosis
Division that makes four sex cells with half the chromosomes.
Gamete (sex cell)
An egg or sperm cell, with one set of chromosomes.
Allele
One version of a gene.
Genotype / phenotype
The two alleles an individual has / the trait that shows.
Dominant / recessive
Shows with one copy / shows only with two copies.
Homozygous / heterozygous
Two equal alleles / two different alleles (a carrier).
Punnett square
A table that crosses the sex cells of two parents to show the possible offspring.
Ratio
A comparison of one part with another part, such as 3 : 1.
Sex-linked trait
A trait whose gene is on the X chromosome.
Family tree (pedigree)
A chart that follows a trait through several generations.
