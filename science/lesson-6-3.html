Lesson 6.3 · Heat and waves

How energy travels: heat, which always flows from warm to cool by three paths, and waves, from radio to gamma rays.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.6 · Expressing scientific informationSP.7 · Formulas and scientific notation

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell heat from temperature and explain which way heat always flows.
  • Identify the three ways heat moves: conduction, convection, and radiation.
  • Label the properties of a wave: wavelength, amplitude, frequency, and speed.
  • Explain the inverse relationship between wavelength and frequency when speed stays the same.
  • Place the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum by wavelength and energy.

1Heat and temperature are not the same

In everyday speech heat and temperature are used almost as if they meant the same thing; on the test they are told apart precisely. This is the difference:

Temperature and heat compared: what each measures, its unit, whether it depends on the amount of matter, and an example
CriterionTemperatureHeat
What it measuresThe average kinetic energy of the particlesThe energy that passes from one object to another because of a temperature difference
Usual unitDegrees Celsius (°C); in the U.S., also degrees Fahrenheit (°F)Joules (J), the same energy unit as in Lesson 6.2
Does it depend on the amount of matter?No: it is an average per particleYes: the more matter, the more total energy it can pass on
ExampleA cup of tea and a bathtub of water can both be at 60 °C (140 °F)The bathtub holds far more thermal energy than the cup, and can pass on much more

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The last row is the key to the whole section: same temperature does not mean same amount of energy. A spark from a campfire can be at a thousand degrees and not burn you, because its mass is tiny and the energy it passes on is next to nothing.

Heat flows one way only

It always goes from the warmer object to the cooler one, never the other way, and it keeps flowing until both reach the same temperature. That is why an ice cube does not “give cold” to a drink: the drink gives heat to the ice cube. “Cold is coming in through the window” is a handy phrase but wrong in physics: what really happens is that heat is going out.

2The three ways heat moves

Heat can move in three different ways, and the test asks you to recognize which one is at work in a situation.

The three ways heat moves: where each works, whether it needs matter, and everyday examples
WayWhere it worksNeeds matter?Examples
ConductionMostly in solids, especially metalsYes, with direct contactA spoon handle getting hot; a cold floor under bare feet
ConvectionIn liquids and gases, which can moveYes, and the material must be able to flowBoiling water; warm air rising to the ceiling; sea breezes
RadiationAnywhere, including empty spaceNoThe Sun’s heat; the warmth you feel in front of a campfire without touching it

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The question that settles most items is whether there is matter in between and whether that matter moves. If heat crosses empty space, it can only be radiation: that is what happens with the Sun, and it is how its energy reaches Earth across about 93 million miles (150 million kilometers) of empty space. Keep in mind too that in many everyday situations more than one way is at work at once: in a pot on the stove there is conduction through the metal, convection in the water, and radiation from the flame.

Convection also explains something Lesson 5.2 set up: the warm part of a fluid rises because, when it heats up, its particles spread apart and its density goes down. It is the same floating rule as then, now applied to air and water.

3Waves and their properties

A wave is a disturbance that carries energy from one place to another without carrying matter. A cork floating on water bobs up and down as waves pass, but it does not travel with them: what moves forward is the energy, not the water or the cork.

Wave properties with their definition, unit, and what each one tells you
PropertyWhat it isUnitWhat it tells you
Wavelength (λ)Distance between two crests in a rowMeters (m)The “size” of each wave
AmplitudeHeight from the rest line to a crestMeters (m)The energy it carries: more amplitude, more energy
Frequency (f)Complete waves passing a point in one secondHertz (Hz)How quickly the disturbance repeats
Speed (v)How fast the wave moves forwardMeters per second (m/s)Depends on what it travels through

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

How the three are linked

One formula ties them together: v = λ × f. The important result: if the speed does not change, wavelength and frequency are inversely related. If the wavelength is cut in half, the frequency doubles; if the wavelength doubles, the frequency drops by half. Shorter waves always mean higher frequencies.

Example · Sound in the air

Sound travels through air at about 340 m/s. A note has a frequency of 170 Hz. What is its wavelength?

1What is missing. Speed and frequency are given; wavelength is asked: λ = v ÷ f.

2Calculate. λ = 340 ÷ 170 = 2 m (about 6.6 feet).

3Check. A note twice as high (340 Hz) would have half the wavelength: 1 m. Inverse relationship.

Amplitude and frequency are independent

Mixing them up is the most common mistake in this section. Amplitude has to do with intensity: in sound, with volume. Frequency has to do with repetition: in sound, with how high or low the note is. Turning up the radio does not change the note, and singing higher does not mean singing louder.

4The electromagnetic spectrum

Visible light is only a narrow band of a much larger family of waves, all of the same kind and all traveling through empty space at the same speed: the speed of light, about 3 × 10⁸ m/s. The only thing that sets them apart is their wavelength, and with it their frequency and energy.

The whole spectrum follows one rule, and if you keep it in mind you can rebuild all of it: the shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency and the energy. The facts the test asks about follow from it. Radio waves are harmless and are used to send information because their energy is tiny. Ultraviolet rays can already damage the skin. X-rays and gamma rays, with wavelengths of trillionths of a meter, go through the body, which is why they are handled with protection.

Comparing powers of ten is all you need

It is the skill from Lesson 2.1. Between 10⁻⁵ m for infrared and 10⁻¹² m for gamma rays there are seven orders of magnitude, a factor of ten million. And remember that a more negative exponent means a smaller number: 10⁻¹² is much smaller than 10⁻⁵, even though twelve looks like the bigger number at first glance.

The visible band is amazingly narrow. Everything the human eye can see fits between about 4 × 10⁻⁷ and 7 × 10⁻⁷ meters. Almost the whole spectrum lies outside that slit, and yet it is invisible to us. Infrared, right next to it, is what we feel as heat: that is why a space heater warms you without touching anything, and why the radiation of section 2 and the waves of this section are the same thing.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Thermal comes from the Greek thermós, heat, already seen in Topic 5 with exothermic and endothermic (and in thermometer). Conduction comes from the Latin conducere, to lead together, the root of conduct and duct. Convection, from convehere, to carry: it names the motion of the fluid itself. Radiation, from radius, ray, as in radio and radial. Amplitude comes from amplus, large or wide (as in ample). Frequency, from frequens, repeated: how often something repeats. Infrared and ultraviolet explain themselves once you know the prefixes: infra-, below, and ultra-, beyond; they sit just below red and just beyond violet in the spectrum. And hertz is named after Heinrich Hertz, with the usual rule: name in lowercase, symbol in uppercase, Hz.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice how much of the energy becomes light and how much becomes heat.

📖 Two bulbs and the same outlet

An incandescent bulb makes light by heating a metal filament until it gives off visible radiation. It works, but not efficiently: most of the electrical energy it uses comes out as infrared radiation, that is, as heat, and only a small fraction comes out as visible light. That is why this kind of bulb is too hot to touch after a few minutes.

A light-emitting diode, or LED, makes light in a different way, without heating a material to a high temperature. Efficiency tests show that an LED gives a similar amount of light using a fraction of the electrical energy, and that its surface stays much cooler.

A laboratory compared both types by measuring the light given off and the electricity used by each bulb, at the same room temperature and the same supply voltage. The results showed large differences in efficiency in favor of the LED, in line with the manufacturers’ published data.

The report warns, however, that efficiency is not the only way to compare two bulbs. How long they last, their price, the color of their light, and how they work in the cold also matter, and the test described did not measure any of those.

📘Key words

Temperature
The average kinetic energy of an object’s particles, in °C or °F.
Heat
Energy that passes from a warmer object to a cooler one, in joules.
Conduction
Heat moving by direct contact through a material.
Convection
Heat carried by a moving fluid: warm rises, cool sinks.
Radiation
Heat carried by electromagnetic waves; it can cross empty space.
Wavelength (λ)
Distance between two crests in a row, in meters.
Amplitude
Height from the rest line to a crest; shows the energy carried.
Frequency (f)
Complete waves per second, in hertz (Hz).
Electromagnetic spectrum
The family of waves from radio to gamma rays; shorter waves carry more energy.
