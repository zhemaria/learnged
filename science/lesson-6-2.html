Lesson 6.2 · Work, energy, and conservation

What it costs to move something and where that energy goes: work, power, kinetic and potential energy, and the principle that energy only changes form.

Before you start

A change of question. Lesson 6.1 asked what makes motion change, and the answer was the net force. This lesson asks something else: what it costs to move something and what happens to that cost afterward. The answer is energy, and its most useful property is that it is not lost: it changes form.

Rearranging formulas is already familiar from 5.2 and 6.1, so the work here is choosing the right formula and giving the result in the right unit. This lesson brings two new units, the joule and the watt, and both are easy to mix up. All the formulas (W = F · d, KE = ½ m v², PE = m · g · h) are on the GED formula sheet.

How to study this lesson: predict before you calculate, then compare your prediction with the number. Whoever reasons with conservation of energy first solves many test items without any math, because the test often asks “where is it greatest” and not “how much is it.”

1Mechanical work

In physics, work does not mean effort. It means something very specific: a force that manages to move an object in the direction of that force. If nothing moves, no work is done, no matter how much you sweat.

The formula

W = F × d, where F is the force in newtons and d the distance moved in meters. The result is in joules (J). One joule is the work of a one-newton force over one meter: 1 J = 1 N · m.

Example 1 · Work

A person pushes a cart with a force of 25 N for 4 meters. How much work is done?

1What is missing. Force and distance are given; work is asked: W = F × d.

2Substitute and calculate. W = 25 N × 4 m = 100 J.

3Unit. Newtons times meters are joules, by definition. An answer in newtons or in meters would be wrong.

Zero work does not mean zero effort

Holding a heavy suitcase for ten minutes without moving is very tiring, and yet the mechanical work is zero, because the distance moved in the direction of the force is zero. The same happens when you push a wall that does not budge. The test asks about this often, and the right answer usually seems to go against common sense.

2Power: work and time

Two people lift the same box to the same height, one in five seconds and the other in twenty. They did the same work, because force and distance are the same. What sets them apart is how fast they did it, and that is power.

The formula

P = W ÷ t, where W is the work in joules and t the time in seconds. The result is in watts (W): one watt is one joule per second.

Two meanings of W

The letter W is used for two different things: as a quantity it means work, and as a unit it means watt. Tell them apart by where they are: before the equals sign or inside a formula, it is work; next to a number as the unit of the result, it is watts. A 12 W light bulb uses twelve joules every second.

Example 2 · Power

A motor does 600 J of work in 30 seconds. What is its power?

1What is missing. Work and time are given; power is asked: P = W ÷ t.

2Substitute and calculate. P = 600 J ÷ 30 s = 20 W.

3Meaning. The motor delivers twenty joules every second. If it did the same work in half the time, its power would double, even though the work would not change.

3The forms of mechanical energy

Energy is the ability to do work, and it is measured in the same unit: joules. This lesson focuses on two forms and their sum.

In the potential energy formula, g is the acceleration due to gravity, which on Earth is 9.8 m/s². It is on the GED formula sheet, so you do not have to memorize it; some problems round it to 10 to keep things simple, and when they do, the question says so.

Example 3 · Potential energy

A 50 kg car sits at the top of a slope, 10 meters high (about 33 feet). What is its potential energy?

1Formula. PE = m · g · h.

2Substitute. PE = 50 kg × 9.8 m/s² × 10 m.

3Calculate. PE = 4,900 J.

Example 4 · Kinetic energy and the effect of the square

A 2 kg object moves at 6 m/s. Find its kinetic energy. Then do it again for 12 m/s.

1First case. KE = ½ × 2 kg × (6 m/s)² = ½ × 2 × 36 = 36 J.

2Second case. KE = ½ × 2 kg × (12 m/s)² = ½ × 2 × 144 = 144 J.

3What to notice. The speed doubled, but the energy was multiplied by four. That comes straight from the speed being squared.

That four explains the reading in Lesson 6.1

Braking distance does not grow in step with speed, but much faster: a car going twice as fast carries four times as much kinetic energy, and the friction of the brakes has to take all of it away. That is why road safety campaigns focus on speed and not only on reaction time. It is also the most common gut mistake with this formula on the test: thinking that doubling the speed doubles the energy.

4Conservation of energy

The principle is short and very powerful: energy is neither created nor destroyed; it only changes from one form to another. It is the same idea that ran Topic 5 with matter, now applied to energy.

On a ride like a roller coaster, that means the potential energy the car had at the top does not vanish on the way down: it becomes kinetic energy. And on the way up again, the kinetic energy turns back into potential energy. The sum of both, the mechanical energy, stays the same if nothing is lost.

You can fill in that table without a single calculation, and that is the skill the test rewards. One reading rule is enough: where the track is higher, potential energy is greater; where it is lower, kinetic energy and speed are greater.

Why the second hill is always lower

If energy is conserved, you might expect the car to climb back to exactly its starting height. It never does, and the reason ties straight back to the last lesson: friction with the rails and the air turns part of the mechanical energy into heat. That energy has not disappeared (the conservation principle still holds), but it is no longer available to move the car.

That is why no roller coaster has a second top as high as the first, and why a pendulum left alone swings less and less until it stops. In both cases the energy still exists: it is spread out as heat in the air, the rails, and the object itself.

The exact wording matters on the test

Saying that “energy is lost” is imprecise and is usually the wrong option. What happens is that energy turns into heat and can no longer be used for motion. The right answer almost always mentions the change of form, not disappearance.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Energy comes from the Greek enérgeia, made of en-, in, and ergon, work: literally, the ability to do work that is inside. The same root erg is in ergonomics. Kinetic comes from kinesis, motion, the root of cinema, which first meant moving pictures. Potential comes from the Latin potentia, power or ability: it is the energy that is not acting yet but could, because of where the object is. Power, on the other hand, measures speed of work, not ability, so do not mix them up despite how alike they sound. Joule and watt are named after James Joule and James Watt: the unit name is written in lowercase and its symbol in uppercase, J and W, just like the newton in Lesson 6.1.

📖 Where a car’s energy goes

When fuel burns in a car’s engine, it releases a certain amount of energy. Only part of it ends up moving the car; the rest turns into other forms that do not help it move.

Several efficiency studies agree that, in a regular gasoline engine, about three quarters of the energy released is given off as heat, through the exhaust, the cooling system, and the rubbing of moving parts. Another share goes to running the car’s own accessories.

Of the energy that does reach the wheels, part is used up pushing through the air, which gets much harder as speed goes up, and part in the rolling resistance of the tires. When braking, the kinetic energy the car has built up turns almost completely into heat in the brakes and goes off into the air.

Some vehicles have regenerative braking, which recovers part of that energy and stores it in a battery instead of giving it off as heat. The studies reviewed point out that the savings depend a lot on the kind of trip, and that they are much larger on trips with frequent braking than on the open highway.

📘Key words

Work (W)
Force times the distance moved in the direction of the force: W = F × d, in joules.
Joule (J)
The unit of work and energy: 1 J = 1 N · m.
Power (P)
How fast work is done: P = W ÷ t, in watts.
Watt (W)
The unit of power: one joule per second.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion: KE = ½ m v².
Potential energy
Energy of position (height): PE = m g h, with g = 9.8 m/s².
Conservation of energy
Energy is neither created nor destroyed; it only changes form.
