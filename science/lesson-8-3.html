Before you start

The last lesson, and it does not teach science. All the content is done and the project is finished. What is left is what separates a student who knows from one who also performs: knowing the screen, sharing out the time, and knowing where you yourself tend to slip.

Here is why it matters. Points are lost on the test for three different reasons: not knowing the content, not knowing how to reason with data, and not knowing how to handle the screen or the clock. The first two were worked on for twenty-three lessons. The third is solved here, and it is the easiest to get rid of.

The science practices SP.1 to SP.8 are not a separate block: they run through all three areas. A biology item can test reading a graph, and a physics item can test judging a conclusion. That is why Topics 1, 2, and 8 have no area of their own: they teach what applies to all of them.

About how deep the questions go

Most items do not ask you to recall a fact, but to apply it or reason with it from a passage you just read. So memorizing definitions pays off little, and rereading the passage or graph carefully pays off a lot. Almost always, the information you need is in front of you.

2The five item types

These are the five kinds of question that appear on the screen. None is hard in itself; what costs points is meeting them for the first time on test day.

Format 1 · Multiple choice

The classic question: a prompt and four options, and you pick one.

How to handle it

Read all four options before you decide. Often two are clearly out and the real choice is between the other two, which differ in a detail. Be wary of absolute words like “always,” “never,” and “the best,” as Lesson 7.3 warned.

Format 2 · Drag and drop

You take items from one area and place them in boxes, on a diagram, or in an order. It is the format used to balance chemical equations or sort organisms into feeding levels.

How to handle it

Place the items you are sure of first and leave the doubtful ones for last, when fewer boxes are left. You can move an item again: nothing is final until you submit. Check that no box is left empty.

Format 3 · Drop-down menu

A sentence has one or more blanks, each with its own list of options. When you choose, the sentence is complete.

How to handle it

Read the whole sentence again after you choose, to check that it makes complete sense. When a sentence has two menus, the first choice usually affects the second.

Format 4 · Fill in the blank

You type the answer, with no options to choose from. It is usually a number or a word.

How to handle it

Type only what is asked. If the question asks for a number, type the number, with no unit or extra text, unless the question says otherwise. Watch the decimals: use the format the question uses.

Format 5 · Hot spot (select an area)

You click on a specific place on a picture, diagram, or graph: the point where kinetic energy is greatest, the right layer of a diagram, the part of a graph where something happens.

How to handle it

Find the answer in your head first, and only then click. You can change the point by clicking somewhere else before you submit.

What these formats have in common

None of them tests computer skills: they test science. But a student who sees a drag-and-drop screen for the first time spends a minute figuring it out, and that minute comes out of thinking time. Practicing them first removes a source of mistakes that has nothing to do with what you know.

3Managing your time

Ninety minutes for about forty questions gives you a little over two minutes per question. That average does not mean spending two minutes on each one: it means the easy ones must leave room for the hard ones.

Time plan for the practice test in three blocks, with the goal and the warning sign of each
WhenWhat to doWarning sign
First third · minutes 1 to 30Move on without getting stuck. Answer what you know and mark the rest for review.If at minute 30 you have not reached question 13, you are going too slowly.
Second third · minutes 31 to 60Keep the pace. Long passages often show up here: reading them once carefully is faster than reading them three times.If at minute 60 you have not reached question 27, speed up and mark more questions.
Last third · minutes 61 to 90Finish every question and go back to the marked ones with the time left.If 10 minutes are left and some questions are still unopened, answer them all, even without being sure.

Never leave a question blank

There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so a guess has some chance of being right and a blank has none. Before you submit, the last check is always the same: nothing left unanswered.

The question that eats the time of three others

Getting stuck for eight minutes on a hard question costs much more than the point it is worth: it also costs the three questions at the end that you never get to read. If after a long minute you cannot see the way, mark it and move on. Coming back later, with a clear head, usually works better than insisting.

4After the practice test: reading your own mistakes

Finishing the practice test is not the end of the work, but the start of the most useful part. The overall score says little; what guides your review is knowing which science practice your mistakes are concentrated in. Two students with the same score can need opposite reviews: one misreads graphs but knows the content; the other reasons well but is missing concepts. The report of the practice test separates those two situations.

How to review a mistake

Looking at the right answer is not enough. The question that pays off is: why did the one I chose look right? There is almost always a reason, and that reason is the real mistake to fix. A mistake you understand does not happen again; a mistake you only correct does.

Do it in real conditions

Ninety minutes in a row, with no break, no notes, and no interruptions. Choose a quiet time and place, and let people at home know not to interrupt you. A practice test with pauses does not measure what it needs to measure, which includes stamina and managing your time.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The five formats. Do every activity on the practice page until each format feels familiar.
  • The practice test. Take the timed practice test in real conditions, and print or save the report.
  • Your review plan. With the report in hand, write down your two weakest practices and the lessons you will review, and for three of your mistakes write why the wrong answer looked right.

➡️Coming next

This is the end of the LearnGED Science course: twenty-four lessons, from the first hypothesis in Topic 1 to this practice test.

It is worth looking back once. The course did not teach three separate sciences: it taught a few mechanisms that came back again and again. Convection boiled water, moved Earth’s mantle, and spreads the planet’s heat. Density explained what floats, which plate sinks, and why warm air rises. Conservation ruled the atoms of a chemical equation and the energy of a roller coaster. And evidence was always judged by the same rule, for evolution, for Wegener, and for the Big Bang: several independent lines that agree weigh more than one.

What matters most is left, and it is a habit more than a piece of content: asking what the data support and what they do not. It helps on the test, which is what comes first, and it keeps helping long after. Good luck on the test.
