Before you start

The GED® Science test does not ask you to remember names. It gives you a text or a set of data and asks what was investigated, how, and whether the conclusion holds up. This first lesson gives you a structure you will use in the whole course: every investigation has an anatomy you can recognize. If you can recognize it, you can read any science text, even on a topic that is new to you.

How to study this lesson: read section 1 and copy the six stages on a card by hand. Keep it in front of you during the whole topic. Then do the “Try it” questions, and open the practice page when a blue card invites you.

1The six stages of an investigation

A scientific investigation does not start with an answer. It starts with something that catches our attention. From there it follows an ordered sequence. The result does not close the process: it almost always opens a new question. That is why the method is drawn as a cycle, not as a straight line.

  1. 1Observation
  2. 2Question
  3. 3Hypothesis
  4. 4Design and data
  5. 5Analysis
  6. 6Conclusion

↻ The conclusion leads to new questions, so the cycle starts again.

The six stages of an investigation, what each one is, and the question that gives it away in a text
StageWhat it isQuestion that gives it away in a text
1. ObservationA phenomenon is recorded as it happens, without explaining it yet.What did someone notice that was not expected?
2. QuestionThe observation becomes a specific, limited question.What exactly did they want to find out?
3. HypothesisA possible answer is proposed that can be tested.What answer did they expect before measuring?
4. Design and dataThe procedure is planned, the conditions are controlled, and things are measured.What did they do, and what did they measure?
5. AnalysisThe data are organized in tables or graphs to look for patterns.What do the numbers show?
6. ConclusionThey decide if the data support the hypothesis or not, and new questions come up.Does the evidence support the claim?

The conclusion is not the end

An investigation that answers its question almost always creates two or three new questions. That is why the diagram goes back to observation. A result that closes the topic completely is the exception, not the rule.

2Observation and interpretation are not the same

An observation is what is recorded directly with the senses or with an instrument. An interpretation is the explanation someone adds to it. Mixing them up is the most common mistake when reading a science text, and the GED® test checks it often.

Example 1 · Separate what was measured from what was explained

A researcher writes: “The plants in group A grew to 18 cm, and the plants in group B grew to 11 cm. Blue light helps the stem grow.” (18 cm is about 7 inches.)

1The first sentence reports heights measured with a ruler: it is an observation.

2The second sentence proposes a cause that nobody measured directly: it is an interpretation.

✓Practical test: if you can check the statement by looking at the instrument or the data table, it is an observation. If it has to explain why something happened, it is an interpretation.

Watch the verbs

Words like shows, causes, leads to, means that, or is due to almost always introduce an interpretation. Verbs like measured, recorded, counted, or reached introduce observations.

3A hypothesis you can test

A hypothesis is a possible answer to the research question. To be useful in science, it must do three things: connect two things that can be measured, allow us to collect data that support it or go against it, and be written clearly enough that another person could test it.

The “if… then…” form

Writing the hypothesis as “if… then…” forces you to name the two variables and to predict the result. That turns a vague idea into something you can check.

Example 2 · From a vague idea to a hypothesis

First idea: “I think salt affects plants.”

1Name what you will change: the amount of salt in the water you use on the plants.

2Name what you will measure: the height of the stem after fifteen days.

3Write it as “if… then…”: “If the plants are watered with salt water, then after fifteen days they will be shorter than plants watered with water without salt.”

✓Result: the hypothesis names what changes, what is measured, and which way the change is expected to go.

When a statement cannot be tested

A statement cannot be tested when it cannot be measured, when it is a personal preference, or when it talks about something no instrument can record.

Statements that cannot be tested, why, and a version that can
StatementWhy it cannot be testedA version that can be tested
Plants grow better with classical music.“Better” is not defined, and it cannot be measured.If a plant hears 60 minutes of sound at 70 decibels every day, then its height after 20 days will be different from the height of plants kept in silence.
Blue is the most beautiful color in the spectrum.It is a personal taste, not a fact you can measure.Does not apply: the statement belongs to taste, not to science.
Someday we will find life in another galaxy.No evidence we can collect today could prove it wrong.If scientists study the atmosphere of a planet outside the solar system, then they will find (or not find) methane and oxygen at the same time.
Sleeping too little is harmful.Neither “too little” nor “harmful” has a number.If people sleep 5 hours a night for a week, then their score on a memory test will be lower than the score of people who sleep 8 hours.

Quick test

Ask yourself: what result would make me give up this hypothesis? If no result you can imagine could go against it, the statement cannot be tested. A statement that cannot be tested is not necessarily false: it simply cannot be decided by science.

4Hypothesis, theory, and law: three different things

In everyday language, “theory” means a guess. In science it means the opposite: an explanation that has been confirmed again and again. This mix-up shows up on the test often.

Hypothesis, scientific theory, and scientific law: what each one is, how much evidence supports it, and an example
IdeaWhat it isSupportExample
HypothesisA possible answer to one specific question. It has not been tested yet.None yet: it is proposed so it can be tested.Salt water will make the stem shorter.
Scientific theoryA broad explanation of a phenomenon, supported by a large amount of independent evidence.Decades or centuries of data from several sciences that agree.Cell theory: every living thing is made of cells.
Scientific lawA description of a pattern that always holds, often written as a formula. It describes what happens, not why.Observed again and again, with no known exceptions.The law of conservation of matter.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Common mistake

Thinking that a hypothesis “grows up” into a theory and then into a law. There is no such ladder. A theory explains and a law describes: they answer different questions, and one never turns into the other.

5Science reading

A research summary written for this level. Read it, then answer the questions.

📖 Sleep and remembering new words

A team noticed that students in a night class remembered fewer new words than students in a morning class, even though both groups had the same teaching. The team asked whether the amount of sleep before class affected how many words the students remembered.

Sixty adults took part. They were put into two groups of 30 at random. For seven nights, one group slept 8 hours and the other group slept 5 hours. Every morning, the participants studied 20 new words for 15 minutes, and they were tested 24 hours later. Everyone studied the same words, in the same room, at the same time.

On average, the 8-hour group remembered 14.2 words and the 5-hour group remembered 9.6 words. The difference stayed the same on all seven days. The authors point out that memories are strengthened during certain stages of sleep, and that sleeping less cuts that process short.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your own hypothesis. Write an “if… then…” hypothesis about something in your daily life. Say what you would change and what you would measure. Then write a paragraph that explains how you would test it.
  • Testable or not? Write six claims you have heard this week in ads, the news, or conversations (for example, “this shampoo makes hair grow”). Mark each one testable or not testable, and rewrite the ones that are not until you can test them.
  • Observation or interpretation. Find a short science news story. Copy three sentences that are observations and three that are interpretations, and write the word that gave each one away.

📘Key words

Observation
What is recorded directly with the senses or an instrument, without explaining it.
Interpretation
The explanation someone adds to an observation.
Hypothesis
A possible answer to a research question that can be tested, often written as “if… then…”.
Testable
A statement is testable if some result could prove it wrong.
Scientific theory
A broad explanation supported by a large amount of independent evidence.
Scientific law
A description of a pattern that always holds; it says what happens, not why.
