Lesson 7.3 · The solar system, the universe, and energy sources

Leaving the planet: what keeps planets in orbit, the evidence for the Big Bang, and how to compare energy sources with data instead of opinions.

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of the solar system and explain what keeps planets in orbit.
  • Handle astronomical numbers in scientific notation and compare them by orders of magnitude.
  • Identify the three lines of evidence for the Big Bang theory.
  • Compare renewable and nonrenewable energy sources with clear criteria of efficiency, cost, and impact.
  • Back a position with cited data and admit its limitations.

1The solar system and orbits

The solar system is organized around a star that holds most of its mass. Eight planets orbit it, and they fall clearly into two families:

The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter, and beyond Neptune is the Kuiper belt, home of Pluto, now classed as a dwarf planet.

What keeps a planet in orbit

This question is answered with Topic 6, not with new content. A planet does not fall into the Sun or fly off because two things act at once: gravity, which pulls it toward the Sun, and its own speed, which carries it forward. Together they produce a closed, curved path.

It is exactly what a thrown ball shows: a steady force to one side does not stop the forward motion, it bends it. An orbit is that same thing carried on forever. And by Newton’s first law, there is no friction in space to slow the planet, so it keeps its speed with no engine at all.

The usual test mistake here

Thinking that something must push the planet to keep it moving. Nothing has to: the first law says a moving object keeps moving as long as no net force changes it. There is no real friction in space, so all that is needed is gravity to bend the path. A satellite in orbit is actually falling toward Earth all the time, but it moves forward so fast that it never reaches the ground.

Example · Comparing by orders of magnitude

How many times farther from Earth is the Sun than the Moon?

1The data. Sun: 1.5 × 10⁸ km. Moon: 3.8 × 10⁵ km.

2Compare exponents first. From 10⁵ to 10⁸ there are three orders of magnitude, a factor of about a thousand. That is enough for many test questions.

3If more precision is needed. 1.5 ÷ 3.8 ≈ 0.39, and 0.39 × 1,000 ≈ 390 times.

4Common-sense check. The result should be a bit less than a thousand, because the first front number is smaller than the second. It is.

One result of these numbers deserves attention: sunlight takes about eight minutes to reach Earth. It travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s, the speed from Lesson 6.3, and it still needs that long. What we see of the Sun is always how it was eight minutes ago.

2The evidence for the Big Bang

The test does not ask you to explain the theory in detail. It asks something more useful and more in line with practice SP.4: knowing which observations support it. There are three, and they are independent of each other, which is exactly what gives them strength: the same rule used for Wegener in 7.1 and for evolution in 4.3.

Redshift makes sense with the waves of Lesson 6.3. If a light source is moving away, its waves arrive stretched: the wavelength grows and the color shifts toward red, the long-wave end of visible light. That is what we see in almost every galaxy, and the shift is bigger the farther away they are. The interpretation is that the universe is expanding.

A detail the test sometimes uses as a trap

All galaxies moving away from us does not mean we are at the center of anything. In an expanding universe, an observer in any galaxy would see exactly the same thing. The usual comparison is dots drawn on a balloon being blown up: every dot moves away from every other one, and none of them is the center.

The link to Topic 5

The third piece of evidence, the amount of hydrogen and helium, has to do with the atoms of Lesson 5.1. The lightest elements on the periodic table are also the most common in the universe, in the proportions the model predicts. Heavier elements formed later, inside stars: the carbon, oxygen, and iron in our bodies were made there.

3Auditing energy sources

This section is studied differently. There is no right answer to memorize, because no energy source is the best on every criterion at once. What you practice is comparing with clear criteria and backing a position with data, which is exactly what practice SP.5 checks. Treat it as a debate with yourself where evidence is required: no claim counts without a number from the table behind it, and for every source you defend, you must admit at least one drawback.

Note on the data: efficiency, emissions, and cost figures for energy sources change quickly, and the numbers in this lesson illustrate the comparison method; they are not reference values. Current data are available from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) and the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). Always check the year of the data.

Those two rows are enough to break the most common mental shortcut: sorting all sources into “good” and “bad.” A source can be renewable and give off gases, or nonrenewable and not give them off. And each one carries other costs: land use, waste, dependence on weather or time of day.

How to answer a comparison item on the test

These items show a data table and ask which source is best according to a given criterion. The answer does not come from your opinion about energy, but from reading the column the question points to. If it asks about emissions, look at emissions; if it asks about cost, look at cost. A source can win in one column and lose in the next, and that is not a contradiction: it is the point.

The trap of the absolute statement

Options like “energy X is the best” or “source Y has no drawbacks” are almost always wrong, because no source wins on every criterion. Correct answers are usually limited: according to the table, and in terms of emissions, source X has the lowest value. Be wary of superlatives with no criterion.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Orbit comes from the Latin orbis, circle or wheel. Planet comes from the Greek planétēs, wanderer, because in the ancient sky they were the points that moved against the fixed stars. Asteroid joins astro, star, with -oid, shaped like: objects that looked like stars without being stars. Cosmology, from kósmos, order or universe, and -logy, study. Galaxy comes from gálaktos, milk, because of how the Milky Way looks; lactose shares the idea. Renewable is built on novus, new: the resource becomes new again. And fossil, in fossil fuels, is the same word as in Lesson 7.1, from the Latin fossilis, dug up: coal and oil come from living remains buried for millions of years, which explains at once why they are not renewable on a human time scale.

4Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice that both positions cite correct data and still disagree.

📖 Two reports on the same solar farm

A town is studying whether to build a solar farm on farmland that is no longer used, and it has ordered two independent technical reports. Both start from the same measurements of sunlight in the area and the same installation prices.

The first report recommends building it. It points out that the cost per unit of energy has fallen steadily over the last decade, that running the farm produces no emissions or waste during the panels’ working life, and that the proposed land has not been farmed for years. It estimates the investment will be paid back within the equipment’s warranty period.

The second report advises against building it at that site. It does not dispute the cost or emissions figures, and it agrees they are correct. Its objection is different: solar production is intermittent, and the town has no storage system or backup agreement with the power grid, so it would still need another source for the hours without sun. It adds that the payback calculation leaves out the cost of recycling the panels at the end of their life.

Both reports use data that can be checked, and no calculation errors were found in either. The disagreement is not about the data, but about which criteria are treated as decisive and which costs are included in the balance.

This lesson closes Topic 7 and the last of the test’s three content domains. Twenty-one lessons have covered life science, physical science, and Earth and space science, and now that the whole picture is visible, one thread stands out: almost nothing was studied twice. 📘Key words

Orbit
The curved path of a planet or moon, made by gravity plus forward speed.
Rocky planets
Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars: small, dense, with solid surfaces.
Giant planets
Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune: huge, mostly gas and ice.
Redshift
Light from a source moving away arrives stretched to longer wavelengths.
Cosmic microwave background
Faint, even radiation from every direction: leftover heat of the early universe.
Renewable source
One whose resource is replaced on a human time scale; renewable does not always mean low emissions.
Intermittent
Producing only at some times (sun, wind); needs storage or backup.
