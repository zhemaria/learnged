Lesson 1.3 · Reading science: technical vocabulary and multistep procedures

The GED® Science test is, in large part, a reading test. Here you learn to read science.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.1 · Reading scienceSP.2.d · Steps of an investigation

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Work out the meaning of a technical word by breaking it into its Greek and Latin roots.
  • Work out the meaning of an unknown word from the clues around it.
  • Follow a multistep procedure without skipping or switching any step.
  • Find the central idea of a science text without mixing it up with a supporting detail.
  • Recognize how the author organized the text: sequence, cause and effect, or compare and contrast.

Before you start

Lessons 1.1 and 1.2 taught you to rebuild an investigation and to audit its design. Both need something we took for granted until now: understanding the text. This lesson closes Topic 1 with the skill that holds up the other two.

Let’s say it clearly: the GED® Science test is, in large part, a reading test. Nobody has to know ahead of time what thermoregulation means. What the test checks is whether you can work it out with what the text puts in front of you.

1Break the word apart: prefix, root, and suffix

Most science words are not new words: they are old pieces put together. If you know thirty pieces, you can get close to the meaning of hundreds of words you have never seen.

Example 1 · A word you have never seen

A text talks about the hydrolysis of a large molecule. You do not know the word.

1Separate the pieces you know: hydro- and -lysis.

2Hydro- means water. The ending -lysis appears in analysis and paralysis: it has to do with breaking apart or loosening.

3Guess a meaning before you look it up: breaking something apart with water.

✓Result: the guess is right. Hydrolysis is breaking a molecule apart with water. You did not need to memorize it.

Endo- and exo- are worth points

This pair shows up on the test very often in Topic 5, with chemical reactions. An exothermic reaction gives off heat to the outside; an endothermic one takes heat in. If you remember the two pieces, you do not need to memorize the two definitions.

2When the root is not enough: context clues

Not every word can be broken apart. When it cannot, the author of a science text almost always leaves a clue near the word, because the author wants to be understood. Knowing the kind of clue speeds up your reading a lot.

Four kinds of context clues, the signal words that give them away, and an example of each
Kind of clueSignal wordsExample
Definitionthat is, in other words, is defined as, commas or parentheses that explainHomeostasis, that is, keeping the inside of the body steady, takes energy.
Examplefor example, such as, like, includingArthropods, such as insects, crabs, and spiders, have an exoskeleton.
Contrastin contrast, unlike, whereas, but, howeverAutotrophs make their own food; heterotrophs, in contrast, must get it from outside.
Synonymalso called, known as, or simplyPrecipitation, also called rain when it falls as a liquid, brings water back to the ground.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A contrast can trick you if you read fast

In “autotrophs make their own food; heterotrophs, in contrast, must get it from outside,” the word defined by the second half is heterotroph. In contrast tells you that what follows is the opposite of what came before. If you read only half the sentence, you get the definition backward.

3Multistep procedures

A lab procedure is not a list of suggestions: it is a sequence. Each step sets up what the next step needs. Switching two steps or skipping one does not just slow the experiment down: it ruins it.

Example 2 · An eight-step procedure

Preparing a sample of onion skin to see its cells under the microscope:

1Cut a small piece of onion and peel off a thin, clear layer from the inside.

2Lay the layer flat on the glass slide, with no folds.

3Add a drop of stain on the sample.

4Wait one minute so the stain soaks in.

5Cover it with the cover slip, lowering it slowly at an angle to avoid air bubbles.

6Remove the extra stain with paper towel at the edge.

7Look first with the lowest-power lens and focus.

8Switch to the highest-power lens and adjust only the fine focus.

What happens if the order changes

Staining after covering keeps the stain from reaching the cells. Starting with the highest power leaves the sample out of view and you have to start over. Covering all at once, without tilting, traps bubbles that can look like parts of a cell.

Words that mark the order

First, next, then, once, before, after that, last, finally. When you read a procedure, underline them: they are the skeleton of the order, and the answer to a test question is often right in one of them.

Three ways to check the order
  • Look for what a step needs. If step 4 talks about “the stained sample,” it cannot come before the step that stains it. The clue is usually in a noun, not in the verb.
  • Try switching them in your head. If you switch two steps and the answer is “nothing happens,” they can go in either order. If the answer is “it can’t be done” or “it would be ruined,” you have found the order.
  • Some steps really can be switched, and the test will not ask you about those, because there would be no single right answer.

4Central idea and text structure

The central idea is what the whole text says. The supporting details are the facts, examples, and numbers that back it up. Taking an eye-catching detail for the central idea is the most common mistake, and the test always offers that option among the wrong answers.

The structure is the way the author organized that idea. In science texts, three structures cover almost every case.

Three structures of science texts, the signal words that identify them, and the question each one answers
StructureSignal wordsQuestion the text answers
Sequencefirst, then, next, while, last, finallyIn what order does it happen?
Cause and effectbecause, due to, causes, leads to, therefore, as a resultWhy does it happen, and what are the results?
Compare and contrastin contrast, unlike, whereas, both, like, howeverHow are they alike, and how are they different?

Central idea or detail?

If a statement covers only one paragraph, it is a detail. The central idea has to cover the whole text. A good check: does the statement leave out an important part of what you read? If it does, it is not the central idea.

Read with a pencil: four marks

Mark the text as you read: ? next to a word you do not know, ★ next to the central idea, ✓ next to a supporting fact, and a circle around each order word. The first time, read slowly and out loud, as if you were explaining it to someone.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Read it using the four marks, then answer the questions.

📖 How the body keeps its temperature

The human body works well only within a narrow range of temperature, around 37 °C (98.6 °F). Keeping that value steady, even when the surroundings change, is called thermoregulation. It is one case of homeostasis, that is, keeping conditions inside the body constant.

When the outside temperature goes up, the blood vessels in the skin get wider, and more blood comes to the surface, so heat is lost to the air more easily. Also, the sweat glands release sweat: as the water evaporates, it takes heat from the body and cools it.

When the outside temperature goes down, the opposite happens. The blood vessels in the skin get narrower and lose less heat, while the muscles make fast, automatic contractions (shivering) that produce extra heat.

These responses have a limit. If the surroundings are extreme, or the effort lasts too long, the inside temperature moves out of the safe range, and heat stroke or hypothermia can happen. That is why thermoregulation is not a luxury for the body: the body needs it to work.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Break it apart before you look it up. Find six science words you do not know in a text (a news story, medicine instructions, or a product label). Break each one into its roots and write a meaning before you look it up. Then compare and write down which piece gave you the key clue.
  • Narrate a procedure. Put the steps of a procedure in order on the practice page. Then tell the procedure out loud, step by step, and write one sentence about what would go wrong if two of the steps were switched.
  • Four marks. Take the texts of this lesson and use the four marks: ? for a word you do not know, ★ for the central idea, ✓ for a supporting fact, and a circle for each order word. Write the central idea of each text in one sentence, and name its structure with the words that gave it away.

📘Key words

Prefix, root, suffix
The pieces of a word: at the start, in the middle (the main meaning), and at the end.
Context clue
Words near an unknown word that help you work out its meaning: a definition, an example, a contrast, or a synonym.
Procedure (protocol)
A sequence of steps where each one prepares what the next one needs.
Central idea
What the whole text says.
Supporting detail
A fact, example, or number that backs up the central idea.
Text structure
How the author organized the text: sequence, cause and effect, or compare and contrast.
