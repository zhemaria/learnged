Science · Topic 1 — The scientific method and research design

Lesson 1.2 · Variables, control groups, and sources of error

A good experiment changes one thing, measures one thing, and compares.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.2 · Investigation designSP.4 · Evaluating conclusions

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the independent variable, the dependent variable, and the constants in an experiment.
  • Explain the job of the control group and find it in a described experiment.
  • Point out at least two sources of error in a flawed design: sample size, selection bias, no control group, or an uncontrolled variable.
  • Explain why a conclusion does not hold up with the design that produced it.
  • Propose a workable fix for a badly designed experiment.

Before you start

Lesson 1.1 went through the six stages of an investigation and stopped at the hypothesis. This lesson goes into stage four: the design. A well-written hypothesis is useless if the experiment that tests it cannot tell what caused what. Here you will learn how to build a design that can, and how to spot one that cannot.

How to study this lesson: draw three columns on a sheet: what is changed, what is measured, what stays the same. For every experiment, fill in the three columns before you answer any question. If a column is empty or unclear, that is usually where the flaw is.

1The three kinds of variables

Every experiment is built around three questions: what is changed, what is measured, and what stays the same. Each one is a kind of variable. Mixing them up ruins a design.

The three kinds of variables and the question that identifies each one
Kind of variableQuestion that identifies itAlso called
Independent variableWhat does the researcher change on purpose?the treatment, the factor tested
Dependent variableWhat is measured at the end, to see if it responded?the result, the response
Constants (controlled variables)What is kept the same on purpose?controlled conditions

Example 1 · Fertilizer and stem height

A researcher waters one group of plants with 10 mL of fertilizer and another group with no fertilizer. After twenty days, she measures the height of every plant.

1What changes on purpose: the amount of fertilizer. It is the independent variable.

2What is measured at the end: the height of the stem. It is the dependent variable.

3What must stay the same: the kind of plant, the soil, the amount of water, the light, the temperature, and the time. They are the constants.

✓Reading rule: the dependent variable depends on the independent one. If you can say “the height depends on the fertilizer” and not the other way around, you sorted them correctly.

Only one at a time

A well-designed experiment changes one independent variable at a time. If two things change and the result changes, there is no way to know which one caused it. The experiment produces numbers, but not knowledge.

2The control group: the measuring stick

The control group is the group that does not get the treatment being tested. Everything else is exactly the same as in the experimental group. It is not there for decoration: without it there is nothing to compare, and without a comparison you cannot say that the treatment caused the result.

Why the comparison works

If absolutely everything is the same in the two groups except the fertilizer, then the 7 cm difference can only come from the fertilizer. That is all the logic of a controlled experiment. (7 cm is a little less than 3 inches.)

No control, no conclusion

If the researcher had grown only the group with fertilizer and the plants reached 18 cm, she would know nothing. Is that a lot? A little? Would they have grown just as tall without fertilizer? One group gives a number, not an answer.

Watch for the placebo

In studies with people, the control group does not get “nothing”: it gets a treatment that looks the same but has no active ingredient (a placebo). That way the only difference between the groups is the active ingredient, and not the fact of being treated and feeling cared for.

3Four sources of error you must be able to find

The test often shows a design with a flaw and asks why its conclusion does not hold up. The flaw almost always belongs to one of these four families.

Four sources of error in an experimental design, with what they are, a warning sign in the text, and how to fix them
Source of errorWhat it isWarning sign in the textHow to fix it
Sample too smallSo few cases are studied that the result could be due to chance.“It was tried on 4 people,” “3 plants were used.”Use more participants or more repeated trials.
Selection biasParticipants were not assigned at random, so the groups were already different before the study began.“The volunteers chose their group,” “the most motivated were chosen.”Assign participants to the groups at random.
No control groupThere is no group without the treatment to compare with.“Everyone took the supplement,” “it was measured before and after, and that’s all.”Add a group that does not get the treatment.
Uncontrolled variableSomething besides the treatment also changed between the groups and could have caused the result.“They also changed their diet,” “one group was by the window.”Make that condition the same in both groups, or measure it and take it into account.

Example 2 · Audit a design

“Six volunteers from a gym took a supplement for one month, and all of them got stronger. The supplement increases muscle strength.”

1Six participants is a sample too small to draw a general conclusion.

2They were gym volunteers: there is selection bias, because they were already training.

3Nobody went without the supplement: there is no control group.

4They kept training during the month: training is an uncontrolled variable that explains the result just as well.

✓Result of the audit: the study shows that six people who train got stronger. It does not show that the supplement caused it.

4Science reading

A research summary written for this level. Read it like an auditor, then answer the questions.

📖 A tea to sleep better

A company says its herbal tea helps people sleep better. To prove it, the company put an ad in a health magazine, inviting people who had trouble sleeping to take part. Twelve people signed up.

For two weeks, all 12 drank a cup of the tea before going to bed. Every morning they wrote down on a form how many hours they thought they had slept. At the start, the average they reported was 5.4 hours; at the end, 6.8 hours.

The company’s brochure concludes: “Our tea increases sleep by 1.4 hours a night.”

The study does not mention that, during those two weeks, the participants also received a brochure with sleep tips: go to bed at the same time, turn off screens, and avoid caffeine in the afternoon.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The three-column chart. Draw three columns on a sheet: what is changed, what is measured, and what stays the same. Fill it in for two experiments of this lesson and for the greenhouse. In each one, check that the dependent variable really depends on the independent one.
  • The auditor’s report. Take the herbal tea study and write a short report with three points: which variable was not controlled, what is missing for a control group, and what you would change.
  • Redesign the experiment. Propose a corrected version: how many participants, how they would be put in groups, what the control group would get, and which conditions would be the same. Then check that your fix really removes each source of error you found.

📘Key words

Independent variable
What the researcher changes on purpose.
Dependent variable
What is measured to see if it responds to the change.
Constants
Everything that is kept the same on purpose.
Control group
The group that does not get the treatment; it is the point of comparison.
Placebo
A treatment that looks real but has no active ingredient.
Sample
The group of cases or people that is studied.
Selection bias
Groups that were already different before the study because they were not formed at random.
Confounding variable
A factor that changes along with the treatment and confuses the conclusion.
Replication
Repeating an experiment to check its result.
