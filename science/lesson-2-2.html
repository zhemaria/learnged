Science · Topic 2 — Quantitative reasoning in science

Lesson 2.2 · From the table to the graph, and from the graph to the text

Most questions on the test come with a graph or a table. Read it in five steps.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.1.c · Graphs and tablesSP.3 · Reasoning from data

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the same five steps to read any science graph.
  • Tell apart the four most common graphs on the test (multiple lines, double bars, scatter, circle) and what each one is for.
  • Read values from a graph and describe its trend with numbers.
  • Interpolate and extrapolate, telling what the graph supports from what is only a guess.
  • Translate the same information between table, graph, and paragraph without losing or adding anything.

Before you start

Lesson 2.1 taught you to write any size, however tiny or huge. This lesson teaches you to read it on an axis. It is the same support for numbers, seen from the other side: there you wrote the number; here you interpret it.

A very large share of GED® Science questions use a graph, a table, or a diagram. You will not be asked to draw them, but you will be asked to take from them exactly the information the question needs. How to study this lesson: describe each graph out loud before reading the question. If you read the question first, you only look for what it mentions and miss half the information.

1Five steps before you read a single number

Every science graph is read with the same five steps, always in this order. Skipping the first steps to go straight to a number is the most common cause of wrong answers: the number you read is right, but it belongs to something else.

The five steps for reading any science graph, what each one checks, and the mistake it prevents
StepWhat you checkMistake it prevents
1. TitleWhat the graph is about: which group, place, or time.Applying the data to the wrong situation.
2. AxesWhich variable is on the horizontal axis and which on the vertical axis.Switching cause and effect when you describe the relationship.
3. UnitsWhat each axis is measured in: years, grams, parts per million, percent.Giving a number with no unit, which means nothing in science.
4. ScaleHow much each division is worth, and whether the vertical axis starts at zero.Seeing a huge difference where there is only a small one.
5. TrendWhere the line is going, or how the bars compare, as a whole.Getting stuck on one point and missing the overall pattern.

The reference graph for this lesson

Average carbon dioxide (CO₂) in the atmosphere, Mauna Loa Observatory (Hawaii), 1960–2020

Example 1 · The five steps, applied

1Title: average CO₂ in the air, measured at one observatory from 1960 to 2020. These are not world data from every station, and not every gas.

2Axes: the horizontal axis is time; the vertical axis is the concentration. The concentration depends on the year, not the other way around.

3Units: years on the horizontal axis, parts per million (ppm) on the vertical axis. Saying “it reached 414” without the unit answers nothing.

4Scale: each vertical division is 20 ppm, and the axis starts at 300, not at zero. The real rise is smaller than the steep line suggests.

5Trend: it rises the whole sixty years, and faster and faster.

2The four kinds of graphs on the test

Each kind of graph answers a different question. If you know that question, you can rule out right away the options that ask the graph for something its format cannot give.

The four kinds of graphs most common on the test, the question each one answers, and what it cannot show
KindQuestion it answersWhat it can NOT show
Multiple linesHow does each group change over time, and when do they separate or cross?The share of each group in a total.
Double barsHow do two conditions compare within each category? It is the typical graph of control group against experimental group.A continuous change: there are no in-between values between two bars.
Scatter plotIs there a relationship between two variables measured on the same individuals? Is it positive, negative, or none?Which variable causes the other. A scatter plot shows correlation, never cause.
Circle graph (pie chart)What fraction of the total is each part?Actual amounts, or changes over time. The parts always add up to 100%.

Correlation is not cause

In a scatter plot, two variables can go up together without one causing the other. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, and the cause of both is a third variable: hot weather. The test often includes an option that confuses correlation with cause, and it is usually the most tempting one.

3Reading values and describing the trend

To read a value, find the point on the horizontal axis, go up to the line, and across to the vertical axis. The hard part is not the steps but the scale: when the point falls between two divisions, you have to estimate the value in between.

Example 2 · Reading a value between divisions

On the CO₂ graph, what is the concentration in 1990?

1Find 1990 on the horizontal axis and go up to the line.

2The point is between the 340 and 360 ppm lines, closer to the top one.

3Each division is 20 ppm, and the point is about three quarters of the way up.

✓Result: about 354 ppm. On the test, the options are far enough apart that a careful estimate is enough.

Describe the trend with numbers

Saying a line “goes up” is right but not enough. A scientific description says how much it goes up, over what time, and whether the pace changes.

Vague descriptions compared with descriptions that use numbers
Not enoughWith numbersWhat it adds
“CO₂ goes up.”“CO₂ went from 317 to 414 ppm between 1960 and 2020: an increase of 97 ppm in sixty years.”How big the change is and over what time.
“It goes up pretty fast.”“In the first decade it rose 9 ppm; in the last, 24 ppm.”That the pace is not steady but speeding up.
“The plants grew more.”“The experimental group reached 18 cm against 12 cm for the control group: 50% more.”The comparison as a difference and as a percent.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A sentence that always works

“The variable on the vertical axis increases / decreases by [amount] [unit] when the variable on the horizontal axis goes from [value] to [value].” If you fill in those four pieces, you have described the trend correctly.

4Interpolate, extrapolate, and know where the limit is

Both operations estimate a value that was not measured. The difference is where that value falls, and that changes everything.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

The two words share a root and differ in the prefix. Inter- means “between” (international, intercellular): to interpolate is to estimate a value between two measured points. Extra- means “outside of” (extraordinary): to extrapolate is to estimate a value outside the measured range. The prefix alone tells you which one is reliable.

Interpolating and extrapolating: where the estimate falls and how reliable it is
OperationWhere the estimate fallsHow reliable
InterpolateBetween two measured points, inside the range of the data.High. Data on both sides support the estimate.
ExtrapolateBeyond the last measured point, outside the range of the data.Limited. It assumes the trend continues, and no data guarantee that.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Example 3 · Both operations on the same graph

Interpolation. What was the CO₂ level around 1995? 1995 is between 1990 (354 ppm) and 2000 (369 ppm), so the value is near the middle: about 361 ppm. The estimate is solid.

Extrapolation. What will it be in 2050? If the pace of the last decade continued, it would pass 480 ppm. But that number depends completely on nothing changing (emissions, what the oceans absorb, environmental policy), and the graph contains no information about any of that.

✓How to word it: “If the trend continues, we can expect…” That condition is not just polite: it is what separates a scientific prediction from a guess.

The extrapolation trap on the test

The options often include one that extrapolates with full certainty (“in 2050 there will be exactly 480 ppm”) and one with a condition (“if the current pace continues, it will approach 480 ppm”). Both have the same number. The right one is always the one that keeps the condition.

5The scale that fools you

Step 4 of the checklist, checking whether the vertical axis starts at zero, looks like a small detail. In practice, it causes the most wrong conclusions. These two graphs have exactly the same data.

The real difference between treatment A and D is six percentage points. On the right, the last bar looks four times taller than the first. No data were faked: the axis was only cut.

How to answer when the scale is cut

A cut axis does not make the graph wrong (often it is needed to see the differences), but it means you must read the numbers instead of trusting the height. For “how many times larger…?” questions, always calculate with the values, never by comparing bars by eye.

6Translating three ways

The same information can be shown as a table, a graph, or a paragraph. All three say the same thing, but each is best for a different purpose: the table gives exact values, the graph shows the shape of the pattern, and the paragraph explains what it means.

Example 4 · From the table to a paragraph

“The solubility of potassium nitrate increases with temperature. At 0 °C, 13 grams dissolve in 100 mL of water, and at 80 °C, 169 grams dissolve, about thirteen times more. The increase is not steady: from 0 to 20 °C it grows 19 grams, while from 60 to 80 °C it grows 59.”

✓What was kept: the extreme values, the direction of the relationship, and the fact that the pace speeds up.

✗What was not added: any explanation of why it happens. Translating between formats cannot add information the original did not have.

The most common mistake when translating

Adding a cause. When going from the table to a paragraph, it is tempting to write “because heat breaks apart the particles of the solid.” That may be true, but it is not in the data: the table records what happens, not why. On the test, the option that explains the mechanism is usually wrong when the question only asks you to describe the graph.

7Science reading

A short science text written for this level. As you read, think about what graph would go with it.

📖 An experiment on seeds sprouting

A research team wanted to find out whether soil temperature affects how fast bean seeds sprout. It planted four batches of one hundred seeds each and kept each batch at a different steady temperature: 10, 15, 20, and 25 degrees Celsius (50, 59, 68, and 77 °F). Everything else (moisture, soil, light, and planting depth) was the same in all four batches.

After ten days, they counted the sprouted seeds in each batch. At 10 °C, 22 had sprouted; at 15 °C, 51; at 20 °C, 78; and at 25 °C, 84. The team noted that the increase was large between 10 and 20 degrees, but much smaller between 20 and 25.

In their report, the authors said the study cannot tell what would happen at 30 degrees, because no measurement was made above 25. They added that it also cannot show a single best temperature, because the range between 20 and 25 degrees was not studied in enough detail.

The report included a graph with temperature on the horizontal axis and the number of sprouted seeds on the vertical axis.

📘Key words

Axis
One of the two reference lines of a graph: horizontal (x) or vertical (y).
Scale
How much each division of an axis is worth.
Trend
The overall direction of the data.
Interpolate
To estimate a value between two measured points.
Extrapolate
To estimate a value outside the measured range.
Correlation
Two variables that change together; it does not prove that one causes the other.
ppm (parts per million)
A unit for very small concentrations: how many parts of a substance in a million parts of the mixture.
