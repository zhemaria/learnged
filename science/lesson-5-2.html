Before you start

Lesson 5.1 worked on the single atom and ended with a statement left hanging: the bond in water is covalent, but the electrons are not shared equally. This lesson pays that debt. How water acts as a solvent, its density, and even the fact that ice floats are all explained by it.

A word on method: this is the most numerical lesson of the topic. You already know how to rearrange formulas from Math Level 3, so it is not taught again here: it is used. What is worked on explicitly is what the test penalizes most often in these items, which is not the algebra but the units and the decision of which variable to solve for.

How to study this lesson: whenever you can, measure before you calculate. Density is one of the few ideas where the number and what you see (it floats or it sinks) can be checked at the same moment, with a simple glass of water.

1The three states and the energy of particles

The same substance can come in three states and still be the same substance. Liquid water, ice, and steam are all made of identical H₂O molecules: what changes is not the molecule, but how much energy it has and how freely it can move.

Changes of state

Each change of state has its own name, and they fall into two families depending on the energy: those that absorb heat and those that release it.

VaporizationLiquidGasAbsorbsWater boiling in a pot.
CondensationGasLiquidReleasesDrops on the outside of a cold glass.
SublimationSolidGasAbsorbsDry ice, which turns straight into gas.
DepositionGasSolidReleasesFrost forming on a cold surface.

A change of state is not a chemical change

Water is still water in all three states: its molecules do not break, they only move apart or closer together. That is why you can reverse the process just by adding or removing heat. Lesson 5.3 brings chemical changes, where bonds break and new substances appear. Telling one from the other is a common test question.

2The heating curve: why the temperature stops

If you heat a block of ice with a steady heat source and write down the temperature every minute, the graph is not a straight line that keeps going up. It has two flat parts, two plateaus, and those flat parts are what this section is about.

The explanation is the same for both plateaus. The heat going in is not used to speed up the particles (which is what the thermometer measures), but to pull them apart, that is, to overcome the forces holding them together. As long as any ice is left to melt, all the energy goes to that job, and the thermometer stays stuck at zero.

The mistake the test is hunting for

Reading a plateau as “nothing is happening” or “the heat was turned off.” The most important thing is happening: that is exactly where the change of state takes place. A plateau on a heating curve always means two states are present at the same time.

This shape of graph has appeared before in the course. In Lesson 3.2 photosynthesis leveled off at the saturation point, and in 4.3 population growth leveled off at the carrying capacity. In all three cases a plateau means the same thing: something keeps the process from going up. The limiting factor changes, not the way you read the graph.

3Density: one formula, three forms

Density answers a question that mass alone cannot: how much matter is packed into a given volume. A kilogram of lead and a kilogram of feathers have the same mass, yet nobody would confuse the two bundles. The difference is the volume that mass takes up.

One formula, three forms

The relationship is one, written three ways depending on what you are looking for:

  • To find the density: d = m ÷ V
  • To find the mass: m = d × V
  • To find the volume: V = m ÷ d

Rearranging it is the same as in Math Level 3 and needs nothing new. The only thing to decide before calculating is which of the three quantities is missing, because the question always gives two and asks for the third.

Units, where the points are lost

Density is given in grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³) for solids and grams per milliliter (g/mL) for liquids. They are not two different units: 1 cm³ is exactly 1 mL, so the values can be compared with no conversion. (In U.S. units, 1 g/cm³ is about 62 pounds per cubic foot.)

From that table comes the prediction rule, with no calculation needed: a material floats in water if its density is less than 1.00 g/cm³ and sinks if it is greater. And it shows something worth stopping for: ice is less dense than liquid water, which is very rare in nature, where the solid is usually denser than its liquid. It happens because when water freezes its molecules line up in an open structure with gaps, which takes more space. The cause is the uneven covalent bond named in 5.1.

Why it matters that ice floats

If ice sank, lakes would freeze from the bottom up and the living things in them would not survive the winter. The layer of ice on top acts as insulation and keeps the water below liquid. It is a good example of how a physical property of water shapes the ecosystems of Topic 4.

Example 1 · Finding the density

An object has a mass of 54 g and a volume of 20 cm³. What is its density? Will it float in water?

1What is missing. You have mass and volume, so you want density: d = m ÷ V.

2Substitute. d = 54 g ÷ 20 cm³.

3Calculate. d = 2.7 g/cm³. The unit is not invented: it comes from dividing grams by cubic centimeters.

4Interpret. 2.7 is greater than 1.00, so it sinks. From the table, it could be aluminum.

Example 2 · Finding the mass

Cooking oil has a density of 0.92 g/mL. What is the mass of 250 mL of oil?

1What is missing. Density and volume are given; mass is asked: m = d × V.

2Substitute. m = 0.92 g/mL × 250 mL.

3Calculate. m = 230 g. The milliliters cancel and grams are left, just what was asked.

4Common-sense check. The result should be a bit less than 250, because each milliliter weighs a bit less than a gram. It is.

Example 3 · Finding the volume

A sample of mercury has a mass of 100 g. Its density is 13.6 g/cm³. What volume does it take up?

1What is missing. Mass and density are given; volume is asked: V = m ÷ d.

2Substitute. V = 100 g ÷ 13.6 g/cm³.

3Calculate. V ≈ 7.4 cm³. The grams cancel and cubic centimeters are left.

4Common-sense check. One hundred grams of mercury take up much less space than one hundred grams of water, which would take 100 cm³. A small result is just what you expect from such a dense material.

🧮 Three warnings about units on the test

The result always needs a unit, because a number alone is not a density. You get the unit by working with the units of the data, not by choosing it from memory. And when a question mixes milliliters and cubic centimeters, there is nothing to convert: they are equal. On the TI-30XS, also check that the memory is clear before doing several problems in a row.

4Mixtures, solutions, and solubility curves

Almost nothing we handle every day is a pure substance. Air, tap water, milk, and steel are mixtures, and it helps to sort out the vocabulary before reading a curve.

Pure substance, homogeneous mixture (solution), and heterogeneous mixture: what each is, how it looks, and an example
KindWhat it isHow it looksExample
Pure substanceOne kind of matter: an element or a compoundUniform, and cannot be separated by physical meansDistilled water, gold, pure salt
Homogeneous mixture (solution)Two or more substances mixed evenlyYou see one phase: you cannot tell the parts apartSalt water, air, steel
Heterogeneous mixtureTwo or more substances not spread evenlyYou can see the partsSand in water, salad, granite

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

In a solution, the substance that dissolves is the solute and the one that dissolves it is the solvent. In salt water, salt is the solute and water the solvent. Water is the most common solvent on the planet for exactly the reason given in 5.1: its molecule has one side slightly positive and the other slightly negative, which lets it surround and separate the ions of many compounds.

Saturation: how much fits

A solvent cannot take unlimited solute. Depending on how much it holds, a solution is unsaturated if it can still take more solute; saturated if it has reached the most it can dissolve at that temperature; and supersaturated if it holds more than that maximum, which can only be done by heating and then cooling carefully, and which is unstable.

Read the curve like any two-variable graph from Lesson 2.2: start at the temperature on the horizontal axis, go up to the curve, and read on the vertical axis how many grams dissolve in 100 g of water. What this graph adds is that the curve itself is a boundary: points on it are saturated solutions, points below are unsaturated, and points above are supersaturated.

Solids and gases do opposite things with temperature

The test asks with everyday examples. Sugar dissolves much better in hot coffee than in cold coffee: it is a solid. But a soda loses its fizz much faster when it is warm, and fish suffer in the warm water of a river because it holds less dissolved oxygen: those are gases. Short rule: heat helps a solid dissolve and drives a gas out.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Density comes from the Latin densus, thick or packed, and describes exactly that: how tightly matter is packed in a volume. Solute and solvent share the Latin root solvere, to loosen or untie, the same as in dissolve and solution: the solvent “unties” the solute and spreads it out. Saturated comes from satur, full; so supersaturated literally means fuller than it can hold, and the saturation point of photosynthesis in Lesson 3.2 has the same name for the same reason. Sublimation, from the Latin sublimare, to lift up, is going straight from solid to gas. Homogeneous and heterogeneous differ by their Greek prefix: homo-, same, against hetero-, different, with génos, kind.

5Science reading

A short science text written for this level. Notice what the study measures and what it does not.

📖 Dissolved oxygen in a river

The oxygen fish breathe is not the oxygen in the water molecule, but oxygen gas dissolved between the molecules. How much a river can hold depends on several things, and one of the main ones is temperature: cold water holds more dissolved gas than warm water.

For two years, a team measured the dissolved oxygen in a stretch of river downstream from a power plant that uses river water to cool itself and returns it warmer. Measurements were taken every week at two points: one upstream of the plant and another three hundred meters (about 1,000 feet) downstream.

The downstream point was two to four degrees warmer and had consistently less dissolved oxygen. In the summer months the difference grew, and at the lower point the values came close to the limit several species can tolerate. The authors also recorded fewer trout in that stretch during the same months.

In their report, the authors say the drop in oxygen fits the rise in temperature they recorded, but they warn that an irrigation canal, whose water was not analyzed, also empties into the lower stretch. They conclude that more sampling would be needed to find out how much each factor adds to the difference.

7Homework
