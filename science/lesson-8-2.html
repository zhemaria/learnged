Science · Topic 8 — Putting it together: evaluating conclusions and GED preparation

Lesson 8.2 · Final project: my own investigation

Design a small investigation about something around you, collect your own data, and defend a conclusion that fits them.

⏱️ About 4 hours, spread over two or three weeks SP.1 – SP.8 · All the practices of the course No quiz · the project is the assessment

🎯What you will do

  • Design a simple investigation about something in your own surroundings.
  • Write a question you can investigate and a hypothesis you can test.
  • Name the variables and set up a fair control.
  • Collect your own data, organize them in a table, and show them in the right kind of graph.
  • Write a conclusion that fits the data and admits at least one limitation.
  • Present your investigation out loud, using correct science words.

Before you start

This page is your workbook. It is not a lesson to read: it is a template you fill in over about three weeks. Each stage has its fields, and at the end everything comes together in one report.

The calendar. Start early, because some investigations need several days of observation. A good pace: week one for stages 1 to 3, week two to collect data, and week three for the conclusion and the presentation.

The circle closes. Topic 1 taught you to write a testable hypothesis and isolate a variable. Everything below is doing it for real, with your own question and your own data.

💾How to use this workbook

Type straight into the fields. The page saves a draft in this browser by itself every few seconds, and you can also press Save draft. When you come back to this page on the same device, you will find your work where you left it.

A saved draft is not a backup

It is only on this device and is lost if the browser’s data are cleared or if you use another device. To keep your work, press Make the report and copy the text, or use Print or save as PDF.

📊The rubric, from day one

This rubric does not appear at the end to grade you: you get it now, at the start, because it is the guide for the work. Each criterion says what the project must contain, and the “Advanced” column shows what to aim for. Five criteria, four levels each: up to 20 points.

Look at criterion 4

Admitting a limitation adds points; it does not take them away. A project that admits what its data cannot show is better done, not worse.

❓Choosing the question

A question you can investigate is one you can answer by measuring something. Questions that ask for an opinion (“Is coffee better than tea?”) or that you answer by looking it up (“At what temperature does water boil?”) do not count. The test is simple: if answering it means doing something and writing down numbers, you can investigate it.

Start small

A good project for this course is done at home, with what you have at home, and changes only one thing. Projects almost never fail for lack of ambition: they fail by trying to measure too many things at once.

Twenty questions you can investigate at home, by area
AreaPossible questions
Heat and temperatureDoes coffee cool faster in a ceramic mug or a metal one? · Does a lid change how fast it cools? · How long does an ice cube take to melt in different liquids? · Does a dark surface or a light one get hotter in the sun?
Plants and sproutingDo seeds sprout faster with light or without it? · Does the amount of water change a plant’s height? · Does a plant grow the same with tap water and with boiled, cooled water? · Does planting depth matter?
Dissolving and materialsDoes sugar dissolve faster in cold or hot water? · Does stirring change the dissolving time? · Which material soaks up the most water in one minute? · How many drops of water fit on a coin, by coin size?
Motion and forceDoes a ball rolled from a higher ramp go farther? · Does the surface change how far an object slides? · Does the shape of a sheet of paper change how long it takes to fall? · How long does a pendulum take to stop, by its length?
Everyday lifeWhich appliance at home uses the most electricity in an hour? · Does the temperature change between rooms during the day? · How much water is used brushing your teeth with the faucet on versus off? · Does street noise change with the time of day?

Any other question is welcome, as long as it meets three conditions: it is answered by measuring, only one thing changes at a time, and it can be done safely with what you have. If you come up with your own, check with your teacher that it meets all three before you start collecting data.

Complete each stage at its time. Do not write the conclusion before you have the data, and do not start measuring before you decide what stays the same.

1Stage 1 · The question

Write the question you will investigate. It must be something you can answer by measuring.

Write it as a full question. Example: “Does coffee cool faster in a ceramic mug than in a metal one?”

Two or three lines. If something from the course relates to your question, mention it.

2Stage 2 · The hypothesis

A hypothesis predicts a specific result and could turn out to be false. “I’ll see what happens” is not a hypothesis; “coffee will cool faster in the metal mug” is.

The formula that helps

“If [I change this], then [this other thing will happen], because [reason].” The “because” part is what makes a hypothesis more than a simple guess.

3Stage 3 · The design

Here you decide what you change, what you measure, and what you keep the same. It counts the most in the rubric, and it ruins the most projects when it is rushed.

Only one. Example: the material of the mug.

Also say with what tool and in what unit. Example: temperature, with a thermometer, in degrees Celsius or Fahrenheit.

Everything else. Example: the same amount of liquid, the same starting temperature, the same place, the same time of day.

What do you compare with? How many times do you repeat each measurement? Repeating at least three times reduces the effect of a one-time error.

Write it so that someone else could repeat it exactly without asking you anything.

Safety first

No project in this course needs dangerous substances, fire without supervision, household electrical wiring, or animals. If your design seems to need any of those, change it before you go on.

4Stage 4 · The data

Write down each measurement as you take it, not from memory at the end. If a value looks odd, do not erase it: write it down and comment on it later. Write the condition the same way every time (for example, always “metal” and “ceramic”), so the page can group your trials.

Which graph?

Choose by your data: bars to compare different categories (metal mug versus ceramic); lines to follow a change over time; circle (pie) only for parts of a whole. The summary above draws a bar graph of the averages to help you check; your own graph, by hand or with a spreadsheet, needs labeled axes and units.

5Stage 5 · The conclusion

Answer your starting question with what your data show, no more and no less. It is exactly the rule of Lesson 8.1, applied to your own work.

Start by answering the question, and right away cite the data point behind it. Example: “Coffee cooled faster in metal: it dropped 32 °F (18 °C) against 20 °F (11 °C) in the same time.”

If not, say so clearly. A hypothesis disproved with honest data is worth as much as a confirmed one; what the rubric penalizes is forcing the data to fit.

Required, and it earns points. Example: I measured on only one day, or I used one thermometer without checking it, or I did three trials and more might be needed.

6Stage 6 · The presentation

Five minutes, following the order of the stages, in front of a family member, a friend, or your phone’s camera. Then ask the person listening to ask you two questions. Prepare your script here.

One or two sentences per stage: question, hypothesis, design, data, graph, conclusion. Do not read the whole report: tell what matters.

Expecting them is the best preparation. Your answers should rest on your data.

🔤Project vocabulary

Hypothesis comes from the Greek hypó-, under, and thesis, placing: what is placed underneath as a temporary base, waiting to be checked. Variable comes from the Latin variare, to change; the independent one changes because the investigator decides it, and the dependent one changes in response. Control, from the old French contre-rôle, the duplicate record used to check the original: the control group is exactly that second copy you compare with. Data comes from datum, what is given, and that is its nature: you collect it, you do not decide it. Conclusion, from com- and claudere, to close together: what closes the reasoning. Limitation, from limes, border: marking how far your claim can go.

✅How to grade it

Press Make the report, copy the text, and give it to your teacher to grade with the rubric of this project, criterion by criterion. Before you hand it in, grade it yourself with the same rubric, and apply the rule of Lesson 8.1: does every claim in your conclusion rest on a number in your table? Then check your presentation against criterion 5.
