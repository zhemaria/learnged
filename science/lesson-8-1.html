Science · Topic 8 — Putting it together: evaluating conclusions and GED preparation

Lesson 8.1 · Multiple findings: judging conclusions with evidence

When two studies disagree, find where they differ; when data meet a conclusion, decide whether they support it, contradict it, or are beside the point.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SP.4 · Judging conclusions with evidenceSP.5 · Reconciling sources that disagree

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Reconcile results from two or more sources that do not agree.
  • Find the exact point where two studies differ: method, sample, or scope.
  • Decide whether a data set supports, contradicts, or is irrelevant to a proposed conclusion.
  • Tell fact, interpretation, and speculation apart in the same science text.
  • Justify every verdict by citing the specific data point it rests on.

Before you start

The last topic, and the purpose changes. The seven topics before this one taught content. This one adds none: it takes the two most tested science practices, SP.4 (judging conclusions) and SP.5 (auditing sources), and works them hard.

Here is why. On the Science test, most items do not ask what you know, but what you can conclude from the passage you just read. A student who knows the content but cannot tell what a data point supports from what it only suggests loses points on questions whose content they know perfectly well. This lesson trains that difference.

The practice has already started. Lesson 4.3 asked you to separate a supported conclusion from one that goes beyond the data; 7.1 showed that Wegener lacked a mechanism, not evidence; 7.3 presented two reports that disagreed without either one lying. Here, all of that becomes a system.

1When two studies do not agree

Finding two different results about the same thing triggers a quick and mistaken reaction: assuming one of them is wrong. Sometimes that is true. But most disagreements are not contradictions; they are differences in how each result was obtained.

Before saying two studies contradict each other, check three things.

The three places where studies can differ, with an example of each and the right conclusion
Where they differExampleRight conclusion
MethodOne study measures the water temperature 10 cm (4 inches) below the surface and the other at the surfaceThe values cannot be compared directly; there is no contradiction
SampleOne study looks at a lake in the north and the other at a lake in the south, in different seasonsBoth results can be right at the same time
ScopeOne says “the fertilizer increases growth” and the other “it does not increase it in clay soil”No contradiction: the second one limits a condition of the first
None: a real contradictionSame method, same sample, same scope, and the results still cannot both be trueA genuine disagreement that calls for more research

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The question that organizes the whole analysis

It is not “which one is right?” but “where exactly do they differ?” Finding the difference usually settles the apparent conflict without throwing anyone out. And when the test asks how to reconcile two findings, the right answer almost always points to a difference in conditions, not to a mistake.

And yet, not everything can be reconciled

Avoid the opposite extreme: accepting any result on the grounds that “each study measures something different.” When method, sample, and scope match and the data still cannot both be true, the honest answer is that there is a real disagreement and more data are needed. Saying so is a correct answer on the test, not a dodge.

2Do the data support the conclusion?

This is the second block of the lesson and the most common one on the test. Given a proposed conclusion and a set of data, there are only three possible verdicts. Name them all, because the third is the one people forget.

The three possible verdicts when judging whether data support a conclusion, with when each applies and an example
VerdictWhen it appliesExample
SupportsThe data point the same way as the conclusionThe conclusion says the plant grows more with light, and the plants in light were taller
ContradictsThe data point the opposite waySame conclusion, but the plants in the dark were taller
Is irrelevantThe data say nothing about the conclusion, for or againstThe conclusion is about light, and the data record only the type of pot

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

“Irrelevant” is a real answer, and rarely chosen

The test includes true data that are beside the point on purpose, because someone who feels they must take a side ends up forcing a link that is not there. A fact can be correct and add nothing to the conclusion being judged. With any piece of data, the first question is whether it is about the same thing.

The four-column template

1Claim. Write the proposed conclusion as it is stated, without softening it.

2Evidence for. Which specific data support it, cited with their numbers.

3Evidence against. Which data contradict or limit it.

4Verdict. Supports, contradicts, or irrelevant, with the specific data point that justifies it. A verdict with no data does not count.

✓The last column pays off most. Writing “supports” alone proves nothing; writing “supports, because the plants in light measured 18 cm against 11 cm” does. You do not write the justification on the test, but the right option is always the one that could be justified that way. Use the template on paper, in every exercise, with no exceptions: repetition is what makes it automatic when the clock is running.

3Fact, interpretation, and speculation

All three appear mixed in the same science text, written in the same tone and with no warning. Telling them apart is the third skill of the lesson, and the most useful one outside the test.

Fact, interpretation, and speculation: what each is, the words that usually signal it, and an example
CategoryWhat it isWords that usually signal itExample
Fact (observation)What was measured or observed. It can be checked by measuring again“was recorded,” “was measured,” “the data show”The average temperature of the lower stretch of the river was 3 °C (5 °F) higher
InterpretationThe explanation the author gives for those facts. It is reasoned, but it is not the data“this indicates,” “the results suggest,” “is attributed to”That difference is attributed to the power plant’s discharge
SpeculationWhat might happen or explain things, with no data to support it yet“it is possible that,” “could,” “one might expect,” “if confirmed”It is possible that the effect extends downstream of the stretch studied

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The verb is the most reliable clue

“Was recorded” introduces a fact; “suggests” introduces an interpretation; “could” introduces speculation. Careful science texts mark that difference, and test passages respect it. Reading with an eye on the verbs solves most of these items.

Speculating is not a flaw

An article that ends by proposing what to study next is doing its job, as the solar-farm reading in Lesson 7.3 did without losing rigor. The problem comes when the reader takes speculation for fact. The test does not punish the author for speculating; it punishes the reader who does not notice.

🔤 Science vocabulary: roots

Evidence comes from the Latin videre, to see, the root of visible and evident: what can be shown. Reconcile, from re-, again, and conciliare, to bring together: to make two things that seemed opposed fit again, which is exactly the task of section 1. Interpret comes from interpres, go-between: someone who stands between the data and the reader to explain it. Speculate comes from speculari, to watch from afar; it keeps that sense of looking beyond what is in front of you. Verdict joins verum, true, and dictum, said: what is declared true after weighing the evidence. And criterion, from the Greek kritḗrion, a means of judging, from krínein, to separate or decide, which also gave us critic.

4Science reading

A short science text written for this level. As you read, sort out in your head what was measured, what is explained, and what is only proposed.

📖 Two bee counts that did not match

Two teams studied the bee population in the same farming county and published results that seemed opposite. The first reported a 30 % drop in five years. The second, working in the same county over the same period, found no noticeable drop.

Comparing the methods revealed the difference. The first team counted hives kept by beekeepers, listed in an official census. The second counted wild bees using traps placed across the land. They were not measuring the same thing: one number reflects beekeeping activity and the other, the wild population.

A later analysis brought both data sets together. The authors point out that the drop in registered hives happened at the same time as several beekeeping farms in the area shut down, and that this fact explains much of the first count’s drop without assuming a high death rate.

The report adds that the wild population might have benefited from less competition for nectar, though it warns that the study design cannot check this and that a specific follow-up study would be needed to find out.

📘Key words

Method
How a study measured: instruments, place, depth, time of day.
Sample
What or whom a study observed: population, place, period.
Scope
How broad a claim is: general or limited to a condition.
Verdict
Supports, contradicts, or irrelevant, always with the data point behind it.
Fact
What was measured or observed; can be checked again.
Interpretation
The author’s reasoned explanation of the facts.
Speculation
What might be true, with no data behind it yet.
