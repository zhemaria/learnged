1The coordinate plane and ordered pairs

A link with Level 3. In Level 3 you used the Pythagorean theorem (a² + b² = c²) to find a missing side of a right triangle. Level 4 opens by taking that same theorem to a new place: the coordinate plane. The distance formula is not a new formula to memorize; it is the Pythagorean theorem written with coordinates. On this base you will build, in the next lessons, everything about lines and functions.

The coordinate plane is made of two perpendicular number lines: the x-axis (horizontal) and the y-axis (vertical). They cross at the origin, (0, 0). Each point is located with an ordered pair (x, y): first count across (x), then up or down (y).

The axes split the plane into four quadrants, numbered with Roman numerals counterclockwise, starting at the upper right.

Sign of x and of y in each quadrant, with an example

IInegative (−)positive (+)(−4, 3)
IIInegative (−)negative (−)(−2, −3)

The order matters

The pair (3, 2) is not the same point as (2, 3). In (x, y), the first coordinate always tells the move across and the second, the move up or down. A point on the x-axis has the form (x, 0); a point on the y-axis has the form (0, y).

2The distance between two points

To find the distance between two points, a right triangle appears naturally: the horizontal difference of the coordinates (Δx, “delta x”) is one leg, the vertical difference (Δy) is the other, and the distance you want is the hypotenuse. By the Pythagorean theorem:

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Example 1: legs 3 and 4. Find the distance between P(1, 2) and Q(4, 6).

1Find the differences. x₂ − x₁ = 4 − 1 = 3 and y₂ − y₁ = 6 − 2 = 4.

2Substitute. d = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16).

3Add and take the square root. d = √25 = 5.

✓Answer. The distance is 5 units.

Example 2: with negative coordinates. Find the distance between A(−2, 1) and B(3, 13).

1Differences. 3 − (−2) = 5 and 13 − 1 = 12.

2Substitute. d = √(5² + 12²) = √(25 + 144) = √169.

3Square root. d = 13.

✓Answer. The distance is 13 units.

The order of the subtraction does not matter

Because the differences are squared, it makes no difference whether you subtract (x₂ − x₁) or (x₁ − x₂): squaring turns any result positive. Be careful with negative coordinates, though: from −2 to 3 is 3 − (−2) = 5, not 3 − 2.

🧮 With the calculator

When the sum of the squares is not a perfect square, use the calculator. Between (1, 1) and (4, 3): √(3² + 2²) = √13 ≈ 3.6. Round to the tenth unless the problem says otherwise.

3The midpoint of a segment

The midpoint is the point exactly halfway along the segment that joins two points. Its x-coordinate is the average of the two x-coordinates, and its y-coordinate is the average of the two y-coordinates:

M = ( (x₁ + x₂) ÷ 2 , (y₁ + y₂) ÷ 2 )

Example 3: a midpoint. Find the midpoint of the segment from A(2, 4) to B(8, 10).

1Average of the x's. (2 + 8) ÷ 2 = 10 ÷ 2 = 5.

2Average of the y's. (4 + 10) ÷ 2 = 14 ÷ 2 = 7.

✓Answer. The midpoint is M(5, 7).

Example 4: finding an endpoint from the midpoint. The midpoint of a segment is M(4, 3) and one endpoint is A(2, 1). What is the other endpoint B(x, y)?

1For x. (2 + x) ÷ 2 = 4, so 2 + x = 8, and x = 6.

2For y. (1 + y) ÷ 2 = 3, so 1 + y = 6, and y = 5.

✓Answer. The other endpoint is B(6, 5). Check: the midpoint of (2, 1) and (6, 5) is (4, 3).

Two common slips

The first is adding and forgetting to divide by 2: the point lands twice as far away. The second is subtracting instead of adding: the difference is used for the distance, not for the midpoint. If a sum is odd, the coordinate ends in .5, and that is fine.

4Figures on the plane: the perimeter

When the vertices of a figure are given as points on the plane, the distance formula lets you measure each side and add them to get the perimeter.

Example 5: the perimeter of a triangle. A triangle has vertices A(0, 0), B(3, 0), and C(0, 4). Find its perimeter.

1Side AB (horizontal). Same y, so subtract the x's: 3 − 0 = 3.

2Side AC (vertical). Same x, so subtract the y's: 4 − 0 = 4.

3Side BC (slanted). d = √[(0 − 3)² + (4 − 0)²] = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

4Perimeter. 3 + 4 + 5 = 12.

✓Answer. The perimeter is 12 units.

A useful shortcut

When two points have the same x-coordinate, the segment is vertical and its length is the difference of the y's. When they have the same y, it is horizontal and its length is the difference of the x's. Only slanted sides need the whole formula.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with a notebook and graph paper: (1) Eight points on the plane: two in each quadrant, one on the x-axis, and one on the y-axis. Graph them, write their coordinates, and note the quadrant or the axis of each one. (2) Six distances between two points: two horizontal or vertical segments, two with negative coordinates, and two whose square root is not exact (round to the tenth with the calculator). Write Δx and Δy before you substitute. (3) Four midpoints and two missing-endpoint problems; check each endpoint by finding the midpoint again with both endpoints. (4) A rectangle with vertices (−3, −1), (5, −1), (5, 4), and (−3, 4), and a triangle with vertices (0, 0), (6, 0), and (0, 8): graph them and find the perimeter of each. Say which sides you could measure without the whole formula. About 60 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Take a map of your neighborhood (a printed one or on your phone) and draw a grid on it, with your home at the origin (0, 0) and 1 unit = 1 block. Write the coordinates of three places you go often (the store, the school, the bus stop). Find the straight-line distance from home to each one, and the midpoint between two of them: is there anything there?

📘Key words

coordinate plane
Two perpendicular number lines, the x-axis and the y-axis, that cross at the origin.
ordered pair
(x, y): the move across first, then the move up or down.
quadrant
Each of the four regions the axes make, numbered I to IV counterclockwise.
distance formula
d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]
midpoint
The point halfway along a segment: the average of the x's and of the y's.
