Lesson 2.2 · Composite 3D figures and surface area

Break 3D figures into simple solids to find their volume, find the surface area of cylinders and cones, and compare designs that hold the same volume.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 📐 Formula sheet and calculator

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Break a composite 3D figure into simple solids.
  • Find the volume of composite figures by adding or subtracting volumes.
  • Find the lateral and total surface area of cylinders, cones, and other solids.
  • Compare designs with the same volume by how much material they use.

1Breaking a composite figure into parts

A link with Lesson 2.1. In Lesson 2.1 you found the volume of single solids (sphere, cone, pyramid). Many real objects, though, are not one single solid: a silo is a cylinder topped with a cone; a medicine capsule is a cylinder with two half-spheres. This lesson teaches you to break those figures into simple parts to find their volume, and to find how much material covers their surface.

A composite figure is simple solids put together (or one taken out of another). The plan is always the same:

  1. Name the simple parts that make it up (cylinder, cone, half-sphere, prism…).
  2. Find the volume of each part separately.
  3. Add the volumes if the parts are joined, or subtract if there is a hole.

2The volume of composite figures

Example 1: a cylinder with a half-sphere. A tank is a cylinder with radius 3 feet and height 10 feet, topped by a half-sphere with radius 3 feet. Find its total volume.

1Volume of the cylinder. V = πr²h = π · 9 · 10 = 90π.

2Volume of the half-sphere: half of 4/3 πr³. 1/2 · 4/3 · π · 27 = 1/2 · 36π = 18π.

3Add. 90π + 18π = 108π.

✓Answer. V = 108π ≈ 339.3 cubic feet.

Example 2: with a hole (subtract). A 10 × 10 × 5 inch block has a hole drilled through it: a cylinder with radius 2 inches and height 5 inches. Find the volume of what is left.

1Volume of the block (a prism). 10 · 10 · 5 = 500.

2Volume of the hole. π · 2² · 5 = 20π.

3Subtract. 500 − 20π.

✓Answer. V = 500 − 20π ≈ 437.2 cubic inches.

Add or subtract?

If the parts come together to make a bigger body, add. If a part is taken away (a hole, a hollow), subtract. Make a drawing and label each part before you calculate.

3Surface area

The surface area is the sum of the areas of all the faces or surfaces of a solid. It answers questions like how much paint, paper, or material you need to cover it. These formulas are on the formula sheet too:

Lateral and total surface area formulas of the cylinder and the cone
SolidLateral areaTotal area
Cylinder2πrh2πrh + 2πr²
Coneπrℓ (ℓ = slant height)πrℓ + πr²

Example 3: the lateral area of a cylinder. How much paint covers the side of a round tank with radius 4 feet and height 10 feet?

1Use the lateral area. 2πrh.

2Substitute. 2 · π · 4 · 10 = 80π.

✓Answer. 80π ≈ 251.3 square feet.

Example 4: the lateral area of a cone. A cone has radius 6 inches and slant height (ℓ) 10 inches. Find its lateral area.

1Use the lateral area of the cone. πrℓ.

2Substitute. π · 6 · 10 = 60π.

✓Answer. 60π ≈ 188.5 square inches.

Slant height, not the straight-up height

The lateral area of a cone uses the slant height ℓ (the distance from the edge of the base to the point, along the surface), not the height h. If you only have h, find the slant height with Pythagoras: ℓ = √(r² + h²). And “lateral” means without the base and the top: the total area adds them.

4An idea of optimization

In design and engineering you want a given volume with the least material possible, that is, with the smallest surface area. For example, of two cans that hold the same volume, the one with less surface area costs less material.

Example 5: comparing material. Two boxes hold 100 cubic inches. Box A is 10 × 10 × 1 and box B is 5 × 5 × 4. Which one uses less cardboard (less surface area)?

1Area of A. 2(10·10 + 10·1 + 10·1) = 2(100 + 10 + 10) = 240.

2Area of B. 2(5·5 + 5·4 + 5·4) = 2(25 + 20 + 20) = 130.

✓Answer. With the same volume, box B uses much less cardboard. Shapes closer to a cube tend to have less surface area.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with a notebook, the formula sheet, and a calculator: (1) Three composite figures that you draw. Break each one into parts, write the volume of each part, and add them (or subtract, if there is a hole). (2) A silo made of a cylinder with radius 10 feet and height 30 feet, with a cone 8 feet tall on top. Find the volume of the cylinder and of the cone separately, then the total. (3) A medicine capsule: a cylinder with radius 0.2 inch and length 0.8 inch, with a half-sphere at each end. Find its volume and explain why the two half-spheres make a whole sphere. (4) Painting the silo: find the lateral area of the cylinder (2πrh) and decide whether to include the top and the bottom. If a gallon of paint covers 350 square feet, how many gallons do you need? (5) Four surface areas of different solids (prism, cylinder, cone, or sphere), using the formula sheet. About 70 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Take two boxes or cans from your kitchen that hold about the same amount (a cereal box and a shorter, wider box, for example). Measure them in inches, find the volume and the surface area of each one, and decide which design uses less cardboard or metal. Why do you think the company chose its shape?

📘Key words

composite figure
A solid made of simple solids joined together or with one taken out.
half-sphere (hemisphere)
Half of a sphere: its volume is 1/2 · 4/3 πr³ = 2/3 πr³.
surface area
The sum of the areas of all the surfaces of a solid.
lateral area
The surface area without the bases.
slant height (ℓ)
In a cone or pyramid, the distance along the surface from the edge of the base to the point.
