Lesson 2.1 · Volume of spheres, cones, and pyramids

Use the formula sheet to find the volume of spheres, cones, and pyramids, and find a missing measure from the volume.

1The formula sheet and the three solids

A link with Topic 1. In Topic 1 you measured distances and figures on the plane (two dimensions). Now you move to space (three dimensions). You already know the volume of prisms and cylinders from Level 3; this lesson adds three new solids: the sphere, the cone, and the pyramid. From this topic on, always work with the formula sheet, the same kind you get on test day.

On the GED test you do not memorize the volume formulas: you get them on a formula sheet. The skill that is tested is finding the right formula, substituting the measures correctly, and calculating. On this site, the formula sheet opens with the Reference sheet button at the top of every lesson.

The factor 1/3

The cone and the pyramid both have 1/3. A cone fits three times into a cylinder with the same base and height, and a pyramid fits three times into a prism with the same base and height.

2The volume of a sphere

A sphere is fixed by its radius alone. Its volume is:

V = 4/3 πr³

The radius is cubed (it is a measure in three dimensions), then multiplied by π and by 4/3.

Example 1: find the volume of a sphere with a radius of 3 inches.

1Cube the radius. 3³ = 27.

2Substitute. V = 4/3 · π · 27.

3Simplify. 4/3 · 27 = 36, so V = 36π.

✓Answer. V = 36π ≈ 113.1 cubic inches (with the π key; with π ≈ 3.14 it is 113.0).

The radius, not the diameter

If the problem gives the diameter, divide it by 2 first to get the radius. Putting the diameter straight into the formula is a common mistake.

🧮 Exact or approximate

The exact answer keeps π (for example, 36π). For the approximate value, use what the problem says: π ≈ 3.14 or the π key. The two can differ a little in the last digits; if nothing is said, the π key is more exact.

3The volume of a cone and of a pyramid

The cone has a round base and a point. Its volume is one third of the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height:

V = 1/3 πr²h

Example 2: a cone. Find the volume of a cone with a radius of 3 inches and a height of 10 inches.

1Square the radius. 3² = 9.

2Substitute. V = 1/3 · π · 9 · 10 = 1/3 · π · 90.

3Simplify. 1/3 · 90 = 30, so V = 30π.

✓Answer. V = 30π ≈ 94.2 cubic inches.

The pyramid has a base that is a polygon (often a square or a rectangle). Its volume is one third of the area of the base times the height:

V = 1/3 Bh

Example 3: a pyramid. Find the volume of a pyramid with a square base 6 feet on each side and a height of 10 feet.

1Area of the base. B = 6 × 6 = 36 square feet.

2Substitute. V = 1/3 · 36 · 10 = 1/3 · 360.

3Simplify. V = 120.

✓Answer. V = 120 cubic feet.

Two careful steps

In the cone, only the radius is squared; the height is multiplied as it is. In the pyramid, find the area of the base first (side × side for a square, length × width for a rectangle), and only then use 1/3 × B × h. The h is the straight-up height, not the slanted height of a face.

4Finding a missing measure

Sometimes you know the volume and need the radius or the height. Substitute what you know and solve for the unknown, as in any equation.

Example 4: the height of a cone. A cone has a volume of 30π cubic inches and a radius of 3 inches. What is its height?

1Substitute in V = 1/3 πr²h. 30π = 1/3 · π · 9 · h.

2Simplify the right side. 1/3 · 9 = 3, so 30π = 3πh.

3Divide both sides by 3π. h = 30π ÷ 3π = 10.

✓Answer. The height is 10 inches.

Canceling π makes it easier

When π is on both sides of the equation, it cancels. Then solving only takes simple numbers.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with a notebook, the formula sheet, and a calculator: (1) Four sphere volumes, two from the radius and two from the diameter. Copy the formula from the sheet and write the substitution before you calculate. (2) A round water tank with a radius of 4 feet: how many cubic feet of water does it hold? Also give the answer in gallons (1 cubic foot ≈ 7.48 gallons). (3) Three cones, one of them an ice cream cone with a radius of 1.5 inches and a height of 4 inches. In each one, underline that only the radius is squared, and do not forget the 1/3. (4) Three pyramids, one of them with a square base 755 feet on each side and a height of 481 feet (the Great Pyramid of Giza). Find the area of the base first, and say whether the size of the answer seems reasonable. (5) Two missing-measure problems: from the volume, find the radius or the height. About 70 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Find a ball, a party hat or a paper cup shaped like a cone, and a box-shaped container at home. Measure them in inches and find their volumes. Which holds more? Fill the cone with rice or water and pour it into a cylinder-shaped glass with the same base and height: how many cones does it take?

📘Key words

sphere
A solid where every point is the same distance (the radius) from the center.
cone
A solid with a round base and a point.
pyramid
A solid with a polygon for a base and triangular faces that meet at a point.
height (h)
The straight-up distance from the base to the point, not the slanted one.
cubic units
The units of volume: cubic inches, cubic feet, cubic yards.
