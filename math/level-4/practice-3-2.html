Factoring undoes multiplying

›

Factoring a polynomial means writing it as a product. When you multiply you start from the factors and reach the polynomial; when you factor you start from the polynomial and find which factors produced it. That is why the best check of a factorization is to multiply again. Choose an expression and change the direction.

multiply ⟶
⟵ factor
Two mistakes the check catches. The sign: 3(x − 2) gives 3x − 6, not 3x + 6. And the incomplete factor, when the number outside does not multiply every term: 3(x + 6) gives 3x + 18, not 3x + 6.

The common factor

›

The common factor is made of the greatest common factor (GCF) of the numbers and the smallest power of x that appears in every term. If a term has no x, no x comes out. Check by multiplying: if you do not get the original polynomial back, the factor was wrong or a sign failed.

Difference of squares

›
a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)

A binomial is a difference of squares when both terms are perfect squares and they are subtracted. First decide; then write the two factors.

Both together: common factor, then difference of squares

›

The order never changes: first take out the common factor, then look at what is left inside the parentheses. Sometimes you can keep factoring, and sometimes you cannot. An expression is factored completely when no factor can be broken down any further.
