Math · Level 4 · Topic 3 — Multiplying and factoring

Lesson 3.2 · Factoring: common factor and difference of squares

Factor a polynomial by taking out the common factor and by recognizing a difference of squares, and always check by multiplying.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks ✏️ No calculator

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain that factoring undoes multiplying, and check by expanding.
  • Take out the greatest common factor (number and variable).
  • Factor a difference of squares, a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b).
  • Combine both techniques to factor completely.

1Factoring undoes multiplying

A link with Lesson 3.1. In Lesson 3.1 you multiplied binomials to get a polynomial. Factoring is the way back: you start from the polynomial and find the factors that produced it. That is why the best way to check a factorization is to multiply again: if you get the original polynomial, the factorization is right.

To factor a polynomial means to write it as a product. It is the opposite of multiplying. With numbers it is the same: 3 · 4 = 12 is a multiplication, and writing 12 as 3 · 4 is factoring it.

Always check

After you factor, multiply the factors. If you get the original polynomial, the answer is right. This check catches almost every mistake: the sign (3(x − 2) gives 3x − 6, not 3x + 6) and the incomplete factor (3(x + 6) gives 3x + 18, not 3x + 6).

2The common factor

The first step of any factoring is to look for a common factor in all the terms. Take out:

  • the greatest common factor (GCF) of the coefficients;
  • the variable with the smallest exponent that appears in every term.

Example 1: factor 6x + 9.

1GCF of 6 and 9. 3.

2Take out the 3. 6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3).

3Check. 3(2x + 3) = 6x + 9. ✓

✓Answer. 3(2x + 3).

Example 2: with a variable. Factor 4x² + 8x.

1GCF of 4 and 8 is 4; the common variable with the smallest exponent is x.

2Take out 4x. 4x² + 8x = 4x(x + 2).

3Check. 4x(x + 2) = 4x² + 8x. ✓

✓Answer. 4x(x + 2).

Example 3: factor 10x³ − 15x².

1GCF of 10 and 15 is 5; the smallest power of x is x².

2Take out 5x². 10x³ − 15x² = 5x²(2x − 3).

✓Answer. 5x²(2x − 3).

When a term leaves a 1

If the whole term is the common factor, a 1 stays in its place: 3x + 3 = 3(x + 1), not 3(x). And the factor must be the greatest one: 2(4x + 6) is not finished, because 4x + 6 still has a common factor 2.

3Difference of squares

A binomial of the form a² − b² (a square minus a square) is factored with the identity from Lesson 3.1, now backwards:

a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)

Example 4: factor x² − 9.

1Recognize it. x² is the square of x; 9 is the square of 3.

2Use the identity. x² − 9 = (x + 3)(x − 3).

3Check. (x + 3)(x − 3) = x² − 9. ✓

✓Answer. (x + 3)(x − 3).

Example 5: with a coefficient. Factor 4x² − 25.

1Recognize it. 4x² is the square of 2x; 25 is the square of 5.

2Use the identity. 4x² − 25 = (2x + 5)(2x − 5).

✓Answer. (2x + 5)(2x − 5).

Only differences, not sums

A difference of squares needs a minus sign. A sum of squares such as x² + 9 cannot be factored with real numbers. Both terms must also be perfect squares: x² − 10 is not a difference of squares with whole numbers.

4Both together: common factor, then difference of squares

The golden rule is to always take out the common factor first. Then check whether what is left inside the parentheses is a difference of squares.

Example 6: factor 2x² − 50 completely.

1Common factor 2. 2x² − 50 = 2(x² − 25).

2Inside there is a difference of squares. x² − 25 = (x + 5)(x − 5).

3The complete result. 2(x + 5)(x − 5).

✓Answer. 2(x + 5)(x − 5).

“Completely” means all the way

A factorization is complete when no factor can be factored any further. So after you take out the common factor, always check whether the binomial left is a difference of squares.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with your notebook open and no calculator: (1) The factor detective: before you factor each polynomial, decide whether it needs only a common factor, only a difference of squares, or both: 6x + 18, 4x² − 12x, 12x³ + 8x², x² − 49, 9x² − 16, 3x² − 27, 5x³ − 20x, and 2x² − 50. (2) I always check: expand each of the eight factorizations and check that you get back the original polynomial. If it does not match, find the step where you went wrong. (3) When it equals zero: factor x² − 25, x² − 81, and 4x² − 9, and find which values of x make each factor zero. You will use this idea in Topic 4 to solve equations. About 60 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Mental math with a difference of squares: 21 × 19 = (20 + 1)(20 − 1) = 400 − 1 = 399. Try 31 × 29, 52 × 48, and 99 × 101 in your head, then check them with a calculator. Show the trick to someone at home.

📘Key words

factor
To write a polynomial as a product.
greatest common factor (GCF)
The largest number (and power of x) that divides every term.
perfect square
A number or term that is the square of another: 49 = 7², 4x² = (2x)².
difference of squares
a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)
factored completely
When no factor can be factored any further.
