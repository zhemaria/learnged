The coordinate plane: ordered pairs and quadrants

›

Three ways to practice. Explore: mark points freely and see their coordinates. Place the point: you get the ordered pair and mark it on the plane. Read the coordinates: you get the point and write its ordered pair. When an answer is not right, the page tells you what went wrong: the coordinates swapped, a sign, or just one of the two.

With the keyboard: Tab takes you to the plane; the arrows move the cursor one unit (with Shift, five units); Enter or the space bar marks the point; Home takes the cursor back to the origin.

The order matters: (3, 2) is not the same point as (2, 3). The first coordinate is always the move across from the origin, and the second, the move up or down. A common mistake is to count up first.

The distance between two points

›
d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

The segment that joins the two points is always the hypotenuse of a right triangle whose legs are the horizontal difference (Δx) and the vertical difference (Δy). That is why there is no new formula to memorize: it is the same Pythagorean theorem. Practice on the plane, then measure real distances between cities in Illinois.

On this map grid, y is measured from the top edge down, the opposite of the coordinate plane. That does not change the calculation: the differences are squared, so the sign disappears. The scale is about 0.982 miles per unit, and the result is the straight-line distance, not the distance by road.

The midpoint of a segment

›
M = ( (x₁ + x₂) ÷ 2 , (y₁ + y₂) ÷ 2 )

The midpoint is not a formula to memorize: it is the average of the two endpoints. Add the two x's and divide by 2; do the same with the two y's. That is why the result always lands halfway. Read the endpoints on the graph (or check the box to see them), and find M. When you finish, M appears on the segment.

You can use a decimal point: if a sum is odd, the result ends in .5.

Two common slips: adding and forgetting to divide by 2 (the point lands twice as far), and subtracting instead of adding (the difference is for the distance, not for the midpoint).

Figures on the plane: the perimeter

›

When the vertices of a figure are points on the plane, measure each side and add them. Choose a figure, ask for new coordinates, and see how each side is measured.

Horizontal or vertical side (subtract)Slanted side (distance formula)
Watch the signs. From x = −3 to x = 5 there are 5 − (−3) = 8 units, not 5 − 3 = 2. And a slanted side is not Δx + Δy: square, add, and take the square root.
