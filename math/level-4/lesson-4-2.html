Math · Level 4 · Topic 4 — Quadratic equations

Lesson 4.2 · Completing the square

Complete the square to solve any quadratic equation: add (b/2)², write a binomial squared, and take the square root with ±.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks ✏️ No calculator

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain completing the square with the area model.
  • Solve quadratic equations by completing the square with five steps.
  • Divide by a first when the coefficient of x² is not 1, and find (b/2)², even as a fraction.
  • Follow how completing the square leads to the quadratic formula.

1The visual idea: completing a square

A link with Lesson 4.1. In Lesson 4.1 you solved quadratic equations by factoring. But some equations do not factor with whole numbers. Completing the square is a method that always works, whether the equation factors or not, and it is also where the quadratic formula comes from.

The expression x² + 6x can be drawn as a square with side x plus a rectangle of area 6x. If you cut that rectangle in half and place one half on each side of the square, only a small corner is missing to make a big square. That corner is (6/2)² = 9.

x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)²

Adding that missing term turns x² + 6x + 9 into the perfect square (x + 3)². That is the central idea: add just the right term to form a binomial squared.

2The five steps (when a = 1)

The five steps to solve a quadratic equation by completing the square
StepWhat to do
1Leave the terms with x on one side: x² + bx = −c.
2Add (b/2)² to both sides.
3Write the left side as (x + b/2)².
4Take the square root of both sides, with ±.
5Solve for x.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Example 1: solve x² + 6x − 7 = 0.

1x² + 6x = 7.

2(6/2)² = 9. Add 9 to both sides: x² + 6x + 9 = 16.

3(x + 3)² = 16.

4x + 3 = ±4.

5x = −3 + 4 = 1 or x = −3 − 4 = −7.

✓Solutions. x = 1 and x = −7.

Example 2: solve x² − 4x − 5 = 0.

1x² − 4x = 5.

2(−4/2)² = 4. Add 4: x² − 4x + 4 = 9.

3(x − 2)² = 9.

4x − 2 = ±3.

5x = 5 or x = −1.

✓Solutions. x = 5 and x = −1.

Do not forget the ± sign

When you take the square root of both sides, there are two results, the positive one and the negative one. Leaving out the ± loses one of the two solutions.

3When a ≠ 1: divide first

The five steps assume the coefficient of x² is 1. If it is not, the first move is to divide the whole equation by a.

Example 3: solve 2x² + 8x − 10 = 0.

1Divide everything by 2: x² + 4x − 5 = 0.

2x² + 4x = 5.

3(4/2)² = 4. Add 4: (x + 2)² = 9.

4x + 2 = ±3.

5x = 1 or x = −5.

✓Solutions. x = 1 and x = −5.

Divide the whole equation

When you divide by a, divide every term, including the constant. Then the coefficient of x² is 1 and the five steps work just as before.

Always positive

Even when b is negative, (b/2)² is positive: (−5)² = 25. When b is odd, the result is a fraction: (5/2)² = 25/4.

5The origin of the quadratic formula

If you complete the square in the general equation ax² + bx + c = 0 (no numbers, only letters), the result is exactly the quadratic formula:

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

That is why this method matters so much: it is not an isolated trick, it is the proof of where the formula of Lesson 4.3 comes from.

The derivation, step by step (the same steps as in section 3, with letters)

1Divide everything by a: x² + (b/a)x + c/a = 0.

2Move the constant to the other side: x² + (b/a)x = −c/a.

3Add half the coefficient of x, squared, to both sides: (b/2a)² = b²/4a². x² + (b/a)x + b²/4a² = b²/4a² − c/a.

4The left side is a perfect square; write the right side with a common denominator: (x + b/2a)² = (b² − 4ac)/4a².

5Take the square root, without forgetting ±: x + b/2a = ±√(b² − 4ac) / 2a.

6Subtract b/2a to get x alone: x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a.

✓Result: the quadratic formula, the same one on the GED formula sheet.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with your notebook open: (1) From the picture to the method: solve x² + 6x = 7 and x² + 4x = 12 by drawing the area model. Then use the five steps on x² + 8x + 7 = 0, x² − 10x + 16 = 0, x² + 2x − 8 = 0, x² − 6x − 3 = 0, 2x² + 8x − 10 = 0, and 3x² − 12x + 6 = 0. In each one, write down the term (b/2)² that completes the square. (2) The derivation: after you study the six steps of section 5, close the lesson and complete the square yourself in ax² + bx + c = 0 until you reach the quadratic formula. Compare your result with the GED formula sheet. (3) I do not lose solutions: in each solution below, the ± was forgotten. Find the missing solution and write both: (x + 2)² = 9 → x = 1; (x − 4)² = 25 → x = 9; (x + 1)² = 7 → x = −1 + √7. Then do several exercises of each activity on the practice page, solving them in your notebook first, and write down the ones you miss to review them. About 60 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Cut a square of paper and two equal strips, as in the area model. Show someone at home how the missing corner “completes” the square, and ask them to find the corner for x² + 10x.

📘Key words

completing the square
Adding (b/2)² to x² + bx to make a binomial squared.
perfect square trinomial
x² + bx + (b/2)² = (x + b/2)²
±
“Plus or minus”: a number and its opposite, the two square roots.
quadratic formula
x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
