1The graphing method

A link with Lessons 6.1 and 6.2. In Lessons 6.1 and 6.2 you solved systems with algebra (substitution and elimination). Now you add a third view: the graph. Seeing both lines on the plane helps you understand why a system has one, no, or infinitely many solutions.

To solve a system by graphing, graph both lines on the same plane. The point where they cross is the solution of the system: its pair (x, y) works in both equations.

On the graph, the lines y = x + 1 and y = −x + 5 cross at (2, 3). That is exactly the result substitution or elimination would give.

Graph to see, solve to be exact

The graph shows at a glance how many solutions there are; the algebra gives the exact point, especially when it is not a pair of whole numbers. To know the kind without graphing, write each equation as y = mx + b and compare the slopes and the intercepts.

3Translating a word problem into a system

The most important step in a word problem is to define the variables and write two equations from the text.

Example 1: an adult ticket costs $3 more than a child ticket. Two adults and three children pay $26. How much is each ticket?

1Let a be the adult price and c the child price: a = c + 3 and 2a + 3c = 26.

2Substitute: 2(c + 3) + 3c = 26.

3Solve: 5c + 6 = 26, c = 4. Then a = 7.

✓Answer. The child ticket costs $4 and the adult ticket, $7.

4Modeling: the break-even point

A business has costs (fixed plus variable) and revenue (from sales). The break-even point is where costs and revenue are equal: the business neither gains nor loses. It is the solution of the system formed by the cost line and the revenue line.

Example 2: costs are C = 100 + 5x and revenue is R = 10x (x = units sold). What is the break-even point?

1Set costs equal to revenue: 100 + 5x = 10x.

2Solve: 100 = 5x, x = 20.

✓Answer. The break-even point is 20 units. Below 20 there is a loss; above 20, a profit.

Read the break-even point

To the left of the point, costs are greater than revenue (loss); to the right, revenue is greater than costs (profit).

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with your notebook, graph paper, and the calculator: (1) The break-even point: a workshop has costs C = 200 + 8x and revenue R = 12x, where x is the number of units sold. Graph both lines, find the break-even point, and shade the loss zone and the profit zone. Do it again with C = 150 + 3x and R = 8x. (2) From the text to the system: define the variables, write the two equations, solve with the method you prefer, and interpret the answer. (a) The sum of two numbers is 30 and their difference is 6. (b) A concert sold 100 tickets, at $12 for adults and $5 for children, and took in $920. (3) Three views, one solution: solve the system y = 2x − 1 and x + y = 5 by graphing, by substitution, and by elimination. Check that all three give the same point and write which one was fastest for you and why. Then do several exercises of each activity on the practice page. About 70 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. Think of a small business you know (a food stand, a lawn service). Estimate its fixed costs per month, its cost per sale, and its price, and find how many sales it needs to break even.

📘Key words

graphing method
Graphing both lines and reading the point where they cross.
slope-intercept form
y = mx + b: m is the slope and b the y-intercept.
break-even point
Where costs equal revenue: no gain and no loss.
fixed cost
A cost that does not change with the number of units (rent, equipment).
