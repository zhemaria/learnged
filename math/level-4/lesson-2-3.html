1What is a polynomial?

A link with the first part of Topic 2. You measured volumes and surfaces with numbers. Now the same measures are written in general form with letters: the perimeter of a lot whose sides are (2x + 1) and (3x − 2) is a polynomial. And combining like terms such as 3x² + 5x² is the same idea of “putting together what is alike” that you used to add 3x + 5x in Level 3.

A polynomial is an expression made of terms added or subtracted. Each term has a coefficient (the number) and a variable part with whole-number exponents that are not negative (like x, x², x³).

  • The degree of a term is the exponent of its variable (in 5x³ the degree is 3; a number alone has degree 0).
  • The degree of the polynomial is the largest degree among its terms.
  • The leading coefficient is the number in the term of highest degree.
  • A polynomial is in standard form when its terms go from the highest degree to the lowest.

Example 1: name it and find its degree. Look at the polynomial 4x³ − 2x + 7.

1It has 3 terms. It is a trinomial.

2The largest exponent is 3. It has degree 3.

3The number in the degree-3 term is 4. The leading coefficient is 4.

✓Answer. A trinomial of degree 3 with leading coefficient 4.

The sign goes with the term

Each sign belongs to the term after it: in 3x − 5 the second term is −5, not 5. And when you see no number in front of x, the coefficient is 1: in x² it is 1 and in −x³ it is −1. Expressions with a negative exponent, a fractional exponent, or x in a denominator (like 5/x) are not polynomials.

2Adding and subtracting polynomials

To add or subtract polynomials, combine the like terms: the ones with the same variable and the same exponent. Only the coefficients are added or subtracted; the variable part stays the same.

Example 2: adding. Add (3x² + 2x − 5) + (x² − 4x + 1).

1The x² terms. 3x² + x² = 4x².

2The x terms. 2x + (−4x) = −2x.

3The numbers. −5 + 1 = −4.

✓Answer. 4x² − 2x − 4.

Example 3: subtracting. Subtract (5x² + 3x − 2) − (2x² − x + 4).

1The minus sign changes every sign of the second polynomial. 5x² + 3x − 2 − 2x² + x − 4.

2The x² terms. 5x² − 2x² = 3x².

3The x terms and the numbers. 3x + x = 4x; −2 − 4 = −6.

✓Answer. 3x² + 4x − 6.

The most common mistake in subtraction

Forgetting to change the sign of every term of the second polynomial. The minus sign reaches each term inside the parentheses, not only the first one. And when you add or subtract, the exponent is copied: 3x² + 5x² = 8x², not 8x⁴.

3A monomial times a polynomial

To multiply a monomial by a polynomial, use the distributive property: the monomial multiplies every term of the polynomial. When you multiply the variables, add the exponents (the product rule from Level 3): x · x² = x³.

a(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ad

Example 4: a monomial times a trinomial. Multiply 3x(2x² − 4x + 5).

13x · 2x² = 6x³ (numbers: 3 · 2 = 6; exponents: 1 + 2 = 3).

23x · (−4x) = −12x².

33x · 5 = 15x.

✓Answer. 6x³ − 12x² + 15x.

Example 5: with a negative sign. Multiply −2x²(x³ − 3x).

1−2x² · x³ = −2x⁵.

2−2x² · (−3x) = 6x³ (negative times negative is positive).

✓Answer. −2x⁵ + 6x³.

Two rules at once

When you distribute, watch the sign (negative times negative is positive) and add the exponents of the same variable. Multiplying the numbers and adding the exponents are two separate steps: do not mix them. And the monomial visits every term: 5(x + 2) = 5x + 10, not 5x + 2.

4Evaluating a polynomial

To evaluate a polynomial means to put a number in place of the variable and calculate the result. It is useful, for example, to find the actual value of a perimeter written with x once you know x.

Example 6: evaluate P = 2x² − 3x + 1 when x = 4.

1Put 4 in place of x. 2(4)² − 3(4) + 1.

2Powers, then products. 2(16) − 12 + 1 = 32 − 12 + 1.

3Add and subtract from left to right. 21.

✓Answer. P = 21.

Watch the order of operations

First the exponents, then the multiplications, and last the additions and subtractions. When you put in a negative value, write it in parentheses: (−3)² = 9, not −9.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. For your own work, with your notebook open: (1) Six polynomials named by their number of terms (monomial, binomial, trinomial) and by their degree. (2) Six additions and subtractions of polynomials. In the subtractions, share the minus sign with every term before you combine the like terms. (3) A triangle whose sides measure (2x + 1), (3x − 2), and (x + 4) feet. Write its perimeter as a simplified polynomial, then evaluate it for x = 4. (4) Six products: three of a monomial times a binomial and three of a monomial times a trinomial. Check the coefficient, the sign, and the sum of the exponents in each term. (5) Four polynomials evaluated at values of x that you choose, at least one of them negative. About 60 minutes; check your work with the checklist of this lesson.
  • In your daily life. A garden is x feet wide and (x + 6) feet long. Write its perimeter and its area as polynomials. Then measure (or imagine) a real garden or room and choose x: evaluate both polynomials and check them against your measures.

📘Key words

polynomial
Terms added or subtracted, with whole-number exponents that are not negative.
term
A number, a variable, or a product of them, like 5x³.
like terms
Terms with the same variable and the same exponent, like 3x² and −x².
degree
The largest exponent of the variable in the polynomial.
leading coefficient
The number in the term of highest degree.
