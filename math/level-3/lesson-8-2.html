Math · Level 3 · Topic 8 — Counting and probability

Lesson 8.2 · Compound probability: with and without replacement

Find the probability of two events in a row, with and without replacement, and write it as a fraction, a decimal, and a percent.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🎲 Chance and the tree diagram

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Find the probability of a simple event as favorable cases over possible cases.
  • Find the probability of two events with replacement (independent): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).
  • Find the probability without replacement (dependent): P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B after A).
  • Tell how replacement changes the probabilities.
  • Write the answer as a fraction, a decimal, and a percent.

1Review: the probability of one event

In Lesson 8.1 you learned to count the possible outcomes. Probability uses that counting: it compares the favorable cases with the possible cases. This lesson studies the probability of two or more events together, and the key difference between doing it with replacement and without replacement.

The probability of an event measures how likely it is to happen. It is the ratio of the favorable cases to the possible cases.

P(event) = favorable casespossible cases

Example 1: a bag has 4 red candies, 3 blue, and 2 green (9 in all). What is the probability of drawing a red one?

1Count. Favorable cases (red): 4. Possible cases (total): 9.

✓Answer. P(red) = 4/9.

2Two events with replacement

When the object is put back after the first event (with replacement), the second event is not affected: the events are independent. The probability that both happen is the product of their probabilities.

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

Example 2: with replacement. From the bag (4 red, 3 blue, 2 green; 9 in all), you draw a candy, put it back, and draw another. What is the probability of red and then blue?

1P(red) = 4/9. Since it goes back, the total is still 9.

2P(blue) = 3/9. Multiply: 49 × 39 = 1281.

✓Answer. P(red and then blue) = 1281, which simplifies to 427.

3Two events without replacement

When the object is not put back, the second event does change: there is one object fewer in all (and one fewer of the kind that came out). The events are dependent.

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B after A)

Example 3: without replacement. From a bag with 4 red and 3 blue candies (7 in all), you draw one and do not put it back; then you draw another. What is the probability of red and then blue?

1P(red) = 4/7.

2Without replacement, 6 candies are left, 3 of them blue. P(blue after) = 3/6.

3Multiply. 47 × 36 = 1242 = 27.

✓Answer. P(red and then blue) = 27.

The difference between probability with and without replacement
SituationKind of eventsThe denominator…
With replacement (it goes back)IndependentDoes not change
Without replacement (it stays out)DependentGoes down by 1

Adjust both numbers

Without replacement, on the second draw both the numerator (if an object of that kind came out) and the denominator (always down by 1) change. Forgetting to adjust the denominator is the most common mistake.

4Three ways to write the answer

A probability can be written as a fraction, a decimal, or a percent. The GED test often asks for one of the three, so it helps to know how to convert between them.

Example 4: write the probability 1/4 in the three forms.

1As a fraction. 1/4.

2As a decimal. 1 ÷ 4 = 0.25.

3As a percent. 0.25 × 100 = 25%.

✓Answer. 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

Every probability is between 0 and 1

A value of 0 means impossible, and 1 (or 100%) means certain. If a calculation gives a probability greater than 1, there is a mistake: you probably added when you needed to multiply.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Solve three probability problems with replacement and three without replacement, drawing the tree diagram for each one. Write every answer as a fraction, a decimal, and a percent. Then check two of them in Explore: from the tree to the table.
  • In your daily life. Put 5 coins of two kinds in a cup (for example, 3 pennies and 2 nickels). Draw two without looking, without putting the first back, and write down the result. Repeat 20 times. Compare how often you got two pennies with the probability 3/5 × 2/4.

📘Key words

probability
Favorable cases ÷ possible cases. It is always between 0 and 1.
with replacement
The object goes back before the next draw; the events are independent.
without replacement
The object stays out; the events are dependent.
independent events
Events in which one does not change the probability of the other.
tree diagram
A drawing with one branch for each outcome; multiply along a path.
