1Adding and subtracting like radicals

In Lesson 3.1 you learned to simplify radicals by taking out the largest perfect square. That step is now essential: to add or subtract radicals you look at their irrational part, and two radicals that do not look alike often turn out to be like radicals once they are simplified.

Radicals combine just like like terms in algebra. Just as 3x + 5x = 8x, also 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2: you add the coefficients and the irrational part stays the same.

3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2   (like 3x + 5x = 8x)

Example 1: add 4√5 + 6√5

1Same irrational part. Both terms have √5.

2Add the coefficients. 4 + 6 = 10.

✓Answer. 4√5 + 6√5 = 10√5

Example 2: subtract 7√3 − 2√3

1Same irrational part. √3.

2Subtract the coefficients. 7 − 2 = 5.

✓Answer. 7√3 − 2√3 = 5√3

Different parts do not combine

√2 + √3 is not √5, and it is not 2√5. They have different irrational parts, so they are not like radicals and the sum is left as it is: √2 + √3.

2Alike or not? Simplify first

Sometimes two radicals do not look alike, but once you simplify them they are. So before you decide, simplify each radical.

Example 3: add √8 + √2

1Simplify. √8 = √(4 · 2) = 2√2.

2Now they are alike. Both have √2: 2√2 + √2.

3Add the coefficients. √2 = 1√2, so 2 + 1 = 3.

✓Answer. √8 + √2 = 3√2

3Multiplying radicals

To multiply, use the product property again, now in the joining direction: the product of the roots is the root of the product. Simplify the result at the end.

√a · √b = √(a · b)

Example 4: multiply √3 · √5

1Join them under one radical. √(3 · 5) = √15.

2Simplify? 15 has no perfect squares: it is already simple.

✓Answer. √3 · √5 = √15

Example 5: multiply √6 · √2

1Join them. √(6 · 2) = √12.

2Simplify. √12 = √(4 · 3) = 2√3.

✓Answer. √6 · √2 = 2√3

Example 6: multiply √2 · (√2 + 5)

1Distribute. √2 · √2 + √2 · 5.

2Each product. √2 · √2 = √4 = 2, and √2 · 5 = 5√2.

✓Answer. √2 · (√2 + 5) = 2 + 5√2

A root times itself

√a · √a = √(a · a) = a. For example, √7 · √7 = 7. This is the idea that defines the square root, and it comes up often when you distribute.

4Approximating radicals

An irrational radical has no exact decimal value, but you can approximate it. First trap it between two whole numbers using perfect squares; then use the calculator. This helps you estimate results and check your work.

Example 7: between which whole numbers is √20?

1Perfect squares around it. 16 < 20 < 25.

2Take the roots. 4 < √20 < 5.

✓Answer. √20 is between 4 and 5. The calculator gives 4.472…, so √20 ≈ 4.5 to the nearest tenth.

Example 8: approximate 3√2 to two decimal places

1On the calculator. √2 ≈ 1.41421.

2Multiply by the coefficient. 3 × 1.41421 = 4.24264.

✓Answer. 3√2 ≈ 4.24

🧮 Approximating on the calculator

For 3√2: 3 × 2nd x2 2 enter shows 4.242640687. Round at the end, not before: if you round √2 to 1.41 first, you get 4.23, which is off in the last digit.

Estimate to check

If a sum of radicals gives, for example, 3√3, check that 3√3 ≈ 5.20 and compare it with the decimal value of the original expression. If the decimals match, the operation is right.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

📘Key words

like radicals
Radicals with the same irrational part, like 3√2 and 5√2. Only like radicals can be added or subtracted.
coefficient
The number in front of the radical. In 5√2, the coefficient is 5.
irrational part
The radical that is left after simplifying. In 5√2, it is √2.
approximate
To give a decimal value that is close to the exact value, like √2 ≈ 1.41.
