Adding and subtracting like radicals

›

Radicals combine just like like terms in algebra. Just as 3x + 5x = 8x, also 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2: you add the coefficients and the irrational part stays the same.

3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2   (like 3x + 5x = 8x)

Can they be combined?

Build two terms and see whether they combine. The line of decimals checks the result.

Then try different radicands, for example 2 and 3, or 8 and 2.

Different parts do not combine: √2 + √3 is not √5, and it is not 2√5. They have different irrational parts, so they are not like radicals and the sum is left as it is: √2 + √3.

Alike or not? Simplify first

›

Sometimes two radicals do not look alike, but once you simplify them they are. So before you decide, simplify each radical. Remember that √2 is the same as 1√2.

Multiplying radicals

›

To multiply, use the product property in the other direction: the product of the roots is the root of the product. The coefficients outside are multiplied together, and you simplify at the end.

√a · √b = √(a · b)

Join and simplify

A root times itself: √a · √a = √(a · a) = a. For example, √7 · √7 = 7. This idea comes up often when you distribute: √2 · (√2 + 5) = 2 + 5√2.

Approximating radicals

›

A radical that is not exact has no decimal that ends, but you can trap it between two numbers using perfect squares: since 16 < 20 < 25, √20 is between 4 and 5. Then you narrow it down to the tenth.

Trap the root

Practice: estimate

Write it yourself: approximate to the nearest tenth

Type a decimal with one digit after the point, for example 4.5. You can use the calculator to check.

Estimate to check: if a sum of radicals gives 3√3, you can check that 3√3 ≈ 5.20 and compare it with the decimal of the original expression. If they match, the operation is right.
