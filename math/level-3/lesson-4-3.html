Lesson 4.3 · Equations with fractions, decimals, and special cases

Clear fractions and decimals before you solve, and recognize equations with no solution or with infinitely many solutions.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🧮 Calculator to check

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Solve equations with fraction coefficients by clearing the fractions (multiply by the LCM).
  • Solve equations with decimal coefficients by multiplying by the right power of 10.
  • Identify an equation with no solution (a contradiction: 0 = 5).
  • Identify an equation with infinitely many solutions (an identity: 0 = 0).
  • Classify the result of an equation: one solution, no solution, or infinitely many.

1Clearing fractions: multiply by the LCM

In Lesson 4.2 you solved linear equations with the five-step strategy. This lesson adds two tools for harder cases: a way to “clean up” fractions and decimals before you solve, so the equation has only whole numbers, and how to recognize two special results that do not give a single value: no solution and infinitely many solutions.

An equation with fractions becomes much easier if you clear the fractions first. To do that, multiply every term on both sides by the least common multiple (LCM) of the denominators.

Example 1: solve x2 + 3 = 7

1Multiply every term by 2. The only denominator is 2: 2 · (x2) + 2 · 3 = 2 · 7.

2Simplify. x + 6 = 14.

3Subtract 6. x = 8.

✓Answer. x = 8

Example 2: solve x2 + x4 = 6

1The LCM of 2 and 4 is 4. 4 · (x2) + 4 · (x4) = 4 · 6.

2Simplify. 2x + x = 24, that is, 3x = 24.

3Divide by 3. x = 8.

✓Answer. x = 8

Multiply every term

The LCM must multiply every term, including the ones with no fraction (like the 3 in Example 1 or the 6 in Example 2). Forgetting a term is the most common mistake when you clear fractions.

2Clearing decimals: multiply by a power of 10

With decimal coefficients the idea is the same: multiply every term by 10, 100, or 1000, depending on the greatest number of decimal places, to turn the decimals into whole numbers.

Example 3: solve 0.2x + 1 = 3

1One decimal place. The most decimal places is one, so multiply every term by 10.

2Whole numbers. 2x + 10 = 30.

3Solve. Subtract 10 and divide by 2: 2x = 20, x = 10.

✓Answer. x = 10

Which power to use

One decimal place → × 10; two places (like 0.25) → × 100; three places → × 1000. Choose it from the term with the most decimal places, and multiply the whole equation by that same power.

3A special case: no solution

Sometimes, as you solve, the variables disappear and you are left with a false statement, like 5 = 9. That means no value of the variable can make the equation true: it is a contradiction, and the equation has no solution.

Example 4: solve 2x + 5 = 2x + 9

1Subtract 2x from both sides. 5 = 9.

2The variable is gone. 5 = 9 is false.

✓Answer. The equation has no solution (a contradiction).

4A special case: infinitely many solutions

In other cases, the variables disappear and you are left with a statement that is always true, like 0 = 0. That means every value of the variable works: it is an identity, and the equation has infinitely many solutions.

Example 5: solve 3(x + 2) = 3x + 6

1Distribute on the left. 3x + 6 = 3x + 6.

2Subtract 3x from both sides. 6 = 6, which is always true.

✓Answer. The equation has infinitely many solutions (an identity).

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.

📘Key words

least common multiple (LCM)
The smallest number that all the denominators divide into. The LCM of 2 and 4 is 4.
contradiction
An equation that leads to something false, like 5 = 9: it has no solution.
identity
An equation that leads to something always true, like 0 = 0: every value is a solution.
power of 10
10, 100, 1000…: multiplying by one of them moves the decimal point.
