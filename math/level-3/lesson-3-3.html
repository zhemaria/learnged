Lesson 3.3 · The real number system: rational, irrational, and the number line

Sort numbers into rational and irrational, place irrational numbers on the number line, and turn repeating decimals into fractions.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks ✏️ No calculator needed

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Classify numbers in the real number system (natural, integer, rational, irrational).
  • Recognize how the sets fit inside one another: natural ⊂ integers ⊂ rational ⊂ real.
  • Explain why √2 and π are irrational.
  • Place irrational numbers on the number line using approximations.
  • Convert a repeating decimal to a fraction to show that it is rational.

1The real number system

Lessons 3.1 and 3.2 worked with radicals like √2 and √3, which have no exact decimal value. These numbers belong to a special family: the irrational numbers. This lesson places the radicals in the full picture of the numbers, separating the rational from the irrational and showing how together they make up the real number system.

The real numbers are organized in sets that fit inside one another. The natural numbers (1, 2, 3, …) are inside the integers (…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …), which are inside the rational numbers (the ones you can write as a fraction). Apart from them are the irrational numbers. Together, rational and irrational numbers make up the real numbers.

How to read the diagram

Each number belongs to the smallest set that contains it and to every set around it. The number 3 is natural, but it is also an integer, rational, and real. On the other hand, √2 is irrational and real, but it is not rational or an integer.

2Rational numbers

A number is rational if you can write it as a fraction of two integers (with a denominator that is not zero). Its decimal form always ends (like 0.75) or repeats a pattern (like 0.333…).

Careful with roots

Not every root is irrational. √9 = 3 is rational, because 9 is a perfect square. Only the roots that do not come out exact, like √2 or √5, are irrational.

3Irrational numbers

A number is irrational if you cannot write it as a fraction of two integers. Its decimal form does not end and does not repeat any pattern. The best-known examples are √2, √3, √5, and the number π.

Example 1: why √2 is irrational

1On the calculator. √2 = 1.41421356…, and the digits go on without ending or repeating a pattern.

2If it were rational… it could be written as an exact fraction of integers. But no fraction squared gives exactly 2, and its decimal never closes a cycle.

✓Conclusion. √2 cannot be written as a fraction: it is irrational.

The number π

π ≈ 3.14159265… is the ratio of the circumference to the diameter of any circle. Its decimals go on forever without repeating, so π is irrational, just like radicals that do not come out exact. 22/7 and 3.14 are only approximations of π, and both are rational.

4Placing irrational numbers on the number line

Even though an irrational number has no exact decimal value, it has a precise place on the number line. To place it, use its decimal approximation and find which whole numbers it falls between.

Example 2: place √3

1Trap it. 1 < 3 < 4, so 1 < √3 < 2.

2Approximate. √3 ≈ 1.73, closer to 2 than to 1.

✓Answer. √3 is between 1 and 2 on the number line.

Comparing without a calculator

To compare a root with a whole number, compare their squares. Is √10 greater than 3? Since 10 > 9 = 32, yes: √10 > 3.

5Repeating decimals to fractions

A repeating decimal is rational, because you can always write it as a fraction. There is an algebra procedure to find that fraction.

Example 3: write 0.666… as a fraction

1Name it. x = 0.666…

2Multiply by 10. 10x = 6.666…

3Subtract. 10x − x = 6.666… − 0.666…, that is, 9x = 6.

4Solve. x = 6/9 = 2/3.

✓Answer. 0.666… = 2/3, a rational number.

How much to multiply by?

By 10 if one digit repeats; by 100 if two digits repeat. So 0.272727… gives 100x − x = 27, and x = 27/99 = 3/11. Being able to write the decimal as a fraction is the proof that it is rational. With √2 you never reach an exact fraction, and that is why it is irrational.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Classify four numbers in the real number system, place three irrational numbers between two whole numbers on the number line, and convert three repeating decimals to fractions. When you have mastered the lesson, test your speed with Irrational numbers against the clock: twenty questions to classify, place, approximate, convert, and compare real numbers, each with its explanation.

📘Key words

rational number
A number you can write as a fraction of two integers, like 3/4, 5, or 0.333…
irrational number
A number whose decimal does not end or repeat, like √2 or π.
real numbers
All the rational and irrational numbers together: every point on the number line.
repeating decimal
A decimal in which a group of digits repeats forever, like 0.272727…
