Math · Level 3 · Topic 8 — Counting and probability

Lesson 8.1 · The counting principle, permutations, and combinations

Count outcomes by multiplying, use the factorial, and tell when order matters (permutation) and when it does not (combination).

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🧮 Keys n!, nPr, and nCr

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Use the counting principle to find the total outcomes of events in a row.
  • Understand the factorial (n!) as the product of the whole numbers from 1 to n.
  • Find permutations, where order matters: P(n, r) = n! / (n − r)!.
  • Find combinations, where order does not matter: C(n, r) = n! / [r!(n − r)!].
  • Tell when a problem asks for a permutation and when it asks for a combination.

1The counting principle

Topic 7 summarized and interpreted data. Topic 8 opens combinatorics: the art of counting how many ways things can happen. Counting the possible outcomes is the direct basis of probability, which you will study in the next lesson. It is also the first step in closing Level 3.

If one event can happen in m ways and another in n ways, then the two together can happen in m × n ways. The idea extends to more events: multiply the choices for each one.

Total outcomes = (choices for the 1st) × (choices for the 2nd) × …

Example 1: a menu has 2 main dishes and 3 drinks. How many dish-and-drink pairs are there?

1Multiply the choices. 2 × 3 = 6.

✓Answer. There are 6 possible pairs.

2The factorial

The factorial of a number, written n!, is the product of all the whole numbers from 1 to n. It appears in the formulas for permutations and combinations.

n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 2 × 1

Example 2: find 5!.

1Multiply the whole numbers from 5 down to 1. 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120.

✓Answer. 5! = 120.

The special case 0!

By agreement, 0! = 1. That makes the formulas work correctly.

3Permutations: order matters

A permutation counts how many ways items can be put in order. Use it when the order matters (a podium, a code, the order of arrival).

P(n, r) = n!(n − r)!

Example 3: in how many ways can you arrange 3 books out of 5 on a shelf?

1Here n = 5 and r = 3, and the order matters. P(5, 3) = 5!(5 − 3)! = 5!2!.

2Calculate. 120 ÷ 2 = 60. (Or count the places: 5 × 4 × 3 = 60.)

✓Answer. There are 60 ways to arrange them.

4Combinations: order does not matter

A combination counts how many ways you can choose a group, when the order does not matter (a committee, a hand of cards, a group of friends). That is why its formula also divides by r!, which removes the repeated orders.

C(n, r) = n!r! (n − r)!

Example 4: in how many ways can you choose 3 books out of 5 to take on a trip (order does not matter)?

1n = 5, r = 3. C(5, 3) = 5!3! (5 − 3)! = 1206 × 2.

2Calculate. 120 ÷ 12 = 10.

✓Answer. There are 10 ways to choose them.

How to tell a permutation from a combination
Does order matter?TypeExample
YesPermutationA podium (1st, 2nd, 3rd), a code
NoCombinationA committee, a group of friends

The key question

Before you calculate, ask yourself: “Does the result change if I change the order?” If yes → permutation. If no → combination. Mixing them up is the most common mistake in this topic.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Solve three counting-principle problems (menus, outfits, codes), three permutations, and three combinations. For each one, write first whether order matters, then calculate by hand and check with the calculator's nPr and nCr keys.

📘Key words

counting principle
If one event can happen in m ways and another in n ways, together they can happen in m × n ways.
factorial
n! = n × (n − 1) × … × 1. By agreement, 0! = 1.
permutation
An arrangement in which order matters: P(n, r).
combination
A choice of a group in which order does not matter: C(n, r).
