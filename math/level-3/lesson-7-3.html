Lesson 7.3 · Histograms: make, read, and compare

Make a histogram from a frequency table, describe the shape of a distribution, and choose between a histogram and a box plot.

1What is a histogram?

The box plot in Lesson 7.2 summarizes data with five numbers. The histogram gives another view: it shows how many data values fall in each interval, which reveals the whole shape of the distribution. Together, the two graphs give a richer picture of the same data set.

A histogram groups the data into intervals and draws one bar for each, whose height is the frequency (how many data values fall in that interval). Unlike a bar graph, its bars touch, because they show continuous data.

A histogram is not a bar graph

In a bar graph, the columns stand for separate categories and have spaces between them. In a histogram, the bars stand for intervals of continuous data and touch each other. That is the key difference when you need to tell them apart.

2Making a histogram

Start from a frequency table: each row gives an interval and how many data values fall in it. The height of each bar is that frequency.

Example 1: a table shows that the interval 10–15 has a frequency of 8. How tall is its bar?

1The height of the bar equals the frequency of the interval.

✓Answer. The bar for 10–15 reaches a height of 8.

Intervals of the same width

So that the histogram is easy to read, all the intervals should have the same width (for example, every 5 years). Then comparing the heights is the same as comparing the frequencies. Each interval includes its left end but not its right end: 10–15 includes 10 but not 15.

3The shape of a distribution

The great advantage of a histogram is that it shows the shape of the data at a glance. The shape is described with precise words:

Shapes of a distribution and what they look like
ShapeWhat it looks like
SymmetricThe same shape on both sides of the center.
Skewed rightA long tail toward the high values.
Skewed leftA long tail toward the low values.
UnimodalOne peak (one bar that is highest).
BimodalTwo separate peaks.
UniformAll the bars are about the same height; there is no clear peak.

Example 2: a histogram rises to a peak in the center and falls about the same way on both sides. What shape does it have?

1It has one peak. So it is unimodal.

2The two sides are alike. So it is about symmetric.

✓Answer. It is a unimodal, about symmetric distribution.

Skew is named by the tail

Skew is named by the tail, not by the peak: “skewed right” has its peak on the left and a long tail toward the high values.

4Histogram versus box plot

The two graphs describe the same data from different angles. It helps to know when to use each one.

What each graph shows better
QuestionBetter display
What is the detailed shape of the data?Histogram
What are the quartiles and the outliers?Box plot
Are there one or two peaks?Histogram
How spread out is the middle half?Box plot

5Choosing the most useful display

There is no “best” graph in general: it depends on the question. To see the shape and the peaks, use a histogram; to compare spread and find outliers between groups, use box plots.

Example 3: choosing by the question. You want to know whether the ages in a class split into two very different age groups. Which graph should you use?

1The question is about shape. Are there one or two peaks?

2Compare the graphs. The histogram shows the peaks directly; the box plot does not.

✓Answer. Use the histogram, because it shows whether the distribution is bimodal.

And for categories?

When the data are separate categories (countries, colors, days of the week), neither one fits: use a bar graph.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Take a set of 20 data values (for example, the ages of 20 people). Make a frequency table with intervals of the same width, draw the histogram, and describe its shape in one sentence. Then find the five key values, draw the box plot of the same data, and write what each graph shows that the other does not.
  • In your daily life. Look for a histogram in a newspaper, a website, or a report from your job (for example, ages of customers or waiting times). What shape does it have? What question does it answer well? Would a box plot answer it better or worse?

📘Key words

histogram
A graph of intervals of continuous data, with bars that touch; each height is a frequency.
frequency
How many data values fall in an interval.
symmetric
A distribution with the same shape on both sides of its center.
skewed
A distribution with a long tail on one side; it is named by the side of the tail.
bimodal
A distribution with two peaks.
