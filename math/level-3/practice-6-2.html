The right triangle and the theorem

›

In a right triangle, the two sides that form the 90° angle are the legs (a and b). The side across from the right angle, the longest one, is the hypotenuse (c).

a² + b² = c²

The proof with squares

Draw a square on each side. The theorem says that the areas of the two small squares together are exactly the area of the big square. Change the legs and check it.

The hypotenuse is always c: it is the longest side, and it is across from the right angle. Finding it is the first step, because in the formula it always takes the place that is alone.

3

Finding a leg

›

If you know the hypotenuse and one leg, solve for the other: subtract the square of the known leg from the square of the hypotenuse, and take the square root.

b² = c² − a²

Step by step

For a leg, subtract: the most common mistake is to add the two squares. If your answer is longer than the hypotenuse, something is wrong: no leg can be longer than the hypotenuse.

Practice: find the missing leg

If the result is not exact, round to the nearest tenth (for example, 7.8).

Pythagorean triples

›

Some right triangles have three whole-number sides. Recognizing them saves calculations on the test, and their multiples work too: if 3-4-5 is a triple, so are 6-8-10 and 9-12-15.

5

The converse of the theorem

›

The converse goes the other way: if the three sides make a² + b² = c² true, the triangle is a right triangle. It is a way to check whether a corner is a right angle. The longest side always plays the part of c, even if it is not listed last.

Practice: is it a right triangle?

On the job: carpenters mark 3 feet on one wall and 4 on the other. If the diagonal measures 5, the corner is square. That is the converse of the theorem.
