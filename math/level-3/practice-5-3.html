The four-step strategy

›

To solve for a variable is to rewrite the formula so that this letter is alone on one side. The key: treat the other letters as if they were numbers. That is why solving a formula is the same as solving an equation you already know how to solve.

1Identify the variable you want to solve for.
2Isolate it with inverse operations, treating the other letters as numbers.
3Simplify the result, with a fraction if needed.
4Check that the variable you want is alone on one side.

Numbers and letters, side by side

On the left, an equation with numbers; on the right, the formula. The steps are the same.

Common formulas

›

These formulas come up often on the test. In each one you can solve for any of its letters.

Formulas used often
SituationFormulaWhat the letters mean
Distanced = rtd: distance, r: rate (speed), t: time
Area of a rectangleA = bhb: base, h: height
Volume of a boxV = lwhl: length, w: width, h: height
Simple interestI = PrtP: principal, r: rate, t: time in years
Perimeter of a triangleP = a + b + ca, b, c: the three sides
ForceF = mam: mass, a: acceleration
If the letter is multiplied by others, divide by all of them: in I = Prt, P = I/(rt). If it is added, subtract the others: in P = a + b + c, c = P − a − b.

3

Two-step formulas

›

Some formulas need more than one operation. Undo them in reverse order, just like two-step equations: first remove what is added or subtracted, and last what multiplies.

Step by step

The parentheses matter: C = (5/9)(F − 32) is not the same as C = (5/9)F − 32. First subtract 32 from the whole F, then multiply the whole result.

4

Using the solved formula

›

There are two ways to find a letter: solve first and substitute afterward, or substitute first and solve the equation that is left. Both give the same result. Solving first is better when you have to calculate many times, because the solved formula works for every case.
