Lesson 5.2 · Percent change: increase and decrease

How much did it change? Percent increase and decrease, always compared with the original value, and the change factor.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🧮 Calculator to check

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Estimate the size of a change with benchmarks like 10% and 50%.
  • Find the percent change, dividing by the original value.
  • Tell an increase from a decrease by the sign.
  • Tell absolute change from relative change.
  • Use the change factor (1 + r) or (1 − r) to find the new value.

1Estimate before you calculate

In Lesson 5.1 you solved the three cases of percent. This lesson uses case 3 (finding the percent) for a very common question: how much did a quantity change? It is the tool behind sentences like “the price went up 12%” or “the population went down 3%.”

A good habit is to estimate first whether the change is large or small compared with the starting value. That way you spot silly answers right away.

Example: a quick estimateA price goes up from $40 to $52.

1The change. The increase is $12.

2Use 10%. 10% of 40 is 4, and 12 is three times 4.

✓Estimate. About 30%.

Handy benchmarks

To find 10%, move the decimal point one place to the left. 50% is half. An increase of 100% means the amount doubled.

2The percent-change formula: increase and decrease

% change = new value − original valueoriginal valuenew value − original value/original value × 100

The top is the absolute change (how much it went up or down). The bottom is always the original value, because it is the reference point. If the answer is positive, it was an increase; if it is negative, it was a decrease.

The most common mistake

Dividing by the new value. If a stock goes down from $80 to $60, the change is −20, and you divide by 80 (the original): −25%. Dividing by 60 would give 33.3%, which is wrong.

Example: a price increaseA product cost $40 and now costs $52. What was the percent increase?

1Change. 52 − 40 = 12.

2Divide by the original. 12 ÷ 40 = 0.30.

✓Times 100. 30% increase. It matches the estimate.

Example: a raiseA wage goes from $15.00 to $16.20 an hour. What percent raise did the worker get?

1Change. 16.20 − 15.00 = 1.20.

✓Divide and × 100. 1.20 ÷ 15.00 = 0.08 → 8% raise.

Example: temperatureThe temperature went down from 80 °F to 68 °F. What was the percent decrease?

1Change. 68 − 80 = −12 (negative: a decrease).

✓Divide and × 100. −12 ÷ 80 = −0.15 → 15% decrease.

Example: populationA town had 2,500 people and now has 2,300. By what percent did the population go down?

1Change. 2,300 − 2,500 = −200.

✓Divide and × 100. −200 ÷ 2,500 = −0.08 → 8% decrease.

3Absolute change and relative change

Is a $5 increase always the same percent? No. The absolute change tells how many units something changed; the relative change (the percent change) tells how big that change is compared with the starting point.

The same $5 increase on different original values
ItemOriginalNewAbsolute changePercent change
Lunch$10$15$550%
Shoes$50$55$510%
Bicycle$200$205$52.5%

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

That is why, to compare changes in quantities of different sizes, you use the percent.

Up and down by the same percent is not a round trip

If a $100 price goes up 10%, it becomes $110. If it then goes down 10%, the discount is figured on $110: 110 − 11 = $99. Each change is figured on the value at that moment.

4From the percent to the new value: the change factor

Sometimes you know the percent and want the new value. You can find the change and add it or subtract it, or you can multiply right away by a change factor:

The change factor for an increase and for a decrease
FormulaExample
Increase of r%new = original × (1 + r)12% increase → × 1.12
Decrease of r%new = original × (1 − r)15% decrease → × 0.85

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Example: rentA $1,250 rent goes up 4%. What is the new rent?

1Factor. 1 + 0.04 = 1.04.

2Multiply. 1,250 × 1.04 = 1,300.

✓Answer. $1,300. The change factor will be the main tool of Lesson 5.3.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

📘Key words

percent change
(new − original) ÷ original × 100.
absolute change
How many units something changed: new − original.
relative change
The change compared with the original value, as a percent.
change factor
The number you multiply by: 1 + r for an increase, 1 − r for a decrease.
