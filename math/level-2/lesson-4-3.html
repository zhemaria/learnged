1The key question: “If X doubles, what happens to Y?”

In Lesson 4.2 you worked with direct proportions: if one quantity grows, the other grows in the same ratio. But not every situation works that way. If you hire more painters, the building gets painted in fewer days. This lesson presents inverse proportions and teaches you to decide, before you calculate, which kind each problem is.

Before you set up any proportion, ask one question: if one quantity doubles, what happens to the other?

Going down is not enough

For a proportion to be inverse, when one quantity doubles the other must become exactly half; when one triples, the other must become one third. If you spend money and your balance goes down, that is not an inverse proportion: it is a subtraction.

2The constant k = x × y

In an inverse proportion, the product of the two quantities is always the same number. That number is the constant k, and it usually stands for the “total work.”

The product is always 48: the job takes 48 “worker-days” of work, however you split them.

The idea that ties it together

In a direct proportion the division (Y ÷ X) stays the same; in an inverse proportion the multiplication (X × Y) stays the same. Knowing what stays the same is the key to all of Topic 4.

3Solving inverse proportions

There are two ways to solve an inverse proportion, and they give the same answer. The first uses the constant: find k with the complete data and divide it by the new value. The second is the inverse rule of three: multiply straight across (not crosswise) and divide by the remaining value.

Example: workers and timeFour workers take 9 days to finish a job. How many days would 6 workers take, working at the same pace?

1Ask the question. More workers, fewer days: it is inverse.

2The constant. k = 4 × 9 = 36 worker-days.

3With 6 workers. 6 × x = 36 → x = 36 ÷ 6 = 6.

✓Answer. 6 days

Example: speed and timeA bus covers a route at 60 mph in 5 hours. How long would it take at 75 mph?

1Inverse. Faster, less time. The constant is the distance: 60 × 5 = 300 miles.

2At 75 mph. 75 × x = 300 → x = 300 ÷ 75 = 4.

✓Answer. 4 hours

Example: suppliesThe supplies at a camp last 12 people for 10 days. If 3 more people arrive, how many days will the supplies last?

1Inverse. More people, fewer days. k = 12 × 10 = 120 rations.

2Now 15 people. x = 120 ÷ 15 = 8.

✓Answer. 8 days

Example from science: pressure and volumeAt a constant temperature, a gas takes up 6 liters at a pressure of 2 atmospheres. What volume does it take up at 3 atmospheres?

1Inverse. More pressure squeezes the gas. k = 2 × 6 = 12.

2At 3 atmospheres. x = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.

✓Answer. 4 liters. In science this is called Boyle's law.

The typical mistake

Using the cross products of a direct proportion on an inverse problem. In the workers example it would give 4/9 = 6/x, so x = 13.5 days. That answer says that more workers take longer, which makes no sense. Common sense is the best check.

4Mixed applications

On the GED, problems do not tell you what kind they are. For any proportion problem: (1) ask the question “if X doubles, what happens to Y?”; (2) choose the method (constant ratio or constant product); (3) solve; (4) check that the answer makes sense.

Example: one context, two questionsIn a factory, 5 identical machines make 400 parts in 8 hours.

aSame 5 machines, 12 hours? More hours, more parts: direct. 4008400/8 = x12x/12 → x = 400 × 12 ÷ 8 = 600 parts.

b8 machines, the same 400 parts? More machines, less time: inverse. k = 5 × 8 = 40 machine-hours → x = 40 ÷ 8 = 5 hours.

✓Moral. The context does not decide the type; the pair of quantities you compare does.

📘Key words

inverse proportion
When one quantity doubles, the other is cut in half; the product x × y stays the same.
constant k
In an inverse proportion, the fixed product x × y (the “total work”).
inverse rule of three
Multiply straight across and divide by the remaining value.
worker-days
A unit of total work: workers × days.
