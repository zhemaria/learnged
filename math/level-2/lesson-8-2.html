1What a circle graph is

This lesson brings together two threads of Level 2. From Topic 5 comes the idea of a percent as a ratio out of 100; from Topic 7, the circle. A circle graph joins them: the whole circle stands for 100% of the data, and each slice is a part proportional to its percent. It is one of the most common ways to show data in newspapers, reports, and presentations.

A circle graph (also called a pie chart) is a circle divided into slices, or sectors. The size of each sector matches the percent of its category. The most important rule: all the percents must add up to 100%, because the whole circle is the total.

At a glance you see that food is the largest category, followed by rent. Health and free time are the smallest and are tied. That instant visual reading is the great advantage of a circle graph: you can compare categories without reading a list of numbers.

When a circle graph is a good choice

It is ideal to show how a whole is divided: how a budget is spent, what percent of people live in each neighborhood, how a day is used. It is not the best choice to show change over time (that calls for a line graph) or for many very small categories (the slices become hard to read).

2The central angle of each sector

To make a circle graph you change each percent into a central angle: the angle of the sector measured at the center of the circle. The whole circle is 360° and stands for 100%, so the rule is:

angle = 360° × (percent ÷ 100)

A direct proportion between percent and angle, like the ones in Topic 4.

Example: from percent to angleHow many degrees does a 25% sector take up?

1Formula. 360° × (25 ÷ 100) = 360° × 0.25.

2Multiply. 360° × 0.25 = 90°.

✓Answer. The 25% sector has a central angle of 90°: exactly one fourth of the circle.

Handy shortcuts

100% = 360° · 50% = 180° (half the circle) · 25% = 90° (a fourth) · 10% = 36° · 1% = 3.6°. Recognizing them quickly lets you check that a sector has a reasonable size.

3Percent, angle, and amount

The three forms of a sector are connected. If you know any one of them and the real total, you can find the other two.

Example: from percent to amountIn the budget, “Health” is 10%. How many dollars is that, if the total is $2,000?

1The rule from Topic 5. amount = total × (percent ÷ 100).

2Multiply. 2,000 × 0.10 = $200.

✓Answer. “Health” is $200 of the monthly budget.

Example: from angle to percentA sector has a central angle of 72°. What percent does it stand for?

1Solve the formula. percent = (angle ÷ 360°) × 100.

2Calculate. (72 ÷ 360) × 100 = 0.2 × 100 = 20.

✓Answer. The sector stands for 20% of the total.

Watch the units

Angles go in degrees (°), percents in %, and amounts in the unit of the problem ($, people, hours). Mixing them up is a common mistake. Before you write the answer, ask yourself: “What unit should this be in?”

4How to make a circle graph

Making a circle graph by hand, with a compass and a protractor, helps you understand the geometry behind it. The steps are always the same:

Steps to make a circle graph
StepWhat to do
1Check that the percents add up to 100%. If they do not, fix that first. (If you have counts, first divide each one by the total.)
2Find the central angle of each category: 360° × (percent ÷ 100).
3Draw a circle with the compass. Mark the center and draw a radius straight up as the starting line.
4Measure the first angle with the protractor from the starting radius and draw the second radius.
5Keep going: measure each new angle from the last radius, not from the first one.
6Color and label each sector with its category and percent.
7Check: the last radius must land on the first one. If it does not, a measurement is off.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A visual tip

Ordering the categories from largest to smallest before you draw usually gives a cleaner graph. The smallest sectors end up together and are easier to label from outside, with guide lines if needed.

5Reading real graphs

Reading a circle graph is the most common skill, on the GED test and in daily life. It means finding the largest and the smallest category, comparing two sectors, and sometimes finding the actual amount of one of them.

Example: how a day is spentThe graph shows how a worker spends a 24-hour day. Which is the largest category, and how many hours is it?

1Check the sum. 33 + 38 + 8 + 13 + 8 = 100%. ✓

2The largest. Work, with 38%.

3In hours. 24 × 0.38 = 9.12 hours, about 9 hours and 7 minutes.

✓Reading. The worker spends about 9 hours a day at work, more than on any other activity.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Change five percents into central angles and three angles into percents. Build the full table (percent, angle, amount) for a budget of your choice and check the Total row. Then make that circle graph by hand with a compass and a protractor.
  • In your daily life. Look for a circle graph in a newspaper, a website, or a bill (for example, where your tax dollars go). Write down the largest and the smallest category and find the actual amount of one sector.

📘Key words

circle graph
A circle divided into sectors that show the parts of a whole.
sector
A slice of the circle, between two radii.
central angle
The angle of a sector measured at the center: 360° × (percent ÷ 100).
percentage point
The difference between two percents: 35% − 30% = 5 points.
