Lesson 8.1 · Mean, median, mode, range, and weighted mean

Summing up a set of data in one number: the mean, the median, the mode, and the range; why outliers mislead the mean; and the weighted mean of a grade report.

1The four measures at a glance

Welcome to Topic 8, the last topic of Level 2. Until now you worked with single numbers: an operation, a distance, a percent. Statistics is different: it works with sets of data. The typical question is no longer “how much is this?” but “how does this group of numbers behave?” To answer it, you sum up the group in one or two representative values.

Each of these four measures sums up a set of data in one number, but from a different point of view. Knowing when to use each one is as important as knowing how to find it.

2The mean

The mean, also called the average, is the best-known measure. Add all the values and divide by how many values there are.

mean = (sum of the values) ÷ (number of values)

Example: test scoresA student had these five scores: 78, 85, 90, 72, 95. What was the average?

1Add. 78 + 85 + 90 + 72 + 95 = 420.

2Divide by how many. 420 ÷ 5 = 84.

✓Answer. The average is 84.

The idea behind it

The mean “shares out” all the values equally. If the 420 points were split evenly, each score would be exactly 84. That is why the mean is so useful to represent a group with a single number.

🧮 The mean on the calculator

Type ( 78 + 85 + 90 + 72 + 95 ) ÷ 5 enter. The parentheses make the calculator add first. Without them it would divide only the 95 by 5.

3The median: the middle value

The median is the value in the middle position when the data is in order from least to greatest. It splits the set into two equal halves: half the data is below it and half is above it.

Case 1: an odd number of values

The median is the value in the middle.

6572788595 → median = 78

Case 2: an even number of values

There are two values in the middle. The median is the mean of those two.

607075809095 → median = (75 + 80) ÷ 2 = 77.5

A step you must not skip

Put the data in order before you look for the median. Forgetting this is the most common mistake: the value in the middle of an unordered list is not the median.

Example: an unordered listFind the median of 12, 8, 15, 7, 10, 9, 14.

1Put them in order. 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15.

2Find the middle. There are 7 values (odd). The middle one is the 4th: 10.

✓Answer. The median is 10.

4The mode and the range

The mode

The mode is the value that appears most often. A set can have one mode, two modes (when two values tie for the most), or no mode (when every value appears the same number of times).

479991215 → mode = 9 (it appears three times)

The range

The range measures how spread out the data is: the difference between the greatest and the least value.

range = greatest − least

In 4, 7, 9, 9, 9, 12, 15: range = 15 − 4 = 11.

When to use each one

The mean sums up numbers with no extreme values. The median is better when there are outliers (next section). The mode works even with data that is not numbers (favorite colors, best-selling sizes). The range gives a quick idea of the spread.

5When the mean misleads: outliers

The mean has one important weakness: values that are very different from the rest, called outliers, pull it strongly. A single extreme value can drag the average far from what really represents the group.

Example: the “average” salaryIn a small company, five employees earn these monthly salaries: $3,000, $3,200, $3,100, $2,900, $3,300. A sixth employee, the director, earns $30,000. Compare the mean and the median.

1Sum. 3,000 + 3,200 + 3,100 + 2,900 + 3,300 + 30,000 = 45,500.

2Mean. 45,500 ÷ 6 ≈ 7,583. It sounds like an excellent salary for the company…

3In order. 2,900, 3,000, 3,100, 3,200, 3,300, 30,000.

4Median. (3,100 + 3,200) ÷ 2 = 3,150. That is what most of them really earn.

✓Conclusion. The director's salary is an outlier that inflates the mean. The median represents the typical salary much better.

A lesson for the news

When a headline says “the average salary went up X%”, ask whether it is the mean or the median. The difference between the two often tells the real story behind the data.

6The weighted mean

Sometimes not all the values are equally important. In grades, for example, the final exam counts more than a quiz. Then you find the weighted mean: multiply each value by its “weight” before you average.

weighted mean = Σ(value × weight) ÷ Σ(weights)

The symbol Σ (sigma) means “sum”. Σ(value × weight) is the sum of all the products; Σ(weights) is the sum of the weights.

Weighted mean of a grade report
AssessmentScoreWeightScore × weight
Quiz85201,700
Midterm exam78302,340
Homework92201,840
Final exam88302,640
Totals—1008,520

Example: the final gradeA course counts the quiz 20%, the midterm 30%, homework 20%, and the final exam 30%. A student got 85, 78, 92, and 88. What is the final grade?

1Sum of the products. 1,700 + 2,340 + 1,840 + 2,640 = 8,520.

2Sum of the weights. 20 + 30 + 20 + 30 = 100.

3Divide. 8,520 ÷ 100 = 85.2.

✓Answer. The final grade is 85.2.

A useful comparison

The simple mean of the four scores would be (85 + 78 + 92 + 88) ÷ 4 = 85.75. The weighted mean, 85.2, is a little lower because the midterm (78), the lowest score, weighs more than the homework (92), the highest. The weights change the result and make it fairer to the importance of each part.

7Homework

📘Key words

mean (average)
The sum of the values divided by how many there are.
median
The middle value of the data in order.
mode
The value that appears most often.
outlier
A value very different from the rest of the data.
