Lesson 7.1 · Area of composite figures

Divide and conquer: the area of L shapes, cut corners, and house fronts by adding or subtracting simple pieces, and the cost of materials.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🧮 Calculator to check

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Recall the area formulas for rectangles, triangles, and trapezoids.
  • Split a composite figure into simple figures with helper lines.
  • Use the addition strategy: add the areas of the pieces.
  • Use the subtraction strategy: subtract a missing piece from a bigger rectangle.
  • Find the cost of a material (paint, carpet) from the area and a unit price.

1Review of the basic area formulas

Welcome to Topic 7. So far Level 2 has worked with numbers and comparisons: integers, fractions, PEMDAS, ratios, percents, and money math. Now the setting changes: you start measuring space. This first lesson opens the topic with a key skill: finding the area of figures that are not simple rectangles or triangles, but combinations of several basic shapes.

Area is always measured in square units: square inches (in²), square feet (ft²), square yards (yd²).

Area formulas for the basic figures
FigureFormulaIn words
RectangleA = b × hbase times height
SquareA = s²side squared
ParallelogramA = b × hbase times height (the height, not the slanted side)
TriangleA = (b × h) ÷ 2base times height, divided by two
TrapezoidA = ((B + b) × h) ÷ 2sum of the bases times the height, divided by two

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

One idea behind all of them

All these formulas share one structure: base times height, adjusted by a factor (1 for the rectangle, ½ for the triangle, the average of the bases for the trapezoid). Thinking this way helps you remember them without memorizing.

2What a composite figure is

A composite figure is made by putting together two or more simple figures. They are everywhere: an L-shaped apartment, a yard with one corner cut off, the front of a house (a rectangle with a triangle on top).

Any of these can be measured with one simple idea: divide and conquer. Draw one or more helper lines that split the figure into simple pieces whose area you know how to find; then add them, or in some cases subtract.

Addition and subtraction strategies

3The addition strategy

With the addition strategy, draw a helper line that splits the figure into two (or more) simple pieces. Find each area on its own and add the results.

Example: an L shapeAn L-shaped room has the measurements shown in the plan. What is its total area?

1Helper line. Draw a horizontal line 10 ft below the top edge. The figure splits into two rectangles.

2R1 (top). 18 × 10 = 180 ft².

3R2 (bottom). 12 × 10 = 120 ft².

4Add. 180 + 120 = 300 ft².

✓Answer. The room has an area of 300 square feet.

Visual strategy

Label each piece with a letter or a different color (R1, R2, T1…) and write its area next to it. At the end, adding them up is a mechanical step.

4The subtraction strategy

Sometimes a composite figure looks more like a big rectangle with a piece missing. The subtraction strategy: find the area of the whole rectangle, then subtract the area of the missing piece.

Example: a yard with a corner cut offA 25 ft × 18 ft rectangular yard has one corner cut off (a rectangle 8 ft × 6 ft). What is the usable area?

1The whole rectangle. 25 × 18 = 450 ft².

2The missing piece. 8 × 6 = 48 ft².

3Subtract. 450 − 48 = 402 ft².

✓Answer. The usable area is 402 square feet.

Watch out

Choosing addition or subtraction is a matter of convenience. The same figure can be solved both ways, and the result must be the same. If one way is clumsy, try the other.

Example: one figure, two waysCheck that the L shape from the addition example (300 ft²) also comes out by subtraction.

1The whole rectangle. 18 × 20 = 360 ft² (the outside measurements).

2The missing corner. 6 × 10 = 60 ft².

3Subtract. 360 − 60 = 300 ft².

✓Answer. 300 ft², the same as with addition. ✓

6Real uses: the cost of materials

Once you have the area, you often multiply it by a unit price to find the total cost of a material: paint per square foot, carpet per square foot, tile per square foot.

Example: carpeting the LThe L-shaped room (300 ft²) will be carpeted at $4 per square foot. What will it cost?

1Area. 300 ft².

2Cost. 300 × $4 = $1,200.

✓Answer. Carpeting the room costs $1,200.

Example: painting the front of a houseThe front of a house is a 30 ft × 20 ft rectangle with a triangle on top (base 30 ft, height 12 ft). One gallon of paint covers 350 ft². How many gallons do you need?

1Rectangle. 30 × 20 = 600 ft².

2Triangle. (30 × 12) ÷ 2 = 180 ft².

3Total. 600 + 180 = 780 ft².

4Gallons. 780 ÷ 350 ≈ 2.23. You cannot buy part of a gallon, so round up: 3 gallons.

✓Answer. You need 3 gallons of paint.

🧮 Area and cost on the calculator

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Solve four L- or T-shaped figures by addition and three figures with cut corners by subtraction. Then solve three cost problems (paint, carpet, or tile) and two reverse problems: given the total area and one side, find the other side.
  • In your daily life. Measure a room in your home (or use a floor plan). Sketch it, split it into rectangles, find its area in square feet, and estimate what it would cost to carpet it at $4 per square foot.

📘Key words

composite figure
A figure made of two or more simple figures.
helper line
A line you draw to split a figure into simple pieces.
square unit
The unit of area: in², ft², yd².
unit price
The price of each square unit of a material.
