1A percent is a ratio out of 100

Welcome to Topic 5. In Topic 4 you learned to compare quantities with ratios and to predict them with proportions. This topic is about one very special kind of ratio: the one whose second term is always 100. These ratios are called percents, and they show up in grades, discounts, taxes, statistics, and interest.

Something to think about

On a test with 25 questions, you got 18 right. What percent is that? You will solve it in section 4, in two different ways.

The word percent comes from the Latin per centum, “for every hundred.” A percent is a ratio whose second term is always 100.

25% = 2510025/100 = 0.25

The three forms show the same thing: 25 parts out of every 100. Only the notation changes.

2Converting among percents, decimals, and fractions

Any percent can be written as a decimal or as a fraction, and the other way around.

Rules for converting among the three forms
ConversionHowExample
Percent → decimalDivide by 100: move the decimal point two places to the left.45% = 0.45
Decimal → percentMultiply by 100: move the decimal point two places to the right.0.07 = 7%
Percent → fractionWrite it over 100 and simplify.40% = 40/100 = 2/5
Fraction → percentDivide the top by the bottom and multiply by 100.3/4 = 0.75 = 75%

More than 100% and less than 1%

The rules do not change. 150% = 1.5 (one and a half times the whole), and 0.5% = 0.005 (half of one part out of every hundred). A percent greater than 100% means an amount greater than the whole.

Common mistake

Mixing up 0.5 and 5%. They are different: 0.5 = 50%, but 5% = 0.05. The two-places rule for the decimal point prevents this mistake.

3One formula: the percent triangle

Every percent problem connects three things: the part, the whole (the total you compare to), and the percent. One formula joins them:

Part = Percent × Whole

Inside the formula, the percent is always written as a decimal (15% → 0.15). The triangle helps you remember which operation to use: cover the piece you are missing and read what is left. Pieces side by side are multiplied; one above the other, divided.

the part% × Wholemultiply

4The three basic cases

Depending on which of the three pieces is missing, there are three cases. The key question before you calculate is always: what am I missing?

Case 1 · Find the partWhat is 15% of $80?

1What is missing? The part. You know the percent (15% = 0.15) and the whole ($80).

2Multiply. Part = 0.15 × 80 = 12.

✓Answer. $12

Case 2 · Find the whole30% of a number is 45. What is the number?

1What is missing? The whole. You know the part (45) and the percent (30% = 0.30).

2Divide. Whole = Part ÷ % = 45 ÷ 0.30 = 150.

✓Answer. 150. Check: 0.30 × 150 = 45. ✓

Case 3 · Find the percent: the test from the startYou got 18 of 25 questions right. What percent is that?

1What is missing? The percent. Part = 18, whole = 25.

2Divide. 18 ÷ 25 = 0.72.

3Change to a percent. 0.72 × 100 = 72.

✓Answer. 72%

Which number is the whole?

It is the reference amount, the one that comes after the word of: “15% of $80,” “18 of 25.” Mixing up the part and the whole is the most common mistake in case 3.

5Another method: the percent proportion

Because a percent is a ratio out of 100, you can set up any percent problem as the direct proportion you studied in Lesson 4.2:

partwholepart/whole = %100%/100

Put in the three values you know, put x where the missing one goes, and solve with cross products.

Example: the three cases with a proportion

1Part. 15% of 80 → x80x/80 = 1510015/100 → 100x = 1,200 → x = 12.

2Whole. 45 is 30% → 45x45/x = 3010030/100 → 30x = 4,500 → x = 150.

3Percent. 18 of 25 → 182518/25 = x100x/100 → 25x = 1,800 → x = 72%.

Which method is better?

Both give the same answer. The formula is faster with a calculator; the proportion helps when you do not know which operation to use, because you only have to place the data correctly. Learn both, and use one to check the other.
