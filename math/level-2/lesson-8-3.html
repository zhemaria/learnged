1What simple probability is

Welcome to the last lesson of Level 2. Here you meet probability, which measures how likely an event is to happen, on a universal scale from 0 to 1. You will learn to find a simple probability, to write it in three equivalent ways (fraction, decimal, and percent), and to tell impossible, possible, and certain events apart.

The probability of an event is a number that tells how likely it is to happen. You find it with a simple ratio:

P(A) = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes

The numerator counts the outcomes that match what you are looking for; the denominator counts all the possible outcomes. The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment is the sample space. Listing it correctly is the first step of any probability problem.

Examples of sample spaces
ExperimentSample spaceOutcomes
Toss a coin{heads, tails}2
Roll a number cube (die){1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}6
Draw a marble from a bag with 3 red and 2 blue5 marbles5

Example: rolling a dieYou roll a six-sided die. What is the probability of getting an even number?

1Sample space. {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}: 6 total outcomes.

2Favorable outcomes. The even numbers {2, 4, 6}: 3.

3Formula. P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2.

✓Answer. The probability is 1/2, or 0.5, or 50%. The three forms are the same.

2Three ways to write a probability

A probability is a ratio, so you can write it in the three ways from Topic 5: fraction, decimal, and percent.

P(heads) = 121 over 2 = 0.5 = 50%

The three numbers say the same thing. Choose the form that fits: the fraction is handy for calculating, the decimal goes straight into the calculator, and the percent is easy to understand in conversation.

The probability scale

Every probability is a number from 0 to 1, including 0 and 1. The scale works in any situation, from rolling a die to forecasting the weather.

Impossible, possible, and certain events
EventProbabilityExample with a die
ImpossibleP = 0rolling a 7
Possible0 < P < 1rolling an even number
CertainP = 1rolling a number from 1 to 6

Watch out

A probability can never be negative or greater than 1. If a calculation gives a number outside 0 to 1, there is a mistake. It is a quick check worth doing every time.

3Typical probability problems

Example: a number greater than 4You roll a die. What is the probability of getting a number greater than 4?

1Total outcomes. 6.

2Favorable outcomes. Numbers greater than 4: {5, 6}, that is, 2.

3P. 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333 ≈ 33.3%.

✓Answer. The probability is 1/3, about 33%.

Example: a bag of marblesA bag has 5 red marbles, 3 blue, and 2 green. You pick one without looking. What is the probability that it is blue?

1Total outcomes. 5 + 3 + 2 = 10 marbles.

2Favorable outcomes. 3 blue.

3P. 3/10 = 0.3 = 30%.

✓Answer. The probability is 3/10, or 30%.

Example: a small deck of cardsA small deck has 20 cards, 5 of each suit (hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades). You draw one at random. What is the probability of a heart?

1Total outcomes. 20 cards.

2Favorable outcomes. 5 hearts.

3P. 5/20 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%.

✓Answer. The probability is 1/4, or 25%.

For 3/10: 3 ÷ 10 enter gives 0.3. Then × 100 enter gives 30, that is, 30%.

4Experiment and theory

The probability you find with the formula, favorable ÷ total, is the theoretical probability. If you actually do the experiment many times and record the results, you get the experimental probability (also called the relative frequency).

experimental probability = times it happened ÷ total tries

Example: tossing a coin 20 timesYou toss a coin 20 times and get 12 heads and 8 tails. Compare the experimental probability of heads with the theoretical one.

1Experimental. 12 ÷ 20 = 0.6 = 60%.

2Theoretical. 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%.

✓Comparison. A difference of 10 percentage points, which is normal with a small number of tries.

The law of large numbers (simple version)

The more times you repeat an experiment, the closer the experimental probability gets to the theoretical one. With 20 tosses, being 10 points off is normal; with 10,000 tosses, the share of heads will be very close to 50%.

  • Paper and pencil. Find eight simple probabilities (dice, coins, marbles, and cards) and write each one as a fraction, a decimal, and a percent. Classify six events as impossible, possible, or certain.
