1Decimals are already in your life

You use decimals every day, even if you do not call them that. A product costs $3.99. Gas is $2.75 a gallon. A recipe asks for 1.5 pounds of meat. All of these are decimal numbers.

A decimal is a way to write parts of a whole. The decimal point separates the whole part (on the left) from the part that is less than one (on the right). In $3.99, the 3 is 3 whole dollars, and the .99 is 99 cents: a part of a dollar.

Connection to Topic 1

In Lesson 1.1 you learned the place value chart: ones, tens, hundreds… Decimals are the same chart, continued to the right. Each place to the right of the point is worth 10 times less than the place before it.

📖 How to read 4.625

1Step 1. Read the whole part: "four."

2Step 2. Read the decimal point as "and."

3Step 3. Read the decimal part as a whole number, 625, and then say the name of the last place. The 5 is in the thousandths.

✓Result. "Four and six hundred twenty-five thousandths."

Good to know

In everyday talk, people also say "four point six two five." Both ways are correct. With money, we say dollars and cents: $3.99 is "three dollars and ninety-nine cents." Remember from Lesson 1.1: we do not use "and" when we read whole numbers. The word "and" is saved for the decimal point.

Watch out!

The last place gives the name. 0.7 is "seven tenths." 0.07 is "seven hundredths." 0.007 is "seven thousandths." It is the same digit 7, but its place changes its value, just like with whole numbers.

3Compare and order decimals

Comparing decimals is a lot like comparing whole numbers, with one important rule: compare place by place, from left to right, starting with the whole part.

The trick: add zeros on the right

When two decimals have a different number of digits after the point, you can add zeros on the right without changing the value. 0.5 is the same as 0.50 and 0.500. Then both numbers have the same "length," and they are easy to compare.

Example: compare 0.6 and 0.58

1Step 1. Make them the same length: write 0.6 as 0.60.

2Step 2. Compare 0.60 and 0.58 place by place. Tenths: 6 and 5.

3Step 3. 6 is greater than 5. That decides it.

✓Result. 0.6 > 0.58.

Watch out! The most common mistake

Many people think 0.58 is greater than 0.6 "because it has more digits." That is not true. With decimals, more digits do not mean a greater value. That is why it helps to add zeros and make the numbers the same length before you compare.

4Round decimals

To round decimals, use the same "neighbor on the right" rule you learned in Lesson 1.1. The difference is that now you work to the right of the point.

  • Look at the digit to the right of the place you are rounding to.
  • If it is 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4, the digit stays the same.
  • If it is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9, the digit goes up by one.
  • The digits to the right of that place are dropped. They do not turn into zeros like with whole numbers: they just go away.

Example: round 5.348 to the nearest tenth

1Step 1. The place is the tenths: the digit 3.

2Step 2. Look at its neighbor on the right (the hundredths): it is 4.

3Step 3. 4 is less than 5, so the tenths digit stays 3. Drop the digits after it.

✓Result. 5.3.

Example: round 5.348 to the nearest hundredth

1Step 1. The place is the hundredths: the digit 4.

2Step 2. Look at its neighbor on the right (the thousandths): it is 8.

3Step 3. 8 is 5 or more, so the hundredths digit goes up from 4 to 5.

✓Result. 5.35.

In real life: round to the cent

Rounding to the hundredth is rounding "to the cent." A cash register does this when a calculation gives $4.617: you pay $4.62.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The number line. Go back to the number line in the practice activity and place ten different decimals. Seeing the point on the line is what convinces you that 0.8 is greater than 0.75, even though 75 is greater than 8.
  • In your daily life. Look at three real prices from a store or a receipt. Write them in words, order them from least to greatest, and round each one to the nearest dollar.

📘Key words

decimal
A number with a decimal point. It shows a whole part and a part that is less than one. Example: 3.99.
decimal point
The dot that separates the whole part (left) from the decimal part (right). We read it as "and."
tenth, hundredth, thousandth
The first three places to the right of the point. 0.1 is one tenth, 0.01 is one hundredth, 0.001 is one thousandth.
greater than (>), less than (<)
Symbols to compare two numbers. The open side faces the greater number: 0.6 > 0.58.
round to the nearest…
To change a number to a simpler one close to it, keeping only up to a certain place. 5.348 rounded to the nearest tenth is 5.3.
