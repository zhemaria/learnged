Math · Level 1 · Topic 1 — Whole numbers

Lesson 1.3 · Adding and subtracting in word problems

Read a problem in words, decide whether to add or subtract, solve it, and check that the answer makes sense.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🎯 Calculator only to check

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Decide whether a word problem needs addition or subtraction.
  • Solve word problems with the 4-step method, including problems with information you do not need.
  • Solve problems that need two operations, one after the other.
  • Check that your answer makes sense in the real situation.

1From the calculation to the real problem

In Lesson 1.2 you learned to add and subtract in columns. But in real life, numbers do not come lined up in columns. They come inside a situation: a bill, a trip, a paycheck, a store's stock.

Today the challenge is not the calculation (you already know how to do it). The challenge is to decide what to calculate. The most important question for a word problem is: "Do I add or subtract?"

Key idea

We add when we put amounts together or something increases. We subtract when we take something away, find how much is left, or compare two amounts to find the difference.

2Keywords

Some words in a problem are clues that point to the operation. Here is your reference table:

Words in a problem that point to addition or to subtraction
➕ Words that point to adding➖ Words that point to subtracting

Watch out!

Keywords help, but they are a guide, not a rule. The safest thing is to picture the situation: are the amounts coming together, or is something being taken away?

Look at this problem: "Carlos has $45. He has $12 more than his sister. How much money does his sister have?" It says "more," but the sister has less than Carlos. So we subtract: 45 − 12 = $33.

3The 4-step method

Use these four steps for any word problem:

The 4 steps

1Read and underline. Read the whole problem. Underline the numbers and what the question asks.

2Choose the operation. Are amounts coming together, or is something taken away? Look for the keywords.

3Solve. Write the operation in columns and work it out.

4Does it make sense? Check your answer and its unit. Is it reasonable for the situation?

Example with the 4 steps: an addition

"A family had $1,850 in savings. This month they saved $475 more. How much do they have in savings now?"

1Read and underline: the numbers are $1,850 and $475. The question is how much they have now.

2Choose: "saved more" and "how much now" tell us the amounts come together. It is an addition.

3Solve: 1,850 + 475 = 2,325.

4Does it make sense? Yes. They saved more, so the total ($2,325) must be bigger than what they had ($1,850). The answer is $2,325.

Another example: a subtraction

"A car has 92,480 miles on it. One year ago, it had 87,315 miles. How many miles did it travel in one year?"

1Read and underline: the numbers are 92,480 and 87,315. The question is how many miles it traveled (the difference).

2Choose: comparing two amounts to find the difference is a subtraction.

3Solve: 92,480 − 87,315 = 5,165.

4Does it make sense? Yes. Driving 5,165 miles in one year is reasonable. The answer is 5,165 miles.

"Maria works 8 hours a day. This week she earned $620, and last week she earned $580. How much did she earn in the two weeks?" Which information do you not need?

Tip

Some problems include information you do not need. That is why Step 1 is to underline. If a number does not help answer the question, leave it out.

5Problems with two operations

Some problems need two operations, one after the other. Solve them in parts, in order: first what happened first.

Example: two steps

"A repair shop had 350 parts. It received 180 new parts and then used 215 parts on a repair. How many parts does it have now?"

1It received parts (add): 350 + 180 = 530 parts.

2It used parts (subtract): 530 − 215 = 315 parts.

✓Result: the shop has 315 parts now.

Watch out!

In a two-step problem, the answer to the first operation is used in the second one. Solve one part at a time and write down the answer to each part.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open. Use the examples and the self-check checklist to review your work and decide what to practice next.

  • ThatQuiz. Go to ThatQuiz and practice adding and subtracting numbers with several digits until it feels easy. Then, when you read a word problem, you can put all your attention on the situation, not on the calculation.
  • Write your own problem. Write an addition or subtraction word problem with real numbers from your life: expenses, distances, hours of work. Wait one day. The next day, solve it with the 4 steps, as if someone else wrote it. This shows you if the problem was written clearly.
  • In your daily life. Think about a big purchase you made. Write a two-step problem: how much you had, how much you spent, how much was left. Solve it with the 4 steps, and at the end ask yourself: does the answer make sense?

📘Key words

word problem
A math problem told with words, about a real situation.
keyword
A word in a problem that gives a clue about the operation, like "in all" (add) or "left" (subtract).
difference
The result of a subtraction. It tells how much bigger one amount is than another.
information you do not need
A number or fact in a problem that does not help answer the question.
two-step problem
A problem that needs two operations, one after the other. The answer to the first is used in the second.
reasonable
An answer that makes sense for the real situation.
