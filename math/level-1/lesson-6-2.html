1The metric system: everything in tens

Most countries, and all of science and medicine, use the metric system. You will see it on medicine labels (mg, mL), soda bottles (2 liters), and race distances (5 km).

The metric system has one base unit for each kind of measure, and it works with tens, like our place value:

The base units of the metric system and what each prefix means
MeasureBase unitkilo- (× 1,000)centi- (÷ 100)milli- (÷ 1,000)
Lengthmeter (m)kilometer (km)centimeter (cm)millimeter (mm)
Mass (weight)gram (g)kilogram (kg)—milligram (mg)
Capacityliter (L)——milliliter (mL)

The key idea of this lesson

The prefix tells you the size. Kilo always means 1,000, centi means one hundredth, and milli means one thousandth. So converting metric units is just multiplying or dividing by 10, 100, or 1,000: moving the decimal point, as you did in Topic 3.

To get a feel for the sizes: a meter is a little longer than a yard, a kilogram is a little more than 2 pounds, and a liter is a little more than a quart.

2Convert metric units

The metric equivalences you will use most
LengthMassCapacity
1 km = 1,000 m1 kg = 1,000 g1 L = 1,000 mL
1 m = 100 cm1 g = 1,000 mg
1 cm = 10 mm

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The rule is the same as in Lesson 6.1: to a smaller unit, multiply; to a bigger unit, divide. With powers of ten, that means moving the decimal point one place for each zero.

Example: 2.5 km = ___ m

1Smaller or bigger? Meters are smaller than kilometers: multiply by 1,000.

2Move the point 3 places to the right (1,000 has 3 zeros): 2.5 → 2,500.

✓Answer. 2.5 km = 2,500 m.

Example: 350 cm = ___ m

1Smaller or bigger? Meters are bigger than centimeters: divide by 100.

2Move the point 2 places to the left (100 has 2 zeros): 350. → 3.50.

✓Answer. 350 cm = 3.5 m.

Watch out! Do not mix the two systems

Inside the metric system everything is tens. Between the metric system and the U.S. system it is not: 1 inch is 2.54 cm, not 10. If a GED® problem asks you to change between systems, it will give you the equivalence.

3Time and elapsed time

Units of time and their equivalences

hour (h)1 h = 60 min

week1 week = 7 days
year1 year = 12 months = 52 weeks = 365 days

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Watch out! Time does not work with tens

An hour has 60 minutes, not 100. So 1.5 hours is 1 hour and 30 minutes (half of 60), not 1 hour and 50 minutes. And you cannot subtract times like decimals.

Elapsed time is how much time passes between two moments. The safest way to find it is to count up in jumps: first to the next full hour, then by whole hours, then the minutes that are left.

Example: a shift starts at 9:45 a.m. and ends at 1:20 p.m. How long is it?

1To the next full hour: 9:45 → 10:00 is 15 minutes.

2Whole hours: 10:00 → 1:00 is 3 hours.

3The minutes left: 1:00 → 1:20 is 20 minutes.

✓Add the jumps. 3 h + 15 min + 20 min = 3 h 35 min.

4Temperature

In the United States, the weather and ovens use degrees Fahrenheit (°F). Science and most countries use degrees Celsius (°C). These reference points help you picture a temperature:

To find how much the temperature changed, subtract the smaller temperature from the bigger one, as with any difference.

Example: at 6 a.m. it was 38°F. At 3 p.m. it was 61°F. How much did the temperature rise?

1Find the difference. 61 − 38 = 23.

✓Answer with the unit. The temperature rose 23 degrees (23°F).

Reading a thermometer

Before you read a thermometer, find out how much each small mark is worth. If the numbers go 40, 50, 60 and there are 5 spaces between them, each mark is 10 ÷ 5 = 2 degrees.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Find three metric measures at home (a soda bottle, a medicine, a food label). Convert each one to a smaller or a bigger metric unit. Then write down the start and end times of three activities in your day and find how long each one lasted.
  • In your daily life. Check the weather forecast for this week. Write the high and the low temperature for each day, and find how much the temperature changes each day.

📘Key words

metric system
A system of units based on tens: meter, gram, liter.
prefix
The first part of a unit's name that tells its size: kilo- (1,000), centi- (one hundredth), milli- (one thousandth).
mass
How much matter something has. In daily life we call it weight; we measure it in grams and kilograms.
elapsed time
The time that passes between a start time and an end time.
degree
The unit of temperature: °F (Fahrenheit) or °C (Celsius).
