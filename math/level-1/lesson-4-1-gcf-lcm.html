Lesson 4.1 complement · Factors, multiples, GCF, and LCM

Two small tools that make a big difference: the greatest common factor to simplify, and the least common multiple to add fractions.

⏱️ About 2 hours with practice and homework · take breaks 🧩 Gets you ready for fractions

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell the factors of a number apart from its multiples.
  • Recognize prime numbers and write a number as a product of primes.
  • Find the greatest common factor (GCF) of two numbers in two ways.
  • Find the least common multiple (LCM) of two numbers and use it as a common denominator.
  • Decide whether a word problem asks for the GCF or the LCM.

Why this lesson?

In Lesson 4.1 you simplified fractions by looking for "a number that divides both." When that number is the greatest possible, it has a name: the greatest common factor. And in Lesson 4.2, to add fractions with different denominators, you need a common denominator: the easiest one is the least common multiple.

This lesson explains both ideas from the beginning. The GED® test also asks about them directly ("What is the greatest common factor of 24 and 36?") and inside problems about sharing things and about schedules.

1Factors and multiples

A factor (or divisor) of a number is a number that divides it evenly, with no remainder. 3 is a factor of 12 because 12 ÷ 3 = 4, with no remainder. 5 is not a factor of 12, because 12 ÷ 5 leaves a remainder of 2.

The best way to find factors is in pairs: look for the multiplications that give the number.

Example: all the factors of 12

1Step 1. Try the multiplications that give 12, starting with 1: 1 × 12, 2 × 6, 3 × 4.

2Step 2. The next number, 4, already showed up in the pair 3 × 4. When the pairs start to repeat, the list is complete.

✓Result. The factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12.

A multiple of a number is what you get when you multiply it by 1, 2, 3, 4… The multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24… and the list never ends.

Do not mix them up

A number has few factors, and they are never greater than the number (the factors of 12 end at 12). A number has endless multiples, and they are never smaller than the number (the multiples of 12 start at 12). Factors: you divide. Multiples: you multiply.

These rules save you divisions when you look for factors:

Divisibility rules for 2, 3, 5, and 10
A number can be divided by…if…Example
2it ends in 0, 2, 4, 6, or 8 (it is even).38 yes; 27 no.
3the sum of its digits can be divided by 3.51: 5 + 1 = 6, yes.
5it ends in 0 or 5.45 yes; 52 no.
10it ends in 0.70 yes; 75 no.

2Prime numbers and prime factorization

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. The first primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, and 29. A number with more than two factors is called composite (12 is composite: it has six factors).

Two special cases

1 is not prime: it has only one factor. And 2 is the only even prime: every other even number can be divided by 2.

Every composite number can be written as a multiplication of primes. This is called its prime factorization, and it is like the number's "fingerprint": each number has only one.

Example: the prime factorization of 60

1Step 1. Divide by the smallest prime that works: 60 ÷ 2 = 30.

2Step 2. Keep going with the answer: 30 ÷ 2 = 15. 15 is not even, so try 3: 15 ÷ 3 = 5.

3Step 3. 5 is prime, so you are done.

✓Result. 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5, which you can also write as 2² × 3 × 5.

Always check

Multiply the primes you found: 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 60. If you do not get the number you started with, one of the divisions has a mistake.

3The greatest common factor (GCF)

The greatest common factor (GCF) of two numbers is the greatest factor they share. You can find it in two ways.

Method 1 · Compare the lists of factors

Example: the GCF of 12 and 18

1Step 1. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12.

2Step 2. Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.

3Step 3. Factors in both lists: 1, 2, 3, and 6.

✓Result. The greatest of them is 6. GCF(12, 18) = 6.

Method 2 · Use the prime factorization

With big numbers, the lists get long. With the prime factorization, you take only the primes they share, each one with its smaller exponent.

Example: the GCF of 24 and 36

1Step 1. 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 2³ × 3.

2Step 2. 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3².

3Step 3. Shared primes: 2 (smaller exponent: 2²) and 3 (smaller exponent: 3¹).

✓Result. GCF(24, 36) = 2² × 3 = 4 × 3 = 12.

What it is for: simplify in one step

If you divide the numerator and the denominator by their GCF, the fraction is in simplest form right away. To simplify 182418/24: the GCF of 18 and 24 is 6. 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 24 ÷ 6 = 4. The answer is 343/4.

4The least common multiple (LCM)

The least common multiple (LCM) of two numbers is the smallest multiple they share (not counting zero).

Method 1 · Compare the lists of multiples

Example: the LCM of 4 and 6

1Step 1. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24…

2Step 2. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24…

✓Result. The first number in both lists is 12. LCM(4, 6) = 12.

Method 2 · Use the prime factorization

Take every prime that shows up, shared or not, each one with its greater exponent.

Example: the LCM of 12 and 18

1Step 1. 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3².

2Step 2. Primes that show up: 2 (greater exponent: 2²) and 3 (greater exponent: 3²).

✓Result. LCM(12, 18) = 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36.

🧮 A shortcut with the calculator

If you already know the GCF: LCM = (first number × second number) ÷ GCF. For 12 and 18, type (12×18)÷6enter: the screen shows 36.

What it is for: the common denominator

To add 141/4 + 161/6, the LCM of 4 and 6 is 12, the smallest common denominator: 3123/12 + 2122/12 = 5125/12. A greater denominator (like 24) also works, but then you have to simplify at the end.

5GCF or LCM? How to decide in a problem

In word problems, the question does not say "GCF" or "LCM." You have to recognize what it asks for:

How to tell whether a word problem asks for the GCF or the LCM
It asks for the…when the problem is about…Words that give it away
GCFsharing or cutting into equal groups, as large as possible, with nothing left over."the greatest number of equal groups," "the longest pieces," "with nothing left over."
LCMthings that repeat, and when they happen together again."when will they happen together again?", "at the same time," "every so often."

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Example: a sharing problem (GCF)

"A food pantry has 24 cans of beans and 36 cans of corn. It wants to make identical bags, with the same number of each kind of can and no cans left over. What is the greatest number of bags it can make?"

1Step 1. You share into equal groups, as large as possible: the problem asks for the GCF.

2Step 2. GCF(24, 36) = 12.

✓Result. 12 bags, each with 24 ÷ 12 = 2 cans of beans and 36 ÷ 12 = 3 cans of corn.

Example: a schedule problem (LCM)

"One bus stops at the corner every 15 minutes, and another every 20 minutes. At 8:00 they both stop together. When will they stop together again?"

1Step 1. Things repeat, and you want to know when they meet again: the problem asks for the LCM.

2Step 2. Multiples of 15: 15, 30, 45, 60. Multiples of 20: 20, 40, 60. LCM(15, 20) = 60.

✓Result. They meet again 60 minutes later, at 9:00.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Paper and pencil. Write the prime factorization of 48, 72, 90, and 100, and check each one by multiplying. Then find the GCF and the LCM of 48 and 72, and of 90 and 100, with both methods. Both methods must give the same answer.
  • Simplify in one step. Simplify 24/36, 45/60, and 56/84 by dividing by the GCF in one step.
  • In your daily life. Think of two things that repeat every so often (a medicine every 8 hours and a meal every 6 hours, two work shifts, two bus routes). Find how often they happen together, and write it in a sentence.

📘Key words

factor (divisor)
A number that divides another number with no remainder. 3 is a factor of 12.
multiple
What you get when you multiply a number by 1, 2, 3… The multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12…
prime number
A number with exactly two factors, 1 and itself: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11…
composite number
A number with more than two factors, like 12.
prime factorization
A number written as a multiplication of primes: 60 = 2² × 3 × 5.
greatest common factor (GCF)
The greatest factor two numbers share. GCF(12, 18) = 6.
least common multiple (LCM)
The smallest multiple two numbers share. LCM(4, 6) = 12.
