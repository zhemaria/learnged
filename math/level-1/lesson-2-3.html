Lesson 2.3 · Multiplication and division in word problems

Decide when to multiply and when to divide in real problems, and use the properties to calculate quickly.

1Closing Topic 2: putting it all together

In Lesson 2.1 you learned to multiply, and in Lesson 2.2 you learned to divide. Just like in Topic 1 with addition and subtraction, the challenge now is not the calculation. The challenge is to decide what to calculate when the problem comes in words.

Today's key question is: "Do I multiply or divide?"

Key idea

We multiply when we have several equal groups and want the total. We divide when we have a total and want to split it into equal groups, or find how many groups fit.

2Keywords: multiply or divide?

As in Topic 1, some words in a problem are clues. Here is your reference table for the two new operations:

Words in a problem that point to multiplication or to division
✖️ Words that point to multiplying➗ Words that point to dividing
each … how many in all?split equally, share equally
the total of … equal groupshow much for each person? for each group?
times, twice, double, triplehow many groups?
per (with the number of groups given): $15 per hour for 8 hourshalf, one third, divided by

Watch out!

The words "each" and "per" show up in both kinds of problems. Look at these two:

"Each box has 12 soaps. How many soaps are in 15 boxes?" You know the groups and want the total: multiply. 12 × 15 = 180 soaps.

"180 soaps are packed 12 in each box. How many boxes are there?" You know the total and want the groups: divide. 180 ÷ 12 = 15 boxes.

So the safest thing is to picture the situation: am I putting equal groups together (multiply), or am I splitting a total (divide)?

3The 4 steps, again

The 4-step method you learned in Lesson 1.3 works for any word problem, including multiplication and division:

1Read and underline the numbers and what the question asks.

2Choose the operation: am I putting equal groups together, or splitting a total?

3Solve with the standard method.

4Does it make sense? Check your answer and its unit. If there is a remainder, decide what to do with it.

Example: a multiplication problem

"A hall has 18 rows of chairs. Each row has 24 chairs. How many chairs are there in all?"

1Underline: 18 rows, 24 chairs in each row. The question asks for the total number of chairs.

2Choose: every row has 24 chairs: equal groups that come together. It is a multiplication.

3Solve: 18 × 24 = 432.

✓Does it make sense? 432 chairs in 18 rows is reasonable. The answer is 432 chairs.

Example: a division problem

"A company splits 384 boxes equally among 8 trucks. How many boxes does each truck carry?"

1Underline: 384 boxes, 8 trucks. The question asks for the boxes in each truck.

2Choose: "splits equally" and "each truck": you split a total. It is a division.

3Solve: 384 ÷ 8 = 48.

✓Does it make sense? Each truck carries part of the total, so 48 boxes is reasonable. Check: 48 × 8 = 384 ✓. The answer is 48 boxes.

4Properties as shortcuts

The properties of operations are not just theory. They are shortcuts that help you calculate faster, sometimes even in your head.

Properties of multiplication, what they say, and how they help
PropertyWhat it saysHow it helps
CommutativeThe order does not change the answer. 6 × 9 = 9 × 6Put the factors in the order that is easiest for you.
AssociativeYou can group the factors in any way. (2 × 5) × 7 = 2 × (5 × 7)Group first the numbers that give a round number (2 × 5 = 10).
DistributiveYou can split a factor into parts. 25 × 4 = (20 × 4) + (5 × 4)Turn one hard multiplication into two easy ones.
Zero and onen × 0 = 0 · n × 1 = nThe answer is immediate, with no calculation.

Shortcut: 25 × 4 in your head

1With the distributive property: 25 × 4 = (20 × 4) + (5 × 4).

220 × 4 = 80, and 5 × 4 = 20.

✓80 + 20 = 100. No paper needed.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open. With it, you finish Topic 2.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises change every time. Do at least 10 in each activity, and keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Write your own problems. Write two word problems with real numbers from your work, your kitchen, or a trip: one you solve by multiplying and one you solve by dividing. Solve them the next day. Did the words you wrote really point to the right operation?
  • In your daily life. Find a cost that repeats with multiplication (for example, gas for a month) and a split with division (for example, a budget across the days of the week).

📘Key words

equal groups
Groups that all have the same number of things. 15 packs of 12 soaps are 15 equal groups.
split equally (share equally)
To divide a total so that every group gets the same amount.
per
For each. "$15 per hour" means $15 for each hour.
double, triple
Two times, three times a number. The triple of 35 is 3 × 35 = 105.
shortcut
A faster way to get the same answer, like using a property to calculate in your head.
