A level is marked 🟢 ready only after a GED teacher who teaches in English has reviewed it. Your comments decide that. The Level 1 guide explains the general style; this guide focuses on what is new in Level 2.

2. How to split the work

Nobody needs to read all 48 pages. Two ways that work well:

  • By topic: each reviewer takes one or two topics (3 lessons each) and signs off on them in section 9.
  • By sample: if you have only an hour, read one lesson and its practice page all the way through, and skim the rest of that topic. Good samples: 1.2 (signed numbers), 3.2 (PEMDAS), 5.2 (percent change), 6.1 (simple interest), 7.3 (area of a circle), and 8.1 (mean and median).

In each lesson, please try the 8-question quiz at the end, and in each practice page do at least two exercises per activity, including one with a wrong answer on purpose, to see the hint.

3. What is new in Level 2

  • Calculator. Every page has a Calculator button with a TI-30XS MultiView simulator and the GED formula reference sheet. The lessons show the key sequence for typical problems.
  • Standards. Each lesson ends with the GED Assessment Targets it works on (direct or as a foundation), the Mathematical Practices, and the CCR Standards for Adult Education.
  • Interactive explanations. Before the exercises, most activities let the student move something and see the math: a number line, coin and debt chips, slices of a circle, a budget circle graph, a die rolled 1,000 times.
  • Figures. Geometry and statistics pages draw the figures with their measurements. Every figure has a text description for screen readers.
  • Leo. The LearnGED owl appears in the feedback (hint, well done, congratulations).

4. What to look for

  • Reading level. Can a student who reads with difficulty follow every sentence? Mark any word or sentence that is too hard.
  • Tone. Our students are adults. Does anything sound childish, or too academic?
  • Math accuracy. Numbers, signs, rounding, units, and every step of the worked examples.
  • US usage. Anything that sounds translated or “not how we say it here” (see the questions in section 8).
  • Hints. Do the hints name the likely mistake without giving the answer away?
  • Answer checking. If you type a correct answer in another reasonable form (for example 0.5 instead of 1/2, or $1,200 instead of 1200) and the page rejects it, please tell us.
  • Figures. Labels that overlap, measurements in the wrong place, or colors that are hard to tell apart.
  • Accessibility. Try the pages on a phone and with the keyboard only.

5. How feedback works

The same idea as in Level 1: a mistake is information, not a failure.

  1. First wrong answer: a hint that points to the specific mistake (for example, “You used the diameter as the radius: divide by 2 first.”). The student tries again.
  2. Second wrong answer: the solution, explained step by step.
  3. Questions with only two choices (for example, “addition or subtraction?”, “circumference or area?”) show the explanation right away, because a second try would be a guess.
  4. Praise is about the strategy, not about being smart.
  5. Scores count answers solved on the first try and answers solved after a hint.

6. Math conventions

TopicLearnGED
Negative numbersTrue minus sign: −7. Students may type −7 or -7. When a negative number is substituted, it goes in parentheses: 5 − (−3).
Fractions with signsThe sign goes in front of the fraction: −3/4.
Order of operationsPEMDAS, with the warning that multiplication and division (and addition and subtraction) go from left to right.
Ratios3 to 4, 3:4, and 3/4. Unit rate: “$2.50 per pound.”
PercentsNo space: 25%. Percent change = change ÷ original × 100.
Money$1,200 (no .00 for whole dollars) and $1,240.50; final answers rounded to the cent.
Simple interestI = P × r × t and A = P + I, the letters of the GED formula sheet. The rate goes in as a decimal and the time in years.
Straight-line depreciationD = (V₀ − Vr) ÷ n; book value = V₀ − D × t. (Not on the GED sheet; see question 6.)
UnitsUS customary units everywhere (in, ft, yd, mi, lb, oz, gal, °F). Metric units appear only in Lesson 6.3, which teaches the conversions.
ππ ≈ 3.14 in the worked examples. Answers found with the calculator's π key, or written in terms of π (14π), are also accepted.
RoundingTo the hundredth unless the problem says otherwise; paint, tile, and similar items are always rounded up.
ProbabilityAccepted as a fraction (3/8), a decimal (0.375), or a percent (37.5%).
NumbersComma for thousands, point for decimals, in English and in Spanish.

8. Questions for you

  1. “Rule of three” (Lesson 4.2) is very common in Latin America. Do your students know it, or should we call it only “solving a proportion with cross products”?
  2. Sales tax. The examples use rates from 7% to 10%. Would you rather see real local rates, such as Chicago's?
  3. Percent change. Is “percent increase / percent decrease” what your students see in class, or do you also use “percent of change”?
  4. “Helper line” (Lesson 7.1) for the line that splits a composite figure: is there a better everyday term?
  5. “Weighted mean” or “weighted average”? Which one do your students hear more?
  6. Depreciation (Lesson 6.2) is not on the GED formula sheet. Is it worth keeping in Level 2 as an application of percents and linear patterns?
  7. π. We accept both 3.14 and the calculator's π. Is that how you grade?
  8. Quizzes. Each lesson ends with 8 questions. Is that enough before moving on?
  9. Would a “read aloud” button help your students?
