Lesson 1.2 · Primary and secondary sources: origin, purpose, and context

Before you believe a document, ask it three questions: who made it, what for, and what was happening around it.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.1 · Evidence from sourcesSSP.5 · Purpose and context

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell apart a primary source from a secondary one by its relationship to the events, not by its age or format.
  • Question a document before reading it closely: who made it, when, for whom, and for what purpose.
  • Decide which claims a document supports and which it does not, and say what that decision rests on.
  • Compare two sources about the same place and explain how they complete each other.

Before you start

Lesson 1.1 ended with an inquiry: a compelling question and the supporting questions that hold it up. It promised that here you would look for its sources. But before looking, you need to know how to read what you find, and reading a document is not the same as understanding it.

A document is understood when you know where it comes from: who wrote it, when, for whom, and looking for what. Without that, two texts about the same neighborhood seem to say the same thing, and they do not. This lesson works with two real documents about one Chicago neighborhood at the end of the 1800s.

1Primary and secondary: the difference almost everyone gets backward

The most common mix-up is thinking that primary means old. It does not. A source is primary when it was made by someone who took part in the events or saw them, inside the world it describes. It is secondary when someone wrote it later, from outside, by studying other people’s primary sources.

A history textbook published last year about the Civil War is secondary. A letter written by a soldier in 1863 is primary. So far, it is easy. Now the hard part: a newspaper article from 1863 about that same war is also primary, even if the reporter was not at the battle, because the document belongs to that moment and tells us how the war was being told then. And a video recorded yesterday by a witness to something that happened yesterday is primary, even though it is from this week.

The question that settles almost every case

Does the document belong to the world it describes, or does it look at that world from outside and from later?

Primary and secondary sources: examples
Primary (from inside, at the time)Secondary (from outside, later)
Letters, diaries, and memoirs of people who were thereHistory textbooks and encyclopedias
Laws, court decisions, speeches, and the ConstitutionA historian’s book that analyzes those laws
Census tables and government reports of the timeA documentary that explains the period
Photos, posters, and political cartoons of the timeA website that summarizes the event

And one more twist: the same source changes category depending on what you ask it. A history textbook from 1950 is secondary if you ask about the Civil War. But it is primary if you ask how the Civil War was taught in 1950. The category does not live in the document: it lives in the relationship between the document and your question.

2The three questions: origin, purpose, and context

Classifying a source is the first step, not the last. Knowing that a document is primary does not yet tell you whether it is reliable or what it is good for. For that you need three questions, asked before you read the document closely.

The three questions to ask any document
QuestionWhat to look forWhy it matters
Origin: who made it, and when?The author, the date it was made, the date of the events, and the distance between the two. Also the author’s position: the factory owner, the state inspector, or the worker.A report written the same day does not have the same problems as a memoir written twenty years later.
Purpose: what was it made for, and for whom?What the author wanted: to convince, to inform, to ask for money, to defend himself, to keep a record, to sell. And the reader the author had in mind.A flyer for donors and an internal report on the same topic will not say the same things, even if the same person signs both.
Context: what was happening around it?What people were arguing about at the time, what could be said and what could not, which words were normal then and are offensive now.Context does not excuse what a document says, but it explains why it says it that way.

What these questions are not for

They are not for deciding whether a document is “good” or “bad,” or for throwing it away. A source with an interest is not a useless source: it is a source you must read knowing what it defends. In fact, the most revealing sources are often the ones with the strongest interest, because they show what someone wanted people to believe.

Now let’s use the three questions on two real documents about the same place: the Halsted Street neighborhood, on Chicago’s Near West Side, at the end of the 1800s. First, look at the dates of each one.

📜 Document A · Jane Addams remembers Halsted Street

Document
Chapter V, “The First Days at Hull-House”
Author
Jane Addams (1860–1935), co-founder of Hull-House, a settlement house on Chicago’s West Side
Events it tells
Chicago, 1889 and 1890
Written
1910, twenty-one years later
Published in
Twenty Years at Hull-House, New York, The Macmillan Company, 1910
Kind of source
Primary · published memoir

Fragment 1 · How the author introduces the description (page 97)

Halsted Street has grown so familiar during twenty years of residence that it is difficult to recall its gradual changes,–the withdrawal of the more prosperous Irish and Germans, and the slow substitution of Russian Jews, Italians, and Greeks. A description of the street such as I gave in those early addresses still stands in my mind as sympathetic and correct.

Fragment 2 · The description of the neighborhood (pages 98–99)

The policy of the public authorities of never taking an initiative, and always waiting to be urged to do their duty, is obviously fatal in a neighborhood where there is little initiative among the citizens. The idea underlying our self-government breaks down in such a ward. The streets are inexpressibly dirty, the number of schools inadequate, sanitary legislation unenforced, the street lighting bad, the paving miserable and altogether lacking in the alleys and smaller streets, and the stables foul beyond description. Hundreds of houses are unconnected with the street sewer.

The older and richer inhabitants seem anxious to move away as rapidly as they can afford it. They make room for newly arrived immigrants who are densely ignorant of civic duties.

Look closely at Fragment 1 before you go on

The description of the neighborhood is not what Addams wrote in 1910: it is what she used to say in her speeches of 1889 and 1890, quoted by herself twenty-one years later and declared still correct. They are two documents inside one, and they do not have the same date.

📜 Document B · Agnes Holbrook explains the maps

Document
“Map Notes and Comments,” first chapter of a group social study
Author
Agnes Sinclair Holbrook, a Hull-House resident who drew the maps
Data
Collected from April 6 to July 15, 1893, by four agents of the U.S. Department of Labor, house by house
Written
January 1895, less than two years later
Published in
Hull-House Maps and Papers, New York, Thomas Y. Crowell & Co., 1895
Kind of source
Primary · social study based on a house-by-house survey

Fragment 1 · Where the data come from (page 7)

The entire time of four government schedule men from the 6th of April till the 15th of July, 1893, was devoted to examining each house, tenement, and room in the district, and filling out tenement and family schedules. These schedules were returned daily to Mrs. Kelley; and before they were forwarded to the Commissioner of Labor at Washington, a copy was made by one of the Hull-House residents, of the nationality of each individual, his wages when employed, and the number of weeks he was idle during the year beginning April 1, 1892.

Fragment 2 · What the author admits (pages 12–13)

It is inevitable that errors should have crept in. Carelessness and indifference on the part of those questioned are undoubtedly frequent, and change of occupation as well as irregularity of employment entail some confusion and uncertainty. Then, too, the length of time covered by the investigation is so great — one year — that neither buildings nor tenants remain the same throughout.

Tenants evicted for non-payment of rent form a floating population of some magnitude, and a kodak view of such a shifting scene must necessarily be blurred and imperfect here and there.

Fragment 3 · What she says it is for (page 13)

These notes and comments are designed rather to make the maps intelligible than to furnish independent data; and the aim of both maps and notes is to present conditions rather than to advance theories — to bring within reach of the public exact information concerning this quarter of Chicago rather than to advise methods by which it may be improved.

3What a document supports and what it does not

This is the step the test rewards, and the one that gives meaning to everything before it. It is not about believing or not believing a document. It is about deciding which claims it can support and which ones need something else.

Both documents in this lesson are primary, and both are honest in their own way. But they do not support the same things.

What each document supports and does not support
It supports…It does not support…
Document A · AddamsHow a reformer who lived there saw the neighborhood, what seemed urgent to her, and what words she used for her neighbors. Without meaning to, it also shows the assumptions of her class.How many people lived there or of what nationality: her picture comes from her own speeches, not from a count. Nor what the neighbors thought: none of them speaks for himself.
Document B · HolbrookWho lived on each lot, how the data were collected, and the wages of the families. It also supports something rare: its own margins of error, because the author lists them.Population density (each lot is colored whole, whether one person lives there or a hundred), the causes of anything, or the voices of the people counted, who appear as data, not as people who speak.

Do not ask a document for more than it can give

A source that declares its limits is not true for that reason, and one that does not declare them is not false for that reason. Addams does not lie when she describes dirty streets and houses without sewers: Document B, made in a different way, points in the same direction. When two sources made in different ways agree, the claim gets much stronger. That is called corroboration.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The three-question card (25 minutes). Choose any document you have at home that is not a textbook: an old family letter, a receipt, a campaign flyer, a notice from an agency, a dated photo. Write its card with the three questions: origin, purpose, and context. Then do the same with a second document about the same topic but from a different origin. Check: if your “purpose” only says “to inform,” you are not done; ask what the author wanted to achieve.
  • Supports and does not support (30 minutes). With the same document, make two columns: five claims it supports, and five claims someone might think it supports but it does not. Next to each claim in the second column, write which other source you would need. Check: point to the exact line that supports each claim in the first column.
  • Sources for your inquiry (35 minutes). Go back to the inquiry you wrote in Lesson 1.1. For each supporting question, name two sources that would answer it, one primary and one secondary, with their likely origin and purpose. Check: if you cannot imagine any primary source for a question, the question needs to be rewritten.

➡️Coming next

This lesson set up the second tool of the course: questioning a document before believing it. Origin, purpose, and context, and then the decision the test rewards: separating what a document supports from what it only seems to support.

The two documents here talked about the same neighborhood and did not contradict each other: they completed each other. One remembered it and the other measured it. But often two sources about the same event say things that do not fit together, and then it is not enough to question them one by one. Lesson 1.3 puts two voices side by side about the same week in Chicago: the fight of 1905 over whether the city should own its streetcars.

📘Key words

Primary source
A source made inside the world it describes, by someone who took part or saw it, or that belongs to that moment.
Secondary source
A source written later, from outside, by studying primary sources.
Origin
Who made a document, when, and how far from the events.
Purpose
What a document was made for, and for whom.
Context
What was happening around a document when it was made.
Corroboration
Agreement between sources made in different ways, which makes a claim stronger.
Settlement house
A center in a poor neighborhood where reformers lived and offered classes and services, like Hull-House in Chicago (1889).
