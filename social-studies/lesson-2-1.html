Lesson 2.1 · Graphs, tables, and the TI-30XS calculator

A graph is read by its structure before its content. And when two things go together, that does not mean one causes the other.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.10 · Graphs and correlationSSP.6 · Data in many forms

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Read bar, line, circle, and scatter graphs, always starting with the title, axes, units, and scale.
  • Tell apart the independent and dependent variables and say which axis each one goes on.
  • Describe a correlation without claiming a cause, and suggest other explanations for a pattern.
  • Use the TI-30XS calculator with confidence: the toggle key and the order of operations.

Before you start

Topic 1 ended with two weeklies from 1905 that told the same meeting and seemed to tell two. Neither lied: each chose what to tell and with which words.

This topic carries the same suspicion over to numbers. A graph seems more objective than a paragraph, and that is why it is more dangerous: someone also drew it, and chose what to measure, in which units, and where the scale starts. A number does not speak for itself. We start with the most practical thing, which is reading, and with the tool the test lets you use, the calculator. Good preparation: Math Level 2 (graphs and percents).

1The five steps, always in this order

Most mistakes on the test do not come from bad arithmetic. They come from answering before looking. These five steps take less than twenty seconds and prevent almost all of them.

The five steps, always in this order
StepAsk yourself
1 · TitleWhat is this graph about, and what is it not about?
2 · AxesWhat is on the horizontal axis, and what is on the vertical axis?
3 · UnitsDollars, people, percents, thousands of people? Mixing up “thousands” and “ones” changes the answer a thousand times.
4 · ScaleWhere does the vertical axis start? If it does not start at zero, differences look bigger than they are.
5 · TrendOnly now: does it go up, go down, stay the same, or show no pattern?

The step almost everyone skips

Step 4. A bar graph whose vertical axis starts at 40 instead of 0 can turn a difference of three points into a mountain. It is not faking: the numbers are correct. It is the same one-sided choice of Topic 1, made with a scale.

Line graph · following a change over time

The same nine numbers, now on a real time axis, drawn two ways.

The trap of this lesson

From 1997 to 2009 the federal minimum wage did not move: twelve years at $5.15. The straight line draws that stretch as a gentle slope, and someone reading quickly will conclude it rose little by little. The step line draws it flat and then a jump. A straight line between two points is a guess about what happened in between, and it is not always true.

Circle graph · parts of a whole

How a federal budget is split among five categories. Illustrative data, rounded so they add up to 100%.

A circle graph only works when the parts add up to a whole and there are few of them. With more than five or six slices, the eye can no longer compare, and a table works better.

3Which variable goes on which axis

As soon as a graph relates two measured things, you need to know which one leads.

  • The independent variable is the one that is supposed to act, the one that is chosen, or the one that simply happens first. It goes on the horizontal axis.
  • The dependent variable is the one that is supposed to respond. It goes on the vertical axis.
A trick that almost never fails

Complete the sentence “if ______ changes, ______ will change.” The first blank is the independent variable and goes along the bottom. Time is always independent: nothing we measure makes the years change.

Scatter plot · do two things go together?

Sixteen areas: average years of schooling and average yearly household income, in thousands of dollars. Illustrative data.

Each point is one area. The cloud rises from left to right: where there are more years of schooling, there tends to be more income. That is a positive correlation. Notice that the cloud is wide: some areas have the same schooling and very different incomes.

Negative correlation

Hours of TV and grade average. The cloud goes down to the right: when one goes up, the other tends to go down. Illustrative data.

No correlation

Letters in the last name and grade average. The points have no direction. Nobody expects a name to affect grades, so this is what a cloud looks like when there really is no relationship. Illustrative data.

4Correlation is not causation

This is the central point of the lesson, and the one the test rewards. When two things rise together, that does not prove that one makes the other rise. For any correlation there are always four possible explanations, and the graph alone cannot choose among them.

Four explanations for the same cloud of points
ExplanationSchooling and income
1 · A causes BMore years of school lead to better jobs.
2 · B causes AFamilies with more income can keep their children in school longer.
3 · A third thing causes bothA prosperous area has both better schools and better jobs.
4 · ChanceWith few data, two things can go together by luck.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

How the answer that scores points sounds

Not: “The graph proves that studying more makes you earn more.” Yes: “The graph shows a positive correlation between schooling and income; to know whether one causes the other, we would need data the graph does not give.” The right answer to a scatter plot never starts with “this proves that,” but with “this is consistent with.”

5The TI-30XS calculator clinic

The TI-30XS MultiView is the calculator allowed on the GED® test; on the Social Studies test it appears on screen for many questions. Two things about it cause almost all avoidable mistakes.

The toggle key

The calculator often gives results as fractions. If the question asks for a decimal and you copy the fraction, the answer counts as wrong even if the value is the same. The toggle key (◄ ►) switches the result on the screen between fraction and decimal.

A habit worth making automatic

After every result, look at what the question asks for. If it asks for a decimal and you see a fraction, press the toggle key before you write anything.

The order of operations

The calculator follows the math order: parentheses, then powers, then multiplication and division from left to right, and last addition and subtraction. That is good, but it means you must type the parentheses you have in your head.

The most expensive mistake: the percent change from 40 to 52

✗52 − 40 ÷ 40 = gives 51: the calculator divides 40 by 40 first.

✓( 52 − 40 ) ÷ 40 = gives 0.3, that is, 30%. With the toggle key you would see 3/10.

✓Both were typed correctly and the calculator answered both without complaint. But the first one answers a different question. A calculator never warns you that it answered something other than what you meant to ask.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The five steps out loud (15 minutes). Find three graphs in a newspaper or on a government website. For each one, say the five steps out loud before drawing any conclusion. Note which one has a vertical axis that does not start at zero. Check: if it took you more than thirty seconds per graph, you were reading the content instead of the structure.
  • Schooling and income (25 minutes). With the scatter plot of section 3, write three sentences: which variable is independent and why, how you would describe the correlation, and one explanation different from “studying more makes you earn more.” Check: if your third sentence uses the word “proves,” rewrite it.
  • Twelve calculations (30 minutes). With the calculator: the percent changes from 25 to 30, from 80 to 60, and from 12 to 15; the divisions 3 ÷ 8, 5 ÷ 16, and 7 ÷ 20; and 2 + 3 × 4, (2 + 3) × 4, 100 − 20 ÷ 5, (100 − 20) ÷ 5, 6 + 6 ÷ 6 + 6, and (6 + 6) ÷ (6 + 6). Check: 20%, −25%, 25%; 0.375, 0.3125, 0.35; 14, 20, 96, 16, 13, 1.

📘Key words

Scale (of an axis)
The values marked along an axis and where they start.
Independent variable
The one that acts or comes first; it goes on the horizontal axis.
Dependent variable
The one that responds; it goes on the vertical axis.
Correlation
A pattern in which two variables tend to change together (positive, negative, or none).
Causation
A relationship in which one thing actually makes another happen. A correlation alone does not show it.
Third variable
A hidden factor that causes two things to change together.
Toggle key
The TI-30XS key that switches a result between fraction and decimal.
