Lesson 2.2 · Measures of center in population and economic data

Mean, median, and mode can tell three different stories about the same data. Knowing which one fits is the skill.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.11 · Mean, median, mode, rangeSSP.6 · Box plots and histograms

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Calculate the mean, median, mode, and range of a set of social data.
  • Decide which measure best describes a set, according to its shape.
  • Build the five-number summary and read a box plot.
  • Compare two groups and explain what it means that one box is wider than the other.

Before you start

Lesson 2.1 taught you to read a graph before believing it, and it ended with an uncomfortable idea: a correlation allows four explanations, and the drawing cannot choose. Here we go one level deeper. Before the graph comes the number that sums it up, and those numbers are chosen too. When someone says “the typical income in the neighborhood is ninety thousand dollars,” they made a decision before speaking: they decided what typical means.

1The four measures

Three measures describe the center of a set of data. The fourth describes how spread out it is.

The four measures and how to find them
MeasureWhat it isHow to find it
Mean (average)An equal shareAdd all the values and divide by how many there are.
MedianThe middle valuePut the values in order and take the one in the middle. If there is an even number of values, take the mean of the two middle ones.
ModeThe most frequent valueCount which value appears most. There may be none, or more than one.
RangeHow far apart the values areLargest value minus smallest value.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Always put the data in order first

The median does not exist until the data are in order. It is the most common mistake and the easiest to avoid: if you did not order the list, the number in the middle is not the median.

Example · The ten values 12, 15, 15, 18, 22, 25, 25, 25, 31, 40

1Mean: the sum is 228, and 228 ÷ 10 = 22.8.

2Median: ten values, so take the 5th and 6th: 22 and 25. (22 + 25) ÷ 2 = 23.5.

3Mode: 25 appears three times.

4Range: 40 − 12 = 28.

✓Three different “centers” for the same data: 22.8, 23.5, and 25. None of them is wrong.

2When the mean misleads

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Look at the mean row. Neighborhood A comes out at 90 thousand and B at 41: it looks as if people in A earn more than twice as much. Now look at the median: 40 and 41. Almost the same.

Both rows are correct. The difference comes from a single household, the one with 600 thousand, which pulls the mean up and does not move the median. Ten of the eleven households in A earn 52 thousand or less. None earns 90.

Why it happens

The mean shares equally: if we added up all the money in the neighborhood and split it among the eleven households, each would get 90 thousand. But nobody shares anything. The median only counts positions, so a huge value takes up one position, no more. An outlier moves the mean and leaves the median alone. And the range is the warning: a range of 572 against one of 14 means something in A is very far from the rest.

3The shape decides the measure

No measure is better than the others. One fits the shape of the data, and to see the shape you draw a histogram: the values are split into ranges, and you count how many fall in each one.

Symmetric

Most values gather in the middle and drop off evenly on both sides. The mean and the median almost match. Illustrative data.

Skewed to the right

Most values gather on the left, and a long tail stretches toward high values. The tail pulls the mean above the median. Illustrative data.

Incomes, almost everywhere in the world, have the second shape. That is why the U.S. Census Bureau and other agencies usually publish the median household income, not the mean: it describes the ordinary family better.

Which measure to use
If the data…UseBecause
are symmetric, with no extreme valuesthe meanit uses all the information and matches the median
have outliers or a long tailthe mediana few extreme values cannot pull it
are categories, not numbersthe modeit is the only one that works: you cannot average a color or a language

4The five-number summary and the box plot

A single number never describes a set of data. Five numbers almost always do.

The five-number summary
NumberWhat it is
MinimumThe lowest value.
First quartile (Q1)The median of the lower half. One quarter of the data are below it.
MedianThe middle.
Third quartile (Q3)The median of the upper half. Three quarters of the data are below it.
MaximumThe highest value.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A box plot draws those five numbers. The box goes from Q1 to Q3 and holds the middle half of the data; the line inside is the median; the whiskers reach the minimum and the maximum. Below: yearly salaries of eleven workers in each of two sectors, in thousands of dollars. Illustrative data.

Five-number summary of the two sectors, in thousands of dollars
SectorMinimumQ1MedianQ3Maximum
Retail2226303537
Technology3845557088

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

What a wide box tells you

It is not only that technology pays more. Its box is much wider: inside the same sector, salaries are very unequal. In retail almost everyone earns about the same. The width of the box (Q3 − Q1) is where the middle half of the people are. Two groups can have the same median and very different lives.

With eleven values

Once the eleven values are in order, the median is the 6th, Q1 is the 3rd, and Q3 is the 9th.

5Mean or median: a one-sided choice

Go back to Neighborhood A. Mean 90, median 40. Both numbers are correct, and both describe the same neighborhood. Now picture two people who must speak about that neighborhood in public.

Two true statements about the same neighborhood
Who speaksWhat they sayWhy
An official who wants to attract investment“The average household income here is 90 thousand dollars.”The mean makes the neighborhood look prosperous.
A group asking for public aid“The median household income here is 40 thousand dollars.”The median makes the need visible.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

This is Lesson 1.3 again

Neither one lies, and neither one made a mistake in the arithmetic. One chose the mean and the other the median, just as the two weeklies of 1905 chose which facts to tell. Bias does not need lies: choosing is enough. The defense is always the same question: why this measure and not the other? When someone publishes a mean without the median, or the other way around, ask what looks better with the one they chose, and what stays hidden.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.

📘Key words

Mean
The sum of the values divided by how many there are; the average.
Median
The middle value of the ordered data.
Mode
The most frequent value; the only measure of center for categories.
Range
Largest value minus smallest value.
Outlier
A value far away from the rest.
Skewed distribution
Data with a long tail on one side; incomes are skewed to the right.
Quartiles and box plot
Q1 and Q3 split the data into quarters; the box plot draws the minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and maximum.
