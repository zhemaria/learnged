Lesson 8.1 · Multiple-source scenarios: text, graph, and map

Before saying two sources contradict each other, check period, population, and unit.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.6 · Integrating sourcesSSP.8 · Comparing sourcesSSP.7 · Evaluating evidence

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Reconcile the information in a text, a graph, and a fact about the same subject.
  • Tell apart a real contradiction from a difference of period, population, or unit.
  • Decide whether the evidence supports a conclusion or only brushes against it.
  • Answer three linked questions without rereading the stimulus from the beginning.

1What a scenario is and how to attack it

The seven earlier topics taught content and skills one at a time. Topic 8 brings nothing new: it puts everything together and puts it under a clock. It starts with the format that weighs most on the test. Many questions do not come alone: they come in scenarios, two or three questions about the same set of sources. If you know how to handle them, you save time on the whole test; if you don’t, you lose it rereading.

A scenario gives you two or three sources on the same subject (a text, a graph, a map, a table) and then asks several questions in a row. The sources never say exactly the same thing: if they did, one would be unnecessary. The order in which you read decides how long it takes. This is the order that works.

How to attack a scenario, in four steps
StepWhat to do
1 · Titles and labels firstRead the titles and labels of everything, without going into the content: what each source measures, from what period, and in what units. Thirty seconds.
2 · What each one addsAsk what each source gives that the other does not. That is why both are there, and it is almost always the answer to some question.
3 · The question, then the detailRead the first question and only then go back to the detail, to the piece you need. Do not memorize the whole stimulus.
4 · Do not start overFor the second and third questions, do not go back to the beginning. You already know where everything is. Rereading from zero is what eats the time.

The question that solves half the scenarios

It is the one in step 2: what do you know from this source that you would not know from the other? A text usually gives the reason, the intention, or the context; a graph gives the size and the trend; a map gives the distribution. When a question asks for something only one source can give, the answer is there and you do not need to look at the rest.

2Scenario A · Louisiana, 1896–1904

Answer the three questions in a row, without going back to the beginning of the scenario. Use the four steps: titles first, then what each source adds.

🧩 Scenario A · Louisiana, 1896–1904 · Three sources on the same subject

Source 1 · Text

In 1898 the state of Louisiana adopted a new constitution that made a poll tax and a literacy test requirements for registering to vote. The same constitution included a clause that exempted from both requirements anyone whose father or grandfather had been entitled to vote before January 1, 1867.

Source 2 · Black voters registered in Louisiana

State voter registration records, as compiled by The Historic New Orleans Collection and 64 Parishes. From 130,344 to 1,342 in eight years.

Source 3 · An added fact

In the same period, the number of white voters registered in Louisiana also fell considerably. Many poor white men never used the exemption clause.

✏️

Scenario A · Question 1

What does Source 1 give that Source 2 does not?

✏️

Scenario A · Question 2

Does Source 3 contradict Source 2?

✏️

Scenario A · Question 3

Which conclusion do the three sources support together?

What this scenario trains

Question 2 is the important one and the one most often missed. Two sources that point in different directions almost never contradict each other: they usually speak of different groups, different periods, or in different units. Before writing “they contradict each other,” check those three things. The next section is exactly that.

3When a contradiction is not a contradiction

Two true statements can seem impossible together. Almost always, one of these three is to blame.

Three reasons two true statements seem to contradict each other
CheckWhat happensExample
The periodThey measure the same thing in different years.“Immigration rose” and “immigration fell” are both true: one is about 1901–1910 and the other about 1931–1940.
The populationThey measure different groups, or a group and the whole.Scenario A: Black voters and white voters.
The unitOne measures totals and the other proportions, or they use different scales.The trickiest of the three, and the one coming next.

🧩 Scenario B · Two statements that seem to fight

Statement 1

“In the decade from 1901 to 1910, about 8.8 million immigrants came to the United States, more than in any earlier decade of its history.”

Statement 2

“In 1910 the foreign-born were about 15 percent of the population of the United States, a share similar to 1870 and 1890.”

Sources: yearly reports of the federal immigration service for the first figure; U.S. censuses, as compiled by the Census Bureau, for the second.

✏️

Scenario B · Question 1

Do the two statements contradict each other?

✏️

Scenario B · Question 2

You are asked whether American society in 1910 was made up of immigrants more than in 1870. Which statement do you use?

The rule, in one line

Before saying there is a contradiction, check period, population, and unit. If all three match and the sources still disagree, then yes: it is a real contradiction, and you must decide which source is more credible and why (Lesson 1.2). But that happens much less often than it seems.

4The four-column template

When a question gives you a conclusion and asks whether the evidence supports it, this template solves the problem and stops the impulsive answer. You can draw it on scratch paper in ten seconds.

The four-column template, applied to three conclusions about Scenario A
ClaimEvidence forEvidence againstVerdict
The constitution of 1898 was designed to reduce the Black vote.Poll tax and literacy test, with an exemption that only someone with ancestors who voted before 1867 could meet. The drop from 130,344 to 1,342.None in these sources.Supported.
The constitution of 1898 was the only cause of the drop.The dates match.Nothing in the sources rules out other causes, and no comparison is given.Not supported. The word “only” asks for more than there is.
The measure was not aimed at anyone, because white registration also fell.Source 3.The exemption clause is written so that only one group can meet it. That is design, not a side effect.Not supported. It confuses the effect with the intention written in the text.

Look at what knocks them down

It is not the data: it is a word. “Only” in the second; “not aimed at anyone” in the third. On the test, the difference between a supported conclusion and an unsupported one is often an adverb or an absolute: always, never, only, all, none. When you see one of those words in an answer choice, check whether the evidence goes that far. It almost never does.

The column people forget

It is “evidence against.” And if it stays empty, that does not mean the conclusion is right: it may mean there is not enough evidence, and that is also a verdict.

5The clock

The test lasts 70 minutes and has about 35 questions. That is 2 minutes per question on average, and scenarios are not spread evenly: the first question of each group costs more because you must read the stimulus, and the next ones cost much less.

The clock of one scenario
PartTimeWhat you do
Reading the stimulus≈ 90 secondsTitles, labels, periods, and units. You have not answered anything yet, and that is fine.
First question≈ 90 secondsGo back to the detail you need, not to the whole stimulus.
Second and third≈ 45 seconds eachYou already know where everything is. If it takes as long as the first, you are rereading.

The rule that saves the most points

If a question takes more than two minutes, mark it and move on. Coming back with spare time and a fresh head works better than getting stuck. A blank answer and a wrong answer are worth the same (zero), so never leave a blank at the end: if you must guess, guess, but at the end, not at the moment you are stuck.

6GED-style questions

These questions work like the ones on the GED® Social Studies test. Each one shows the social studies practice it checks and its depth of knowledge (DOK). Each one brings its own sources. Time yourself: about 2 minutes each.

✏️

GED-style question 1 · SSP.8.a · DOK 2–3

Statement 1: Between 1940 and 1970, about five million African Americans left the South for cities in the North and the West.
Statement 2: In 1970, about half of all African Americans still lived in the South.
What is the best way to describe these statements?

✏️

GED-style question 2 · SSP.6.a · DOK 2

Source 1: a line graph shows U.S. unemployment rising from about 3% in 1929 to about 25% in 1933.
Source 2: in his first inaugural address (March 1933), President Franklin D. Roosevelt said, “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself.”
What can you learn only from Source 2?

GED-style question 3 · SSP.7.b · DOK 2–3

About 34% of U.S. women were in the paid labor force in 1950, and about 60% in 2000.
Which conclusion is supported by this information?

GED-style question 4 · SSP.8.a · DOK 2–3

Statement A: In 1907, more than 1.2 million immigrants were admitted to the United States.
Statement B: In 1933, fewer than 25,000 immigrants were admitted.
What best explains the difference?

GED-style question 5 · SSP.6.b · DOK 2

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Time yourself for real (20 minutes). Go back to Scenario A with a timer running. Write down how long you take on the three parts separately: reading the stimulus, the first question, and the other two. Repeat with Scenario B. Check: what matters is the shape, not the total. If questions 2 and 3 cost almost as much as question 1, you are rereading. If so, repeat it covering the stimulus while you answer.
  • Build a scenario yourself (35 minutes). Choose a subject from any topic of the course and gather three sources of your own: a short text, a number, and a third thing. Write three linked questions, and note which source each one needs. Then write a false conclusion that looks true with those three sources. Check: the easiest way is to add an absolute (only, always, all) to a true statement. If you can build the trap, you will recognize it on the test.
  • Four columns on a news story (25 minutes). Find a news story with a graph. Write the conclusion the headline wants you to draw and put it through the four-column template, using only what is in the story. Check: if the “evidence against” column stayed empty, do not accept the conclusion yet. Ask what data would be needed to question it, and whether the story has them.

➡️Coming next

Three ideas to take away. Ask what each source gives that the other does not: that question solves half the scenarios and saves half the time. Before saying there is a contradiction, check period, population, and unit, because two statements that seem to fight almost always measure different things. And what usually sinks a conclusion is one word: only, always, all, none.

None of this is new. It is what you did in Lesson 1.3 with two accounts of the same meeting, in 6.2 with an advertisement and an autobiography, and in 7.3 with two opinions from the same court. The difference is that now there is a clock. Lesson 8.2 asks you to do all of this on your own, on a subject you choose from your own community: the final project.

📘Key words

Scenario
A group of two or three questions about the same set of sources.
Stimulus
The material a question is based on: text, graph, map, or table.
Contradiction
Two claims that cannot both be true with the same period, population, and unit.
Absolute
A word that allows no exceptions: always, never, only, all, none.
Verdict
Your judgment on whether the evidence supports a claim, with the fact that justifies it.
Poll tax
A fee one had to pay in order to vote; banned in federal elections by the Twenty-fourth Amendment (1964).
Grandfather clause
A rule exempting from voting requirements those whose ancestors could vote before a certain date.
