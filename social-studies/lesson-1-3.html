Lesson 1.3 · Bias, point of view, and persuasive language

A source can be one-sided without telling a single lie. The bias is in what it chooses to tell, and in its words.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.5 · Point of view and biasSSP.7 · Fact and opinionSSP.8 · Comparing sources

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell apart bias from falsehood, and recognize that a source can be one-sided without telling a single lie.
  • Detect a document’s point of view through its words, whom it names, and to whom it attributes what it claims.
  • Find what a document leaves out by comparing it with another about the same events.
  • Use two one-sided sources to rebuild what happened, and sort statements into fact, opinion, and reasoned judgment.

Before you start

Lesson 1.2 taught you to question a document on its own: origin, purpose, and context, and then decide what it supports. The two documents there did not contradict each other. They completed each other.

Here something else happens. Two weeklies came out on the same day, Saturday, September 23, 1905. Both tell about the same meeting, held on Friday the 15th in the Chicago City Council. Both are primary sources. Neither lies. And yet they seem to describe two different meetings. Finding out how that can happen is the whole lesson.

1Bias is not lying

The word bias is almost always used as an accusation. Saying that a newspaper is biased sounds like saying it lies. It is worth separating the two ideas from the start, because the GED® Social Studies test does not ask whether a source lies: it asks where it is looking from.

False and biased are not the same
A false sourceA biased source
What it doesStates facts that did not happen.States facts that did happen, but not all of them, and presents them with words that push toward a conclusion.
How you answer itBy checking the facts: it is refuted.By reading another source: it is completed.
Is it useful?Only as evidence of who spread the lie and why.Yes, twice: for its facts, and as evidence of what mattered to the writer.

The idea this lesson hangs on

Bias is not usually in what a document says. It is in what it chooses to tell, in the order it puts things, and in the words it picks. That is why a biased source is still useful: its facts are good, and its choices show whom it serves.

A beginner reader throws a one-sided source away. A skilled reader uses it twice: once for the facts it gives, and once as evidence of what mattered to the person who wrote it.

2Two weeklies, one Friday

In 1905, Chicago had been arguing for years about who should own its streetcars (then called traction lines). The lines belonged to private companies that ran them with city permits called franchises. Mayor Edward F. Dunne had won the April election promising that the city would buy them.

On Friday, September 15, the City Council’s transportation committee met to hear proposals from the companies. The aldermen asked questions. Colonel E. R. Bliss answered for the companies. Eight days later, on the same Saturday, two publications told the story of that meeting. Both are primary sources. Neither one lies. And yet, if you read them one after the other, they seem to describe two different meetings.

📜 Document A · The Public

Publication
The Public, a weekly edited in Chicago by Louis F. Post, who openly supported city ownership of the streetcars
Date
Saturday, September 23, 1905, pages 391–392
Kind of source
Primary · news report in a political weekly
Legal status
Public domain in the United States (published in 1905)

Fragment 1 · The headline and the first lines

Progress of the Chicago traction movement.

At the adjourned meeting of the local transportation committee of the City Council of Chicago on the 15th, held for the purpose of receiving proposals from the existing companies, no progress was made. All the traction interests were represented, and Col. E. R. Bliss acted as their spokesman. Col. Bliss stated that he had a proposition to make, but refused to submit it.

Fragment 2 · Alderman Finn’s questions

Alderman Finn asked him: “Will you agree to sell at any time the city asks you to for the cost of construction, irrespective of franchise rights?”

Col. Bliss replied: “We will not.”

Alderman Finn again asked: “What is the earliest time you will sell to the city, irrespective of franchise rights?”

To this Col. Bliss replied: “We cannot answer now, and will not say that we will sell at any time.”

Fragment 3 · The end of the meeting

Asked if he had a plan to offer, Col. Bliss replied that he had, but would not offer it then. Aldermen Finn and Dever tried to get the plan before the committee, but were voted down, seven to five, on Alderman Foreman’s motion to continue with the questions. The committee adjourned until the 26th.

📜 Document B · Street Railway Journal

Publication
Street Railway Journal, a trade magazine of the streetcar industry, published in New York. Its readers and its advertisers were the streetcar companies.
Date
Saturday, September 23, 1905, page 467
Kind of source
Primary · news report in a trade magazine
Legal status
Public domain in the United States (published in 1905)

Fragment 1 · The headline and the first lines

Chicago company to reply to questions propounded by the City Council.

The meeting of the committee on local transportation of the Chicago City Council on Friday, Sept. 15, ended in the traction companies agreeing to answer on Sept. 26 several questions submitted by aldermen. The tangible results of the meeting were two offers, made by the Chicago City Company, as a part of what it would agree to do in order to secure an extension of franchise.

Fragment 2 · The other company

While the Union Traction representatives did not concur in these offers, they sat silent when the one involving them was made. They are understood to be bound by it.

Fragment 3 · What is still pending

The important questions which the company has agreed to answer at the next meeting of the committee are: In any settlement agreement which may be made will it be provided that at a determined period, or periods, during the life of the agreement the city may take over your lines? Will you agree to the city taking over the lines at any time during the life of the franchise, if one is granted?

✏️

Question 1

Question 2

3The inventory of what is missing

Here is the main tool of the lesson. Put the two documents in columns and note, fact by fact, which one tells it. What appears in one column and not in the other is a decision made by the writer.

What each document tells about the same meeting
Fact from the meetingThe PublicStreet Railway Journal
The date and the committeeYesYes
Bliss refused to submit his planYesNot mentioned
Bliss’s “no” answers to Alderman FinnYes, quoted word for wordNot mentioned
The company’s two offersNot mentionedYes, the center of the story
The 7-to-5 vote against seeing the planYesNot mentioned

Look at the shape of the gap

It is not random. Everything The Public leaves out helps the companies, and everything the Street Railway Journal leaves out hurts them. When all of a source’s omissions point the same way, that direction is its point of view.

Notice one more thing. The two documents never contradict each other on a fact that can be checked. Both say the meeting was on the 15th and the next one on the 26th. When two one-sided sources agree on a detail, you can trust that detail more than if only one of them said it. Two opposite points of view, read together, give a picture that neither one gives alone.

4Four signals of persuasive language

Point of view is not announced. It leaks out through small choices in the writing. These four signals appear in almost any text, and the two documents of this lesson have all of them.

Four signals of persuasive language
SignalIn the two documentsWhy it persuades
1 · A judging word in place of a factA: “no progress was made.” B: “the tangible results were two offers.”Neither “progress” nor “tangible” is a fact: they are judgments about the facts, placed where the reader expects information.
2 · Who is named and who is notA names “Col. E. R. Bliss” again and again, with his rank, for each refusal. B never names him: it says “the company.”Putting a name on the refusals makes them personal; removing it dissolves them into an institution.
3 · A claim with no sourceB: “They are understood to be bound by it.” Understood by whom? It does not say.It presents an interpretation as if everyone already agreed with it.
4 · The headline as a frameA: “Progress of the Chicago traction movement,” over a story that says there was no progress. B: “Chicago company to reply,” about answers not yet given.The headline tells the reader how to read the story before reading it.

Fact, opinion, and reasoned judgment

The test also asks you to sort statements into three kinds. A fact can be checked: “The vote was seven to five.” An opinion is a preference that evidence cannot settle: “Bliss was a rude man.” A reasoned judgment is a conclusion built on evidence that someone could still dispute: “Since the company refused to name a date to sell, the meeting brought the city no closer to owning the lines.” Biased writing often presents an opinion or a judgment in the place where a fact should be.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The inventory of omissions (25 minutes). Find two news stories from today about the same event, in two different media. Make a three-column table like the one in section 3: the fact, and one column for each medium. Write down ten facts. Check: if the omissions do not point in any direction, the two media may share the same point of view; look for another pair.
  • Rewrite without changing the facts (20 minutes). Take Fragment 1 of Document B and rewrite it from the opposite point of view, without adding, removing, or changing a single fact. You may only change words and order. Check: if you needed a new fact to get the effect, you went from bias to falsehood. Go back and do it with words only.
  • The two-source paragraph (30 minutes). Write a paragraph of 8 to 10 lines that answers: What happened at the meeting of September 15, 1905? Use both documents. Include at least one fact that appears only in each one, and point out one place where the language of a source seemed one-sided. Check: a supporter of the city and a supporter of the companies should both be able to read it without protesting.

➡️Coming next

This lesson set up the third tool, and with it Topic 1 ends. The first lesson taught you to ask; the second, to question a document; this one, to read two at once.

The main thing to take away: the two weeklies of 1905 did not contradict each other once. Each told true facts. The whole difference was in which facts to tell and with which words. That is why bias is not fought by throwing sources away, but by adding them up.

Topic 2 changes the kind of source: instead of words, numbers. Graphs, tables, averages, and percents can also be one-sided, and the same questions (who made it, what for, what it leaves out) will work on them.

📘Key words

Bias
A one-sided way of telling true facts: choosing some, leaving out others, and using words that push toward a conclusion.
Point of view
The position from which a source looks at events, shown by what it tells and how.
Propaganda
A message made to persuade many people, often by a government or a movement.
Loaded language
Words that carry a judgment (“tangible,” “greedy,” “progress”) where a neutral word could be used.
Fact, opinion, reasoned judgment
A fact can be checked; an opinion is a preference; a reasoned judgment is a conclusion built on evidence.
Franchise
Permission from a government for a private company to run a public service, like streetcars.
Alderman
A member of the Chicago City Council, elected by a ward.
