Lesson 3.2 · The Constitution: structure and principles

Seven articles, five principles, and an argument between two fears that were both real.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.1 · Reading the ConstitutionSSP.3 · Comparing positionsSSP.8 · Two sources, one question

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Describe the structure of the Constitution: the Preamble, the seven articles, and the amendments.
  • Locate in the text the five principles: popular sovereignty, limited government, separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism.
  • Recognize checks and balances in real situations.
  • Compare the Federalist and Anti-Federalist positions fairly, and the mechanism that answered each fear.

Before you start

Lesson 3.1 ended with the Declaration explaining why to break with a government. Eleven years later, after a war and a first national government (the Articles of Confederation) that proved too weak to pay its debts or settle quarrels among the states, the founders met in Philadelphia to write a new one. The result is the oldest written national constitution still in use.

A reading note from Topic 1: the English text of the Constitution is the legal one. Any Spanish version, including official ones, is a translation, and in a legal text the choice of one word can change the reach of a clause. For studying it works perfectly; for arguing about the exact meaning of a clause, go to the original.

1How it is built

The Constitution is short. You can read it in less than an hour, and its structure can be learned in five minutes once you see the pattern: the first three articles create the three branches, in Montesquieu’s order.

The parts of the Constitution and what each one sets up
PartWhat it sets up
PreambleWho adopts the Constitution and for what purposes. It gives no power, but it says where authority comes from.
Article IThe legislative branch: Congress, its two houses, how a bill becomes law, and the subjects it may make laws about.
Article IIThe executive branch: the presidency, how it is elected, its powers and its limits.
Article IIIThe judicial branch: the Supreme Court and the lower courts Congress decides to create.
Article IVRelations among the states and with the Union: respecting each other’s records and admitting new states.
Article VHow to amend the Constitution. Hard on purpose, but not impossible.
Article VIThe supremacy clause, earlier debts, and the oath of office.
Article VIIHow many states had to ratify it for it to take effect: nine of thirteen.
AmendmentsThe first ten, from 1791, are the Bill of Rights. There are 27 in all today.

A trick so you do not mix up the articles

One, two, and three go in the order in which a government acts: first the law is made (I, Congress), then it is carried out (II, the President), then it is judged (III, the courts).

📜 The opening words of each of the first three articles

Document
Constitution of the United States
Date
Signed September 17, 1787; in effect 1789
Kind of source
Primary · founding document. The English text is the legal one; any translation is only a study aid.

Article I, Section 1

All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States, which shall consist of a Senate and House of Representatives.

Article II, Section 1

The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America.

Article III, Section 1

The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish.

2The five principles and where each one lives

The principles are not a summary of the document: they are mechanisms, and each one has an address in the text. Knowing the address is what the test rewards.

The five principles and where each one lives
PrincipleWhat it meansWhere it is
Popular sovereigntyAuthority comes from the people, not from the government or a king.The first three words of the Preamble: “We the People.”
Limited governmentThe government can do only what it is granted; the rest is closed to it.The list of powers in Article I, and later the Tenth Amendment.
Separation of powersThree jobs in three different hands.The first sentence of Articles I, II, and III, each placing one power in one body.
Checks and balancesEach branch can stop the other two.Spread out: the veto in Article I; impeachment and confirming appointments in Articles I and II.
FederalismPower is divided between the national government and the states.The supremacy clause in Article VI, and in the other direction the Tenth Amendment.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Each arrow is a brake. The design does not aim at speed: it aims at making sure no branch can act fully without at least the silent agreement of the others. That governing is slow is the intention, not a defect.

A principle that is not written down

The power of the courts to strike down a law as unconstitutional (judicial review) is usually counted among the checks. Look for it in the text and you will not find it: the Constitution grants it nowhere. The Supreme Court itself established it in 1803, in the case Marbury v. Madison. It is a good reminder that a constitution is what it says plus what has been done with it, and that it is worth telling the two apart.

3Two visions of the republic

Once the Constitution was written, it had to be ratified (approved by the states), and it was not at all clear that it would be. In 1787 and 1788 one of the most serious political debates ever held in writing took place in the newspapers. On one side, the Federalists, who wanted to ratify. On the other, the Anti-Federalists, who thought it dangerous.

The key to the debate

It is usually told as good guys against bad guys, depending on who tells it. That is a mistake. Both sides wanted the same thing: a free and lasting republic. They disagreed about what threatened it most. The Federalist looked around and saw disorder: quarreling states, unpaid debts, a government unable to act. The Anti-Federalist looked at the document and saw a distant government with open-ended powers. Each feared a real and different danger.

📜 The Federalist Papers

Author
James Madison, signing as “Publius”
Date
1787–1788
Published in
New York newspapers, to win support for ratification
Kind of source
Primary · persuasive political essay

His fear: disorder. A government that is too weak cannot hold the union together, and factions end up devouring the republic.

Federalist No. 51

If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary.

Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.

Federalist No. 10

Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens.

📜 “Brutus,” first essay

Author
Unknown, signing as “Brutus.” Historians have pointed to Robert Yates and to Melancton Smith, both of New York; it is not settled.
Date
October 18, 1787
Published in
New York Journal
Kind of source
Primary · persuasive political essay

His fear: tyranny. He also warned that the “necessary and proper” clause, together with the supremacy clause, would let the national government decide its own limits. A distant government with powers written in open terms would end up swallowing the states.

In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar. If this be not the case, there will be a constant clashing of opinions.

History furnishes no example of a free republic, any thing like the extent of the United States.

Notice something remarkable: both sides quote Montesquieu, and both are right to do so. The Anti-Federalist takes his idea that liberty survives only in small republics. The Federalist takes his division of powers and answers that in a large and varied republic, no faction can become a majority. They look at the same country, lean on the same author, and reach opposite conclusions.

Which constitutional mechanism answers each fear
FearWhoseThe mechanism that answers it
The union falls apart from weaknessFederalistThe Union’s own powers and the supremacy clause of Article VI
One branch takes over everythingAnti-FederalistSeparation of powers and checks among the three
The national government swallows the statesAnti-FederalistThe Tenth Amendment: what is not delegated stays with the states
The government tramples individual rightsAnti-FederalistThe Bill of Rights, added in 1791 to win ratification

Who won?

The Federalists won the vote: the Constitution was ratified. But the Anti-Federalists won the first ten amendments, which were not in the 1787 text and were added because they would not accept it without guarantees. The Bill of Rights, today the most quoted part of the document, exists because the losing side did not give up.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The map of the Constitution (25 minutes). Open the Constitution at archives.gov and write one line for each article saying what it sets up, without looking at the table in section 1. Check: compare your lines with the table; if you mixed up I, II, and III, remember: make, carry out, judge.
  • The addresses of the principles (25 minutes). For each of the five principles, copy the exact words of the Constitution where it lives. Check: if you cannot find judicial review, that is the right answer: it is not in the text.
  • Both sides fairly (30 minutes). Write one paragraph that a Federalist would sign and one that an Anti-Federalist would sign, each stating the other side’s fear in a way the other side would accept. Check: if one of the two sounds foolish, rewrite it: both fears were real.

📘Key words

Ratify
To formally approve; the states ratified the Constitution in 1787–1788.
Popular sovereignty
The principle that authority comes from the people.
Limited government
Government has only the powers it is granted.
Checks and balances
Each branch can limit the others (veto, override, impeachment, confirmation, judicial review).
Judicial review
The power of courts to strike down laws that violate the Constitution; set by Marbury v. Madison, 1803.
Federalists / Anti-Federalists
Supporters and opponents of ratifying the Constitution.
Bill of Rights
The first ten amendments, ratified in 1791.
