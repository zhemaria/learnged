The notice and the narrative: Harriet Jacobs

Double document · Newspaper notice of 1835 and memoir published in 1861 · Edenton, North Carolina
Course plan·Source archive·Document 14

Document record

What it contains
Two texts about the same person and the same events, written by the two sides twenty-six years apart
Text A
Runaway notice signed by James Norcom. American Beacon, Norfolk, Virginia, July 4, 1835
Text B
Passages of Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl. Written by Herself, by Harriet Ann Jacobs, published in Boston in 1861 under the pen name Linda Brent and edited by Lydia Maria Child
Who each one is
Harriet Jacobs (1813–1897), born enslaved in Edenton, a seamstress, later a writer and relief worker. James Norcom, a doctor in Edenton, the man who held her as property
Legal status
Both texts are in the public domain. The notice is from 1835; the book was published in 1861 and its author died in 1897
Checked
The notice, on August 31, 2026, against the transcription published by WGBH from the copy of the American Beacon kept by the North Carolina Division of Archives and History. The book passages, against several matching transcriptions of the 1861 edition
Repositories
Notice: WGBH · Africans in America. Book: Documenting the American South · University of North Carolina

What this document is about

The archive usually pairs two sources on one event: The Public and the Street Railway Journal in Documents 4 and 5; Madison and “Brutus” in 7 and 8; the amendments and Colby’s testimony in 12 and 13. This is the most extreme case of all, because the two sources are not about one event: they are about one person, and one of the two is her.

In June 1835 Harriet Jacobs, twenty-one years old, left. Norcom published a notice offering one hundred dollars for her capture. Twenty-six years later, free and in the North, she published a book that tells the same story.

Side by side, the notice and the book do not contradict each other on the facts that can be checked. They agree on who she is, where she was, and what she is said to have done. They differ on something harder to see and more important: what counts as a fact.

Content warning. Text B is about the sexual harassment Jacobs suffered from her owner from the age of fifteen. She tells it with restraint and without explicit detail, and it appears here that way, without cuts and without additions. It is the reason she fled, and the notice says there was none.

Text A · The notice

$100 REWARD. Will be given for the apprehension and delivery of my Servant Girl HARRIET.

[A description follows: she is 21 years old, about five feet four inches tall, light-skinned; she speaks easily, is a good seamstress, and is used to dressing well.]

As this girl absconded from the plantation of my son without any known cause or provocation, it is probable she designs to transport herself to the North. […]

All persons are hereby forewarned against harboring or entertaining her, or being in any way instrumental in her escape, under the most rigorous penalties of the law. JAMES NORCOM.

Text B · The narrative

Chapter V

But I now entered on my fifteenth year—a sad epoch in the life of a slave girl. My master began to whisper foul words in my ear. […] I turned from him with disgust and hatred. But he was my master.

Chapter XXI

The garret was only nine feet long and seven wide. The highest part was three feet high, and sloped down abruptly to the loose board floor. There was no admission for either light or air. […] Southerners have the habit of stopping and talking in the streets, and I heard many conversations not intended to meet my ears.

The exact words

The noticeAs this girl absconded from the plantation of my son without any known cause or provocation, it is probable she designs to transport herself to the North.
The causeMy master began to whisper foul words in my ear. […] I turned from him with disgust and hatred. But he was my master.
“The North”The garret was only nine feet long and seven wide.
Notice the date. The notice appeared on July 4, the anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. There is no sign Norcom chose it on purpose: notices ran on whatever day came. That the coincidence was accidental does not make it any less readable.

The two texts, point by point

What each text records about the same person.
PointText A · Norcom, 1835Text B · Jacobs, 1861
Who she is“My servant.” Height, build, hair, voice, bearing, clothing.A woman with a grandmother, a brother, two children, and a name.
Why she left“Without any known cause or provocation.”Seven years of harassment by the man who owned her, and the threat that her children would meet the same fate.
Where she is“It is probable she designs to transport herself to the North.”In a garret nine feet by seven, in the same town, for seven years.
What the reader is askedTo capture her, and that no one help her, under penalty of law.To believe her. The book is addressed to the white women of the North.
What counts as a factPhysical marks and clothing, because they serve to identify property.The motive, because it is what turns a flight into a decision.
Neither one lies about the facts that can be checked. That is the hard point, and the one to take away. The notice does not invent her height or age; it describes precisely the person it hunts. Jacobs does not deny leaving or being a seamstress. The difference is not in the facts: it is in what was thought worth writing down and what was not. A source can be accurate in everything it says and still leave out the one thing that explains what it tells.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That the flight of an enslaved person was pursued with a public machinery: newspapers, rewards, jails across the country, and legal threats against anyone who helped.
  • That the language of these notices is the language of an inventory: it describes a person by marks and clothing because it treats her as recoverable property.
  • That the claim “without any known cause or provocation” can be refuted with the person’s own source, and that this is why both are needed.
  • That a source can be accurate and at the same time systematically incomplete, and that the omission is not carelessness but a result of its point of view.

It cannot support

  • That this was the usual case. Jacobs could read and write, had free family in the town, and managed to publish a book. Thousands of people in her situation left no text, and the absence of a source is not the absence of a history.
  • That the book is a sworn record. It was written twenty-six years later, with pen names, changed names, and the editorial help of Lydia Maria Child, with the stated aim of convincing a particular audience. That it is abolitionist writing does not make it false; it means reading it knowing what it was written for.
  • What Norcom thought. The notice records what he chose to make public, not what he knew. He knew perfectly well the cause he says he does not know.
  • The figure of seven years with no support but the book. The author gives it herself, and later research has upheld it, but the source of the claim is her own.
