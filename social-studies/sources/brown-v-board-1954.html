What this document is about

In 1896, in Plessy v. Ferguson, the Supreme Court had held that separating the races did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment if the facilities were equal. That doctrine was called “separate but equal,” and it held up segregation legally for almost sixty years.

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People did not attack that doctrine head on and all at once. For twenty years it brought cases about graduate and law schools, where the material inequality could not be denied, and won decisions that tightened the circle. Brown is the end of that campaign, and that is why it is in the archive as the document of the litigation strategy: it did not fall from the sky; it was the result of a tactic kept up for two decades.

The lawyer who argued it before the Court, Thurgood Marshall, would become thirteen years later the first Black justice of that same Court.

The opinion, as Lesson 6.3 quotes it

We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of “separate but equal” has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.

The exact words

The conclusionWe conclude that in the field of public education the doctrine of "separate but equal" has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.
The harm…generates a feeling of inferiority as to their status in the community that may affect their hearts and minds in a way unlikely ever to be undone.
The pace…with all deliberate speed.

Four things the decision did not do

This is the part to read slowly, because Brown is almost always summed up as the end of segregation, and it was not.

The stated reach of the decision and what lay outside it.
What it didWhat it did not do
It declared segregation in public schools unconstitutional.It did not touch buses, restaurants, hotels, hospitals, or jobs. The sentence itself says “in the field of public education.” For the rest, the laws of 1964 and 1965 would be needed.
It struck down “separate but equal” in that field.It set no deadline. Brown II left it at “with all deliberate speed,” and a decade later most districts in the Deep South had still not integrated.
It rested its argument on the social research of the time.That support became the flank it was attacked on: if the harm was proved by studies, critics said, it was enough to dispute the studies.
It created a precedent to hold on to in lower courts.It brought no federal mechanism to enforce it. Each district had to be sued one by one, and suing costs money, time, and lawyers.
Why this matters for the lesson. Lesson 6.3 compares four strategies of the civil rights movement. Brown is proof of what litigation can achieve: it changes the law of the whole country at once, and unanimously. It is also proof of its limit: a decision declares a right and does not build the machinery that delivers it. That would come ten years later, and by another road.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That since May 17, 1954, school segregation by law has been unconstitutional across the country, and that the decision was unanimous.
  • That the Court expressly rejected the doctrine of Plessy in the field of public education, in those words.
  • That the argument rests on the harm done, not only on the material inequality of the facilities.
  • That the 1954 decision contained no instructions for carrying it out, and that the 1955 decision set a deliberately vague pace.

It cannot support

  • That the schools were integrated. A decision says what is illegal; what happened afterward is a question of data and other sources.
  • That segregation in general ended. The text itself limits its reach to public education.
  • That public opinion changed. The document records the judgment of nine justices, not of the country.
  • That litigation is the most effective strategy. To claim that, you would have to compare it with the other three, which is exactly what the lesson does.
