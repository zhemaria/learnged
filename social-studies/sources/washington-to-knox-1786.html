What this document is about

In the fall of 1786, indebted farmers in western Massachusetts (many of them war veterans who had never been paid) forced the closing of the courts that were seizing their property. The movement took the name of one of its leaders, Daniel Shays. The Confederation government had no way to step in: it could not collect taxes, had no army of its own, and could not force the states to do anything.

Washington had been retired at Mount Vernon for three years, with no office, following events through Knox’s letters and through newspapers he did not trust. This letter is what he wrote the night after receiving fresh news. It is not a speech or a report: it is an alarmed man writing to a friend, and what he says about his country’s government he says with no audience.

That is why it is in the archive. The Articles of Confederation failed: any textbook says so. What this letter adds is how the failure felt from inside, five months before the Philadelphia Convention met, from the man who would preside over it.

The letter, as Lesson 6.1 quotes it

In this, as in most other matter[s], we are too slow. When this spirit first dawned, probably it migh[t] easily have been checked; but it is scarcely within the reach of human ken, at this moment, to say when—where—or how it will end. There are combustibles in every State, which a spark may set fire to. […]

[…] apropos, what prevented the Eastern states from attending the September meeting at Annapolis? […] What are the prevailing sentiments of the one now proposed to be held at Philadelphia, in May next? […]

If the powers are inadequate amend or alter them, but do not let us sink into the lowest state of humiliation & contempt, and become a byword in all the earth. […]

I ask this question for information, I know nothing of the facts.

The complete letter is in Founders Online, the National Archives’ edition of the papers of the founders.

The exact words

Three passages are quoted word for word in Lesson 6.1. The first is the image that became famous; the second is the diagnosis of the institutions; the third is the admission that sets the limit of the source.

The imageThere are combustibles in every State, which a spark may set fire to.
The diagnosisIf the powers are inadequate amend or alter them.
The limitI ask this question for information, I know nothing of the facts.
The third sentence is the most valuable of the three. Washington writes in alarm about a rebellion three hundred miles away, and he himself warns that he does not know what happened. Everything he says about the rebels he knows from Knox, and Knox is not neutral either. When a source states its own limit, take it at its word.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That in December 1786 leading figures in the country believed the Confederation government lacked enough energy, and said so in private.
  • That the Philadelphia convention of May 1787 was already being discussed by that date, and that people were unsure who would attend.
  • That the Annapolis meeting of September 1786 had been poorly attended, and that this was a worry.
  • That Washington feared both internal disorder and Britain taking advantage of it, and joined both fears in one argument for reforming the powers of government.

It cannot support

  • What the Massachusetts farmers did, asked for, or thought. The letter never quotes them; it calls them “insurgents” and charges them with depravity. For that you need their own sources.
  • That the rebellion caused the Constitution. The letter proves the alarm and the call for a convention existed; an event strengthening an argument is not the same as causing it.
  • That this was the general feeling of the country. It is a great landowner writing to a Secretary at War: it records the view of one group, and one with a lot to lose from unpaid debts.
  • That Britain was in fact plotting as he claims. Washington takes it as certain without offering proof; a strong suspicion is still a suspicion.
