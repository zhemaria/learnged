What this document is about

The National Archives put it in one line worth a whole chapter: the Voting Rights Act is an act to enforce the Fifteenth Amendment, signed ninety-five years after that amendment was ratified.

That is why these two laws are in the archive. They invented no new right. The Fifteenth Amendment had forbidden denying the vote on account of race since 1870, and Brown had declared school segregation unconstitutional in 1954. What was missing in both cases was the same thing: who enforces it, with what, and at whose cost.

Document 13 showed where the gap was in 1871: no official turned Abram Colby away. Document 15 showed it again in 1954: a decision declares a right and leaves each family the job of suing its district. These two laws are the attempt to close that gap with two concrete tools: federal money and advance approval.

The two laws, as Lesson 6.3 quotes them

Title VI, 1964

No person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.

Section 5, 1965

[A covered State wishing to change a voting rule] may institute an action in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia for a declaratory judgment that such qualification, prerequisite, standard, practice, or procedure does not have the purpose and will not have the effect of denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race or color…

The exact words

Title IIAll persons shall be entitled to the full and equal enjoyment of the goods, services, facilities, privileges, advantages, and accommodations of any place of public accommodation…
Title VI…or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.
Title of the 1965 actAn act to enforce the fifteenth amendment to the Constitution of the United States, and for other purposes.

The missing machine

This table sums up the whole of Topic 6. Read it from top to bottom.

From 1870 to 1965: what each step declared and how it was enforced.
YearWhat it declaresHow it is enforced
1870 · Amendment XVThe vote may not be denied on account of race.Nothing, unless Congress legislates. The penalty in Amendment XIV was never applied.
1871 · Colby’s testimony—It shows the gap: the violence was private, and the amendment binds only governments.
1954 · BrownSchool segregation is unconstitutional.Suing district by district, with no deadline. Ten years later, much of the Deep South had still not integrated.
1964 · Title VI—Money. A district that segregates loses federal funding. It no longer has to be sued: it is enough to stop paying it.
1965 · Sections 4 and 5—Advance approval and the federal examiner. A covered state may not change a voting rule without prior approval, and the burden of proving it does not discriminate is the state’s.
What changes between 1954 and 1965 is not the right: it is who carries the work. Before, anyone who wanted to exercise a right had to sue, pay lawyers, and wait years. After, it is the state that has to ask permission and prove its rule does not discriminate. That shift of the burden is the practical difference between a right declared and a right you can use, and it is probably the most useful idea a student takes from this topic.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • What became illegal under federal law, and from what exact date, in places open to the public, in employment, and in programs paid for with federal money.
  • That the 1965 act presents itself, in its title, as a law to enforce an amendment ninety-five years older.
  • That lawmakers chose concrete tools: the funding condition, the federal examiner, and advance approval.
  • That the ban on sex discrimination in employment entered the law through an amendment offered on the House floor.

It cannot support

  • That discrimination ended. A law says what is illegal; measuring what happened afterward takes data, and those data are another source.
  • That these laws were the work of one person or one strategy. They came after a thirty-year court campaign, years of mobilization, and a long and contested legislative fight.
  • That they still apply today as written. In 2013, in Shelby County v. Holder, the Supreme Court struck down the coverage formula of Section 4 because it relied on data decades old. Section 5 is still in the code, but with no formula saying whom it applies to, and only Congress can write a new one. It is a politically disputed question today, and this course does not settle it: it records what the Court decided and its effect on the text.
  • What citizens thought. These are laws passed by a Congress, with documented opposition, including a long filibuster in the Senate.
