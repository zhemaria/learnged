What this document is about

On March 18, 1766, Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. In the colonies there were bells, bonfires, and toasts to the king. That same day, the same Parliament passed this second, much shorter law, which almost no one celebrated because almost no one paid attention to it.

Its content fits in one sentence: Parliament drops the tax and keeps the whole right to impose it. Not only the right to tax: the right to make laws for America in all cases whatsoever, with no exception stated.

That is why it is paired with Document 9. If you study only the repeal, 1766 looks like a happy ending and the Revolution ten years later has no explanation. Read the two laws of the same day and you see something else: the immediate conflict (the money) was settled, and the underlying conflict (the power) was left untouched. It is, in pure form, the difference between an immediate cause and a structural cause that Lesson 6.1 works on.

The act, as Lesson 6.1 quotes it

…That the said colonies and plantations in America have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain; and that the King's majesty, by and with the advice and consent of the lords spiritual and temporal, and commons of Great Britain, in parliament assembled, had, hath, and of right ought to have, full power and authority to make laws and statutes of sufficient force and validity to bind the colonies and people of America, subjects of the crown of Great Britain, in all cases whatsoever.

II. …That all resolutions, votes, orders, and proceedings, in any of the said colonies or plantations, whereby the power and authority of the parliament of Great Britain, to make laws and statutes as aforesaid, is denied, or drawn into question, are, and are hereby declared to be, utterly null and void to all intents and purposes whatsoever.

The exact words

Two passages are quoted word for word in Lesson 6.1. The first, because its formula comes back ten years later with the subject reversed. The second, because it cancels Document 9, and any text like it, in advance.

The declaratory clause…have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain… in all cases whatsoever.
Section II…are, and are hereby declared to be, utterly null and void to all intents and purposes whatsoever.
An echo worth hearing. “Have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate.” Compare it with the Declaration of Independence (Document 6): “are, and of Right ought to be Free and Independent States.” The same English legal formula, with the subject reversed, ten years later. Whoever wrote 1776 knew 1766 perfectly well and was answering it in its own words.

How to read the two documents together

The two texts of 1765 and 1766, point by point.
Point in disputeDocument 9 · Colonists, October 1765Document 10 · Parliament, March 1766
Who may taxOnly the colonial legislatures, elected by the colonists themselves.Parliament, with no limit stated.
What the argument is aboutA constitutional right of English subjects.The dependence of some territories on the crown.
What is done about the taxRepeal is requested by loyal petition.It is repealed, in a separate law of the same day.
What happens to the principleIt is claimed that Parliament does not have it.It is claimed that Parliament has all of it, and texts that deny it are declared void.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That Parliament gave way on the tax without giving way on the principle, and did so deliberately and in writing on the same day.
  • That by 1766 the dispute was already about sovereignty, not accounting.
  • That London considered void any colonial resolution that denied its power to legislate, including those of Document 9.
  • That there was a structural cause of conflict that the repeal of the Stamp Act did not touch, and that was therefore still available to flare up again.

It cannot support

  • That this law caused the Revolution. Between 1766 and 1775 there were other laws, other events, and other decisions; a structural cause makes an outcome possible, it does not produce it alone.
  • That the colonists read it in 1766 as a threat. Most celebrated the repeal; the reaction to this second law was late and uneven.
  • That Parliament acted as one block. The law passed with internal opposition and debate; the text records the result of the vote, not the state of opinion.
  • That “in all cases whatsoever” already meant a plan to tax again. It is a claim of power, and the use of a power cannot be deduced from claiming it.
