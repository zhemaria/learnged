What this document is about

The course needed two opposed, reasoned positions on a specific environmental policy, to learn to weigh arguments by the evidence on each side. Newspaper opinion pieces would do, but they are under copyright and cannot enter this archive. A divided Supreme Court decision, on the other hand, is in the public domain and brings the same thing, better organized: two texts, written at the same time, on the same question, by people who read the same documents and reached opposite conclusions.

The case. Twelve states and several cities asked the federal environmental agency to regulate carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases from cars. The agency refused, arguing that the law did not give it that authority and that, even if it did, using it would not be wise at that time. The states sued.

And here is what makes this document unusual in the archive: the argument it contains is still open today.

Text A · The opinion of the Court

A well-documented rise in global temperatures has coincided with a significant increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Respected scientists believe the two trends are related. […]

Because greenhouse gases fit well within the Clean Air Act’s capacious definition of “air pollutant,” we hold that EPA has the statutory authority to regulate the emission of such gases from new motor vehicles. […]

We hold only that EPA must ground its reasons for action or inaction in the statute.

Text B · The dissent

Global warming may be a “crisis,” even “the most pressing environmental problem of our time.” […] It is not a problem, however, that has escaped the attention of policymakers in the Executive and Legislative Branches of our Government, who continue to consider regulatory, legislative, and treaty-based means of addressing global climate change.

Apparently dissatisfied with the pace of progress on this issue in the elected branches, petitioners have come to the courts claiming broad-ranging injury, and attempting to tie that injury to the Government’s alleged failure to comply with a rather narrow statutory provision. I would reject these challenges as nonjusticiable. Such a conclusion involves no judgment on whether global warming exists, what causes it, or the extent of the problem.

Where they disagree, and where they do not

This table is the reason the document is in the archive. Read it before forming an opinion about the case.

The majority and the dissent, question by question.
QuestionThe majorityThe dissent
Does global warming exist?It treats it as documented and describes the mechanism in its first paragraph.It does not judge, and says expressly that it does not. It even calls it a possible “crisis,” quoting the petitioners.
Could these states sue?Yes. Massachusetts is losing coastal land, and the agency’s refusal contributes to that harm.No. The harm is too general, and its link to the refusal too speculative.
Does the law cover these gases?Yes. The legal definition of air pollutant is very broad and includes them.It does not reach the merits: if the states cannot sue, there is no case to decide.
Who should decide?The Court only requires the agency to ground its decision in the law.Congress and the President, who are already working on the issue. Not the courts.
The disagreement is not the one it seems. It is tempting to read this as “some believe in climate change and some do not.” The text itself says otherwise: Roberts writes that his conclusion involves no judgment on whether global warming exists or what causes it. What they argue about is something else, and harder: who decides. Whether a court can make an agency act on a harm that affects the whole world, or whether that belongs to those who are elected.

What happened next, and why it matters today

The events, in order and without judgment.
DateWhat happened
April 2007The decision requires the agency to decide according to the law and explain its decision.
December 2009The agency issues the endangerment finding: six greenhouse gases endanger public health and welfare. That finding becomes the legal basis of federal climate regulation for sixteen years.
August 2025The agency announces its intention to rescind the 2009 finding and opens a public comment period.
February 12, 2026The agency rescinds the finding and repeals the greenhouse gas emission standards for vehicles. It argues that the law does not give it authority to regulate in order to address global climate change, relying among other arguments on the so-called major questions doctrine. The rule took effect on April 20, 2026.
Since thenThe rescission has been challenged in court by states and health and environmental groups. Members of Congress are on both sides, and organizations on both sides have published opposing legal analyses.
What makes the document unusual, and a warning. The question that divided the five from the four in 2007 (what exactly this law authorizes) is the same one on the table now. A student can read the two opinions of 2007 and recognize the same arguments in this year’s news. The warning: this is a politically disputed question, and it is moving. This record states what the Court decided, what each text said, and what has happened since; it takes no side and does not ask you to. Before using the current state of things in your work, check it: it may have changed since the date this record was checked.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That in 2007 the Supreme Court held, five votes to four, that greenhouse gases fall within the legal definition of air pollutant.
  • That the Court did not order regulation: it ordered the agency to ground its decision in the law and explain it.
  • That the dissent rests on the right to sue and on who has the power to decide, and states expressly that it makes no judgment on whether global warming exists.
  • That two equally prepared readers can read the same legal text and reach opposite conclusions, both of them reasoned.

It cannot support

  • What is true about the climate. A court decision settles a legal question; scientific evidence is weighed with scientific sources.
  • What the justices really believe. The text records the legal grounds they give, not their personal convictions.
  • Which of the two positions is right. The archive publishes both and does not referee. Whoever wants to decide must do it with the texts in front of them, knowing which argument they are accepting.
  • What the rule is today. The situation has changed several times since 2007 and is still moving.
