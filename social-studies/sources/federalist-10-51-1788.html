What this document is about

Once the Constitution was written in Philadelphia in 1787, nine states had to ratify it. New York was decisive and far from decided. Between October 1787 and August 1788, three supporters of ratification published eighty-five essays in New York newspapers, signed “Publius.”

Keep this in mind before reading a single line: these are campaign texts. They were written to win a vote. That does not make them false or less valuable (they are among the best things ever written about government), but it explains what they leave out and what they stress. Lesson 1.2 called it purpose, and here it is stated openly.

In No. 10, Madison answers his opponents’ most serious objection, that a large republic cannot be free, and turns the argument around. In No. 51, he explains why the Constitution divides power as it does, resting on a view of human nature he does not dress up.

This record holds long excerpts, not the complete essays. It is the archive’s only exception to the rule of keeping the whole document, and it is stated here. The ratification essays are long, and much of each one is preamble and courtesy addressed to a 1787 newspaper reader. The core of the argument is given; the full English text is linked above, and what is left out is transition, not argument.

The passages Lesson 3.2 quotes

Federalist No. 51

If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary.

Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.

Federalist No. 10

Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens.

More of the core argument

Federalist No. 10 · What a faction is

By a faction, I understand a number of citizens, whether amounting to a majority or a minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adversed to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community.

There are two methods of curing the mischiefs of faction: the one, by removing its causes; the other, by controlling its effects.

Federalist No. 51 · Why checks are needed

But the great security against a gradual concentration of the several powers in the same department, consists in giving to those who administer each department the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the others.

Federalist No. 51 · The double security of federalism

In the compound republic of America, the power surrendered by the people is first divided between two distinct governments, and then the portion allotted to each subdivided among distinct and separate departments. Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people. The different governments will control each other, at the same time that each will be controlled by itself.

The turn to notice. His opponents said a republic survives only if it is small and its people are alike. Madison accepts the diagnosis (factions are unavoidable) and reverses the remedy: precisely because they are unavoidable, a large and varied territory is better, where none can become a majority. The same fact, read the other way.
How to read it with its pair. This document and the essays of “Brutus” were written in the same city, in the same months, for the same readers. Read separately, each one convinces. Read together, you see they are arguing about a fact neither can prove: what happens to a republic when it becomes very large.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • What reasons the supporters of ratification gave in public, and with what arguments they hoped to convince.
  • How the authors understood the design of the Constitution in 1788.
  • That the objection about a large territory was serious enough to deserve a whole essay.

It cannot support

  • That those were the reasons of everyone who voted yes. It is the voice of three men, not of a whole side.
  • What a clause means today. A campaign text is not an authoritative interpretation, even when courts quote it.
  • That Madison’s answer was right. Later history has not settled that debate.
