What this document is about

In March 1765 the British Parliament passed the Stamp Act: a tax on almost all paper used for legal and business purposes in the American colonies. Newspapers, contracts, licenses, deeds, playing cards. It was the first internal tax London placed directly on the colonists, without going through the colonial assemblies.

The reaction came in two very different forms, and they should not be confused. In the streets, the Sons of Liberty burned effigies, broke into tax collectors’ houses, and made sure almost none of them dared to serve. In a room in New York, in October, delegates from nine of the thirteen colonies did something else: they wrote a polite, legal, numbered document that begins by declaring loyalty to the king and subordination to Parliament, and then holds that this same Parliament cannot tax them.

That is what makes the text useful for this lesson. The protest of 1765 is not made in the name of independence (no one asks for it, and several delegates would have called it treason) but in the name of a right the colonists believe they have as British subjects. The grievance is not the amount of money: it is who has the power to ask for it.

The resolutions, as Lesson 6.1 quotes them

1st. That his majesty's subjects in these colonies, owe the same allegiance to the crown of Great Britain that is owing from his subjects born within the realm, and all due subordination to that august body, the parliament of Great Britain.

3d. That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.

4th. That the people of these colonies are not, and from their local circumstances cannot be, represented in the House of Commons in Great-Britain.

The exact words

Lesson 6.1 works on the exact wording of three passages, because in them lies the difference between what bothers the colonists and what they claim.

Resolution 3That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.
Resolution 4That the people of these colonies are not, and from their local circumstances cannot be, represented in the House of Commons in Great-Britain.
Resolution 1…and all due subordination to that august body the Parliament of Great Britain.

Read them together and the tension that shapes the next ten years appears. Resolution 1 grants Parliament “all due subordination.” Resolutions 3 and 4 take away its power to tax. The document does not say where the line between the two lies, and that undrawn line is exactly what Parliament will draw on its own five months later, in Document 10.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That in 1765 there was already a coordinated intercolonial protest, able to meet and write a common text.
  • That the protest was framed in constitutional terms: the argument is about who has the power to tax, not how much is collected.
  • That the protesters still presented themselves as loyal British subjects and sought repeal by petition.
  • That the colonists were also worried about the reach of the admiralty courts, which tried cases without a jury. The legal grievance goes with the tax grievance.

It cannot support

  • That there was any intention of independence in 1765. The text says the opposite explicitly; reading it that way is reading 1776 into 1765.
  • That these fourteen resolutions expressed the opinion of “the colonists.” Nine of thirteen colonies attended, the delegates did not represent people without a vote or property, and not all signed.
  • That the protest was only of this kind. While this polite text was being drafted, other people were burning effigies in the street. A source written by delegates records well what the delegates did and poorly what the crowd did.
  • That the repeal of 1766 was due to this document. The text is a petition, not a proven cause.
A catalog warning. The Avalon Project titles this page “Resolutions of the Continental Congress October 19, 1765.” The name is misleading: the Continental Congress did not exist until 1774. The 1765 assembly is known as the Stamp Act Congress. The text Avalon offers is reliable; the title of its page is not. A useful reminder that an archive’s label is also a claim worth checking.
