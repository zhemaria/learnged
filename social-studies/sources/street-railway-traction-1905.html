What this document is about

The Street Railway Journal was not a city newspaper or a party organ. It was the industry’s professional magazine: it was read by the managers, engineers, and investors of streetcar companies across the country, and it was paid for by its suppliers’ advertising. The same issue carries stock quotations for streetcar companies, the annual report of a Philadelphia company, and news about the car-wheel business.

That does not mean it lies. It means it writes for a particular reader who wants to know, above all, whether his investment is in danger. The question behind the note is not “what is good for Chicago?” but “how is the franchise negotiation going?”

Why this document is in the archive. The two weeklies in this file came out the same day, September 23, 1905, and tell the same session of the Chicago City Council’s transportation committee, held on Friday the 15th. They contradict each other on no fact that can be checked. They differ in which facts they choose to tell.

The document: the session of September 15

Page 467

Chicago company to reply to questions propounded by the City Council.

The meeting of the committee on local transportation of the Chicago City Council on Friday, Sept. 15, ended in the traction companies agreeing to answer on Sept. 26 several questions submitted by aldermen. The tangible results of the meeting were two offers, made by the Chicago City Company, as a part of what it would agree to do in order to secure an extension of franchise. While the Union Traction representatives did not concur in these offers, they sat silent when the one involving them was made. They are understood to be bound by it. The two offers are:

An exchange of transfers between the two companies outside of a fixed zone and a through routing of cars inside of that zone, so as to make a case of "one city, one fare."

An immediate rehabilitation of the service of the company on the lines of the recommendations in the Arnold report.

The important questions which the company has agreed to answer at the next meeting of the committee are:

In any settlement agreement which may be made will it be provided that at a determined period, or periods, during the life of the agreement the city may take over your lines and the price and method of fixing the same be therein set forth?

Will you agree to the city taking over the lines at any time during the life of the franchise, if one is granted?

The other questions are all based on the report of the committee on Dec. 11, 1901, when it outlined the terms of what it then considered a proper franchise ordinance. This report deals with requirements calling for good service on the part of the companies, and then provides for universal transfers; that the city can take over the properties at any time after the first ten years; that all grants terminate at the same time; that the underground trolley be used in the territory bounded by Ashland and North Avenues and Twenty-Second Street, and that the companies operate in subways if the city ever builds them.

Transcription note: the original says “Chicago City Company.” The company was the Chicago City Railway Company; the original’s short form is kept. The “Arnold report” is the technical study of Chicago’s streetcars that engineer Bion J. Arnold delivered to the city in 1902.

The finding that opens Lesson 1.3. The two documents in this file agree on every fact they both mention. The whole difference lies in what each chooses to mention and with what verbs. Where one writes that there was no progress at all, the other writes that there were tangible results. Neither one lies. It is the same meeting.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • That the meeting of September 15 ended with the companies promising to answer several of the aldermen’s questions on the 26th.
  • That the Chicago City Company made two concrete offers: transfers for a single fare, and rehabilitation of service according to the Arnold report.
  • That the representatives of Union Traction did not join the offers and stayed silent.
  • The content of the pending questions and their origin in the committee’s 1901 report.

It cannot support

  • That the companies would really answer, or what they would answer. The document was published before that date.
  • That the offers were enough, or that the committee saw them as progress. The magazine is the one calling them “tangible results.”
  • That Union Traction was bound by them. The document says “it is understood that,” which is an interpretation, not a fact.
  • Anything else that happened at that session: it mentions no refusal, no vote, and no spokesman by name.
