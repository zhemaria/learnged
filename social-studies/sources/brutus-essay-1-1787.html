What this document is about

Nine days before the first number of The Federalist appeared, someone signing “Brutus” began publishing in the New York Journal the most serious criticism the Constitution received. The pen name is no accident: it recalls the Roman who took part in killing Caesar to stop him from ending the republic.

His essays forced the Federalists to explain themselves better. And although his side lost the vote, it won something: the Bill of Rights exists because this side would not accept ratification without written guarantees.

The sharpest part of the essay is not philosophical: it is a technical reading of the proposed text. “Brutus” points to two clauses (necessary and proper, and supremacy) and predicts what will be done with them.

Who wrote this: we do not know for certain. For a long time it was credited to Robert Yates, a New York judge who attended the Philadelphia Convention and left unhappy. Later studies, including analysis of style, have pointed to Melancton Smith, and in 2025 documentary evidence appeared that strengthens that second attribution. Other proposals name John Williams. The question is not settled. This record says so instead of choosing a name, because establishing where a document comes from is part of reading it, and sometimes the honest answer is that its origin is not established.
This record holds excerpts, not the complete essay. The full English text is linked above.

The passages Lesson 3.2 quotes

In a republic, the manners, sentiments, and interests of the people should be similar. If this be not the case, there will be a constant clashing of opinions.

History furnishes no example of a free republic, any thing like the extent of the United States.

The phrase to underline. That the federal government would end up judging for itself the extent of its own powers is this document’s concrete prediction. Lesson 3.3 works on exactly that border, two hundred thirty-eight years later, and the argument is still open. The point is not whether he was right, but that the question he asked is still the question.
How to read it with its pair. “Brutus” and The Federalist both invoke Montesquieu, and both with reason: one his claim that liberty survives only in small republics, the other his division of powers. Same author, same country, same months, opposite conclusions. Neither one lies.

What this document supports, and what it does not

It can support

  • What specific objections were made to the Constitution before it was ratified, and about which clauses.
  • That the Anti-Federalist criticism was technical and not only emotional: it read the text and pointed to clauses.
  • That the tension between federal supremacy and state powers was foreseen from the very start.

It cannot support

  • Who made them. The author is not established, and that must be said every time it is quoted.
  • That all Anti-Federalists thought this way. They were a mixed group, united more by what they rejected than by a common program.
  • That the prediction came true. That is a historical and political argument, not a fact this document proves.
