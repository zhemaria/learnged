Source archive

Real public-domain documents, each with its record, its limits, and a link to the original
Course plan·Source archive

What this archive is

The documents in the lessons of this course were not written to teach. They are real sources: letters, reports, speeches, laws, court decisions, and newspaper pages, produced at the time by people who wanted something. That is the material the GED® Social Studies test works with, and so it is the material used here.

Each document is kept here with its full record and a link to the original in the repository that holds it. In the lessons you see only the working excerpt; here you see where it comes from. The Spanish version of the archive adds the course’s own translation of each document.

How a document gets into the archive

  • Public domain, checked. The legal status of each document is verified, and the record says on what basis and on what date.
  • Not adapted. Irregular spelling and offensive language of the time are kept, because they are part of the evidence.
  • Checkable against the original. Every record says where the original is. When the document is rare, its text is reproduced here; when an official transcription exists online (as with the founding documents), the archive links to it instead of copying it, because the institution that keeps the document checks it better than a copy would.
  • With its limit stated. Each record says what can be supported with the document and what cannot. An archive that only says what a source proves teaches half the job.

What cannot be here, and why

This archive publishes only documents whose public-domain status is confirmed, and some important sources of the course do not have it. The clearest case is Martin Luther King, Jr. The Letter from Birmingham Jail (April 16, 1963) and the speech of August 28, 1963, in Washington are still under copyright, managed by the King estate through a licensing company. Works published in the United States in 1963 with the required formalities are protected for ninety-five years from publication. The King Institute at Stanford University itself states that it cannot grant permission to reproduce any of his writings.

So Lesson 6.3 works with civil disobedience as a strategy through public-domain documents that record its effects (court decisions, federal laws, and executive orders), and points you to licensed editions to read King directly: the King Institute’s page on the letter, the digital archive of the King Center in Atlanta, and the book Why We Can’t Wait (1963), which any public library can lend you. Who owns the words of a public figure, and for how long, is a real question that this course leaves open.

Published documents

Letters from migrants, 1917

Document 1 · Primary source · Personal letters · United States, 1916–1917

Eleven letters written by people in the South to the Chicago Defender and the Chicago Urban League while they decided whether to move north. Collected by Emmett J. Scott and published in 1919.

Used in Lesson 1.1.

First days at Hull-House, 1889

Document 2 · Primary source · Published memoir · Chicago, 1889–1890

The whole of Chapter V of Twenty Years at Hull-House, by Jane Addams: the search for a neighborhood, the house on Halsted Street, the description of the Nineteenth Ward, and the first months of the settlement. Written in 1910, twenty-one years after the events.

Used in Lesson 1.2. Paired with Document 3.

Map notes and comments, 1895

Document 3 · Primary source · Published social study · Chicago, 1893–1895

The first chapter of Hull-House Maps and Papers, by Agnes Sinclair Holbrook. The same neighborhood Addams describes, measured house by house by four federal officials in 1893. It explains how the maps of nationalities and wages were made, and states its own errors.

Used in Lesson 1.2. Paired with Document 2.

The City Council questioning, 1905

Document 4 · Primary source · Opinion weekly · Chicago, September 15, 1905

The account in The Public, Louis F. Post’s weekly, of the session in which the City Council’s transportation committee questioned the streetcar companies: the questions of Aldermen Foreman and Finn, the answers of Colonel E. R. Bliss, and two organizations’ support for Mayor Dunne’s plan.

Used in Lesson 1.3. Paired with Document 5.

The same meeting told by the industry, 1905

Document 5 · Primary source · Trade press · Chicago, September 15, 1905

A note in the Street Railway Journal, the trade magazine of the urban railway companies, about the same session of the 15th. It came out the same Saturday as The Public. It contradicts no fact that can be checked: it tells other facts, with other verbs.

Used in Lesson 1.3. Paired with Document 4.

The Declaration of Independence, 1776

Document 6 · Primary source · Founding document · Philadelphia, July 4, 1776

The text in three parts, with the twenty-seven grievances numbered 1 to 27 so they can be cited, and the phrases that come from Locke. The Spanish record warns that the official Spanish translation leaves out the list of grievances.

Used in Lesson 3.1.

The Federalist, Nos. 10 and 51

Document 7 · Primary source · Campaign press · New York, 1787–1788

The two essays by James Madison that Lesson 3.2 uses: No. 10, on why factions are unavoidable and why a large republic controls them better than a small one; and No. 51, on why power must be divided so that it checks itself. Campaign texts, written to win a vote, and the record says so.

Used in Lesson 3.2. Paired with Document 8.

First essay of “Brutus,” 1787

Document 8 · Primary source · Campaign press against ratification · New York, 1787

The most serious Anti-Federalist criticism the Constitution received, published nine days before the first Federalist. It argues that a free republic cannot fit in a huge territory, and predicts that the necessary and proper clause joined to the supremacy clause will let the federal government judge the extent of its own powers. Its author is not established, and the record says so.

Used in Lesson 3.2. Paired with Document 7.

Declaration of Rights and Grievances, 1765

Document 9 · Primary source · Record of an intercolonial protest · New York, October 19, 1765

The fourteen resolutions adopted by the Stamp Act Congress, with delegates from nine of the thirteen colonies. A polite, numbered text that swears loyalty to the king, grants Parliament “all due subordination,” and then denies it the power to tax. The record warns that the Avalon Project mislabels its own page.

Used in Lesson 6.1. Paired with Document 10.

The Declaratory Act, 1766

Document 10 · Primary source · Act of Parliament · London, March 18, 1766

The law Parliament passed the same day it repealed the Stamp Act. It drops the tax and keeps for itself the whole power to make laws for America “in all cases whatsoever.” Its legal formula echoes in the Declaration of Independence ten years later.

Used in Lesson 6.1. Paired with Document 9.

Washington to Knox, 1786

Document 11 · Primary source · Private letter · Mount Vernon, Virginia, December 26, 1786

A letter written from retirement, with no public office, the night after Washington received news of Shays’ Rebellion. It holds his diagnosis of the failure of the Articles of Confederation, the image of combustibles in every state, and a sentence that sets the limit of the source itself: “I know nothing of the facts.”

Used in Lesson 6.1.

The Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth Amendments

Document 12 · Primary source · Constitutional text · United States, 1865–1870

The three Reconstruction amendments, including the sections of the Fourteenth that textbooks usually skip. The record points out the four clauses the usual summary leaves out: the crime exception of the Thirteenth, the word “male,” the penalty for denying the vote that was never applied, and the fact that the Fifteenth does not grant the vote but forbids denying it on three grounds.

Used in Lesson 6.2, Lesson 5.1. Paired with Document 13.

Testimony of Abram Colby, 1871

Document 13 · Primary source · Sworn testimony · Atlanta, October 27, 1871

Abram Colby, born enslaved, a barber, and elected to the Georgia legislature, testifies before the Joint Committee of Congress. He had been offered five thousand dollars to give up his seat. It is the exact pair of Document 12: the amendments say what the law must be; this testimony says what happened when someone tried to use it.

Used in Lesson 6.2. Paired with Document 12.

The notice and the narrative: Harriet Jacobs

Document 14 · Double document · Newspaper notice of 1835 and memoir published in 1861 · Edenton, North Carolina

Two texts about the same person, written by the two sides twenty-six years apart: the runaway notice James Norcom published offering one hundred dollars, and the passages of Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl in which Harriet Jacobs tells the same events from inside. The notice says she left “without any known cause or provocation” and guesses she is heading North; she spent seven years in a garret nine feet by seven, in the same town.

Used in Lesson 6.2.

Brown v. Board of Education, 1954

Document 15 · Primary source · Supreme Court decision · Washington, May 17, 1954

The unanimous opinion that declared that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,” and the sentence of Brown II that set the pace: “with all deliberate speed.” It is the document of the litigation strategy, and its record lists four things the decision did not do, starting with setting no deadline at all.

Used in Lesson 6.3, Lesson 4.3.

The Acts of 1964 and 1965

Document 16 · Double document · Federal laws · Washington, July 2, 1964, and August 6, 1965

The working passages of the Civil Rights Act and of the Voting Rights Act, which is literally “an act to enforce the fifteenth amendment,” signed ninety-five years after that amendment was ratified. Neither invented a new right: they brought the machinery that was missing, federal money and advance approval. The record includes a table of the arc from 1870 to 1965 and notes what remained of Section 5 after 2013.

Used in Lesson 6.3, Lesson 4.1.

Executive Order 8802, 1941

Document 17 · Primary source · Presidential executive order · Washington, June 25, 1941

A. Philip Randolph announced a march on Washington; Roosevelt met him on June 18, signed this order on the 25th, and the march was called off. It is the product of a credible threat and the first presidential directive on race since Reconstruction. The record shows where its limit lay (the committee could only investigate and recommend, and its members were unpaid) and looks carefully at causation, because the text itself gives another reason.

Used in Lesson 6.3.

Massachusetts v. EPA, 2007

Document 18 · Double document · Supreme Court decision · Washington, April 2, 2007

The majority opinion and the dissent of the same 5-to-4 decision on whether the federal environmental agency can and must regulate greenhouse gases. Two reasoned, public-domain positions on a specific environmental policy, written at the same time by people who read the same documents. The record warns that the disagreement is not about whether global warming exists (the dissent expressly declines to judge that) but about who decides, and it follows events up to the rescission of the endangerment finding in February 2026.

Used in Lesson 7.3.
