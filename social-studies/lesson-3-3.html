Before you start

Lesson 3.2 ended with two fears that were both real: the Federalist fear of disorder and the Anti-Federalist fear of tyranny. The Constitution answered both, and the answer left two clauses that pull in opposite directions. This lesson follows them into daily life: why your driver’s license comes from Springfield and your passport from Washington, and why Illinois can pay a minimum wage twice the federal one.

1Two clauses that pull in opposite directions

American federalism rests on two short sentences that point in different directions. Understanding the tension between them is understanding the whole topic.

📜 Two clauses that pull in opposite directions

Document
U.S. Constitution, Article VI (1787) and the Tenth Amendment (1791)
Kind of source
Primary · founding document; the English text is the legal one

The supremacy clause · Article VI

This Constitution, and the Laws of the United States which shall be made in Pursuance thereof; and all Treaties made, or which shall be made, under the Authority of the United States, shall be the supreme Law of the Land; and the Judges in every State shall be bound thereby, any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding.

The Tenth Amendment · 1791

The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.

What each clause does
ClauseWhat it doesThe detail to notice
Supremacy (Art. VI)When a valid federal law and a state law clash head-on, the federal law wins.“Made in Pursuance thereof”: a federal law that goes beyond the Union’s powers is not supreme, because it is not valid.
Tenth AmendmentSets the default rule: if a subject was not given to the Union, it belongs to the states (or the people).It is one of the amendments the Anti-Federalists demanded: the written answer to Brutus’s fear (Lesson 3.2).

How they fit together

The Tenth Amendment says who is in charge by default: the states, except in what was delegated. The supremacy clause says who wins when there is a clash: the Union, as long as it acts within what was delegated. They do not contradict each other. Almost every real argument is about where exactly the border of “what was delegated” lies.

2Three baskets: federal, state, and shared

The three kinds of powers
KindWho uses itWhere it comes fromExamples
Delegated (federal)Only the national governmentNamed in the Constitution, mostly in Article I, Section 8Printing money, declaring war, post offices, trade between states and with other countries, naturalization
Reserved (state)Only the statesNot delegated and not forbidden: the Tenth Amendment leaves it to the statesPublic schools, driver’s licenses, marriage laws, professional licenses, setting up local governments, running elections
Concurrent (shared)Both levels at onceNeither has it alone; each uses it in its own sphereCollecting taxes, borrowing money, setting up courts, making and enforcing laws

The question that settles almost every case

Is this subject named in the Constitution as a power of the Union? If it is, it is federal. If it is not, check whether both levels actually use it: if both, it is concurrent; if only the states, it is reserved. Education, licenses, and marriage are not mentioned in the document, and that is why they are state matters.

3Where you live, according to the Constitution

Federalism is not an abstraction: it is the reason some errands are done in one office and others in another. This is how the levels stack up for someone who lives in Chicago.

Who does what, for someone who lives in Chicago
LevelSome of its jobs
Federal · United StatesMoney, defense, the mail, immigration, trade between states. The President, Congress, and the federal courts.
State · IllinoisEducation, driver’s and professional licenses, criminal law, family law. A governor, a General Assembly with two chambers, and its own courts: the state repeats the three-branch structure.
County · CookProperty records, trial courts, public health, the county jail.
City · ChicagoThe mayor and the City Council (the aldermen of Lesson 1.3). Police, fire department, garbage pickup, business licenses, local ordinances.
Special districtsThe school district and other districts with a single purpose (parks, water). They exist because the state creates them.

The jump that does not exist

Counties, cities, and school districts do not appear in the U.S. Constitution. They exist because the state creates them, and the state can change them. So talking about “three levels of government” is a handy simplification: in fact there are two constitutional levels, the Union and the states, and everything else is created by the states.

4When the two levels clash

Conflicts between the levels do not all have the same shape. These three are different from each other, and recognizing the shape is what lets you analyze them.

Three shapes of conflict
ShapeExampleWhat is going on
1 · A federal floorThe minimum wage. The federal minimum is $7.25 an hour; Illinois set its own at $15 in 2025.No real conflict: the federal law sets a floor and the state builds above it. A concurrent power working as intended. Many people read it as a clash, and it is not one.
2 · Strings attached to moneyHighways and schools. The Union cannot order a state to pass a law on a reserved subject, but it can offer federal money if the state adopts a rule. (In 1984, highway funds were tied to a drinking age of 21.)Formally there is no supremacy, because nobody imposes anything: there is a deal, and the state can say no and lose the money. In practice people argue about how much financial pressure turns an offer into an order.
3 · A head-on clashCannabis. Illinois has allowed licensed adult sales since 2020. Federal law still does not allow recreational cannabis (in 2026 only state-licensed medical cannabis and FDA-approved products were moved to a less strict category).Supremacy against reserved powers, in pure form. The federal law is still in force and a state cannot repeal it; what has happened is that the Union has mostly chosen not to prosecute activity a state allows. A practical truce, not a legal solution.

The method, in three questions

For any conflict you read about in the news: (1) Is the subject delegated to the Union, reserved to the states, or shared? (2) Are the two laws incompatible, or can they live together? (3) If they are incompatible, is the Union acting within what the Constitution delegates? With those three answers you know which principle is at stake.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your errands (20 minutes). Write ten errands or services you used this year (renewing a license, sending a package, enrolling a child in school, paying taxes, calling 911…) and say which level of government handles each one. Check: if every one came out “federal,” look again: most daily services are state or local.
  • One conflict in the news (30 minutes). Find a news story about a disagreement between a state and the federal government. Answer the three questions of section 4 in writing. Check: your third answer should name the clause (supremacy or Tenth Amendment) that each side would cite.

📘Key words

Federalism
Dividing power between the national government and the states.
Supremacy clause
Article VI: the Constitution and valid federal laws and treaties are the supreme law of the land.
Delegated powers
Powers the Constitution gives to the Union.
Reserved powers
Powers kept by the states under the Tenth Amendment.
Concurrent powers
Powers both levels use, like taxing and making courts.
Grant with conditions
Federal money offered on the condition that a state adopts a rule.
Local government
Counties, cities, and districts, created by the state.
