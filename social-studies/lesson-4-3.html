Before you start

Lesson 4.2 ended with a brake mentioned but not explained: courts can strike down an executive order or a regulation that goes beyond its authority. And Lesson 3.2 left a bigger debt: that power is not written in the Constitution. This lesson pays both debts. It ends with the exercise that best teaches what a precedent is: two decisions on the same question, 58 years apart, with opposite results and the same constitutional text in front of them.

1How a case climbs

There are two court systems working at the same time, the federal one and the one in each state, and they only touch at the very top.

Notice the difference between the two top arrows. A federal case can reach the Supreme Court by the normal road. A state case crosses to the federal track only if it raises a question of federal law (the U.S. Constitution, a federal law, or a treaty); otherwise the state supreme court has the last word and there is no one else to ask.

What almost nobody knows: the Supreme Court chooses

It is not required to hear the cases that reach it. Of several thousand petitions a year it accepts a few dozen, and to accept one it is enough that four of the nine justices want to hear it (the “rule of four”). That means most appeals court decisions are final in practice, not because no one could ask for review, but because the Court did not take them.

Article III gives federal judges their job “during good behaviour,” that is, for life unless they are impeached and removed. The President nominates them and the Senate confirms them (Lesson 4.2). Life tenure is meant to let judges decide without fear of losing their job.

Five words to read any court decision

Court vocabulary
WordWhat it means
JurisdictionA court’s authority to hear a matter. A court without jurisdiction cannot decide, even if the answer is clear. Remember this: it is the key to section 2.
AppealAsking a higher court to review what a lower court decided. It is not a new trial: no witnesses are heard again; the question is whether there was an error of law.
Majority opinionThe text that explains the Court’s decision and sets the rule. It is what creates precedent, so its reasoning matters as much as its result.
Dissenting opinionThe opinion of the judges who disagree. It binds no one, but it records the opposing argument, and sometimes it wins decades later.
Stare decisisLatin for “to stand by things decided”: courts follow their earlier decisions (precedents). It gives the law stability, and it is why overturning a precedent is rare.

2Where judicial review came from

Look in the Constitution for the power of courts to strike down a law as unconstitutional. It is not there. The Supreme Court established it itself, in 1803, and the way it did so is a lesson in strategy.

The story

The facts. President John Adams lost the election of 1800. In his last days in office he named a group of new judges. Some of their commissions (the official papers) were not delivered before Thomas Jefferson took office in March 1801, and Jefferson’s Secretary of State, James Madison, refused to deliver them. William Marbury, one of those named, went straight to the Supreme Court and asked it to order Madison to hand over his commission. He relied on a law of Congress, the Judiciary Act of 1789, which allowed going to the Court that way.

The trap. If the Court ordered the delivery, the new government could simply ignore it, and the Court would look powerless. If it did not, it would look like a court without authority.

What Chief Justice John Marshall did. He split the answer in three. First: Marbury has a right to his commission. Second: the law owes him a remedy. Third: this Court cannot give it to him, because the part of the 1789 law that let him come here directly expanded the Court’s jurisdiction beyond what Article III allows, and so that part of the law is void.

The result. Marbury lost. And the Court, in the act of refusing itself a small power, claimed an enormous one: the power to declare that a law of Congress contradicts the Constitution and cannot be applied. This is judicial review. The government had nothing to resist, because it had won the case.

The heart of the opinion

It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is. Those who apply the rule to particular cases, must of necessity expound and interpret that rule. If two laws conflict with each other, the courts must decide on the operation of each.

Thus, the particular phraseology of the constitution of the United States confirms and strengthens the principle, supposed to be essential to all written constitutions, that a law repugnant to the constitution is void; and that courts, as well as other departments, are bound by that instrument.

The reasoning, in one line

If the Constitution is the supreme law and an ordinary law contradicts it, someone has to decide which one applies. And deciding what the law says in a real case is exactly what courts do.

Why this matters beyond 1803

This answers what Lesson 3.2 left open, and the brake that Lesson 4.2 mentioned (“the courts can strike it down”). A good part of what we call constitutional today is not in the text: it was established later, by practice and by decisions like this one. Telling apart what the Constitution says from what has been done with it is what separates a careful reader from one who repeats.

3From Plessy to Brown: what changed and what did not

Two Supreme Court decisions about racial segregation. The same amendment in between, the Fourteenth, with its requirement of “equal protection of the laws.” Fifty-eight years apart. Opposite conclusions.

Two decisions on the same constitutional question
Plessy v. Ferguson, 1896Brown v. Board of Education, 1954
QuestionCan a state require separate railroad cars by race?Can a state have public schools separated by race?
DecisionYes. Separate is not unequal as long as the facilities are equal (“separate but equal”).No. In public education, separate is inherently unequal.
Central reasoningThe law only looks at formal equality. If a group reads separation as a mark of inferiority, that happens in its perception, not in the law.Separation imposed by law sends a message of inferiority that harms learning. The inequality is not only in the buildings but in the act of separating.
Vote7 to 1; Justice Harlan dissented alone (one justice did not take part)9 to 0, unanimous
Constitutional text appliedFourteenth Amendment (1868): “equal protection of the laws”The same Fourteenth Amendment, unchanged

Cases
Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537 (1896) · Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, 347 U.S. 483 (1954)
Authors
Justice Henry Billings Brown (Plessy majority) · Justice John Marshall Harlan (Plessy dissent) · Chief Justice Earl Warren (Brown, unanimous)
Kind of source
Primary · court opinions

The language of 1896 is the language of its time; it is shown as written because the test asks you to read sources as they are.

1896 · The majority in Plessy

We consider the underlying fallacy of the plaintiff's argument to consist in the assumption that the enforced separation of the two races stamps the colored race with a badge of inferiority. If this be so, it is not by reason of anything found in the act, but solely because the colored race chooses to put that construction upon it.

1896 · Justice Harlan, dissenting

But in view of the constitution, in the eye of the law, there is in this country no superior, dominant, ruling class of citizens. There is no caste here. Our constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens.

1954 · The unanimous Court in Brown

We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of “separate but equal” has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.

The question to answer, and it is not easy

If the Constitution did not change between 1896 and 1954 (and it did not: the Fourteenth Amendment is from 1868 and reads the same), then what changed? Three things, and none of them is the text:

  1. The interpretation. What “equal protection of the laws” means is not defined in the amendment. In 1896 it was read as formal equality: two equal train cars, equal treatment. In 1954 it was read to include the real effects of separation.
  2. The evidence available. In 1954 the Court had studies on the effects of segregation on children that did not exist in 1896. A court does not decide in a vacuum: it decides with what can be shown in its time.
  3. The social context. Between the two decisions there are two world wars, a civil rights movement taking shape, and a different country. Judges do not live outside their time; that is a description, not a reproach.
And a fourth thing, which teaches what a dissent is

In 1896 Justice Harlan was the only one who voted against, and he argued that the Constitution recognizes no classes among citizens. His opinion bound no one: he lost 7 to 1. Fifty-eight years later, the whole Court reached a conclusion close to his. A dissent does not change the result of the case, but it leaves in writing an argument that can win later.

Result and reasoning are not the same thing

“Plessy lost” is a result. “Separation is equal if the facilities are equal” is the reasoning, and that is the part that became precedent and was applied to schools, buses, and restaurants for 58 years. To read a decision, always ask two questions: who won, and what rule does it leave for future cases?

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The path of a case (15 minutes). Draw the two tracks, federal and state, from memory, and mark where a case can cross from one to the other. Add where there is no one left to appeal to. Check: the crossing exists only with a federal question, and there is no one left to ask as soon as the Supreme Court declines a case, which happens almost always.
  • From Plessy to Brown (30 minutes). With the table of section 3 in front of you, write a paragraph answering: if the Constitution did not change, what did the 1954 Court rely on to decide the opposite of the 1896 Court? Name the three things that did change, with one example of each. Check: if your paragraph only says the 1896 Court was wrong, it is missing the point. The question is not who was right, but what lets two courts read the same text and reach opposite conclusions.
  • Result and reasoning (25 minutes). Find a summary of a recent Supreme Court decision. Write in one sentence who won, and in another the reasoning. Then say what rule it leaves for future cases. Check: if your two sentences say the same thing, you have not yet separated the result from the argument.

➡️Coming next

This closes the debt that came from Topic 3. The power to strike down a law is not written in the Constitution: the Court established it in 1803, in a decision lost by the man who had asked for it. And the larger idea of the lesson goes well beyond the test: a precedent can fall without a comma of the text changing, because between the text and the decision there are always three things: how it is interpreted, what can be shown, and the world in which it is decided.

This lesson ends Topic 4 and the civics block, which is half of the test. You have walked through the three branches from the inside: a Congress where lawmaking is hard by design, an executive that chooses every day between speed and permanence, and courts whose greatest power they gave themselves. Topic 5 brings all of this down to the person: rights, elections, and where public money comes from and where it goes.

📘Key words

Jurisdiction
A court’s authority to hear a matter.
Appeal
Asking a higher court to review a decision for errors of law.
Certiorari
The Supreme Court’s order agreeing to review a case; it takes four votes.
Judicial review
The power of courts to declare a law or government act unconstitutional.
Majority opinion
The opinion that explains the decision and sets the rule.
Dissenting opinion
The opinion of the judges who disagree; it binds no one.
Precedent · stare decisis
An earlier decision used as a rule; the principle of standing by it.
