Social studies · Topic 8 — Putting it together: multiple-source scenarios and GED preparation

Lesson 8.2 · Final project: civic inquiry and informed action

Choose a question from your own community, find and judge the sources yourself, and propose an action someone can really carry out.

⏱️ About 8 hours, spread over two or three weeks SSP.1 – SSP.11 · All the practices of the course C3 Framework · Dimension 4: informed action No quiz · the project is the assessment

🎯What you will do

  • Ask a question worth researching that allows more than one reasonable answer.
  • Gather relevant sources and judge the credibility of each one.
  • Join numerical evidence and testimony in one argument.
  • Reach a conclusion the size of the evidence and propose a civic action that can be done.

Before you start

Where we are. In the twenty-one earlier lessons you analyzed the sources you were given. Here you choose them, find them, and judge them yourself, on a subject that really matters to you.

This page is your workbook. It is not a lesson to read: it is a template you fill in over two or three weeks. This project has no quiz: the project is the assessment, and its rubric is on this page from day one. Read it before you start, not after you finish.

What you need: a subject from your community that matters to you (there are twenty below if none comes to mind), internet access to look for public data, and someone to listen to you for five minutes at the end. One person is enough.

💾How to use this workbook

Type straight into the fields. The page saves a draft in this browser by itself every few seconds, and you can also press Save draft. When you come back to this page on the same device, you will find your work where you left it.

A saved draft is not a backup

It is only on this device and is lost if the browser’s data are cleared or if you use another device. To keep your work, press Make the report and copy the text, or use Print or save as PDF.

📊The rubric, from day one

This rubric does not appear at the end to grade you: you get it now, at the start, because it is the guide for the work. Five criteria with four levels each: 20 points in all. It takes the place of the lesson quiz.

Look at criteria 2 and 4

Naming your least reliable source and what is still unresolved adds points; it does not take them away. A project that admits what its evidence cannot show is better done, not worse.

❓The question decides everything

The most important step of the project is the first one, and it is where most people go wrong. A compelling question must meet three conditions at once.

The three conditions of a compelling question
ConditionWhat it meansDoes not work
ArguableIt allows more than one reasonable answer. If there is only one, it is not research: it is a lookup.“How many schools are in my district?”
ResearchableThere are data and testimony you can reach to answer it. If there are not, it cannot be done.“What would my neighbors have thought in 1950?”
BoundedIt has a specific place and period. Without them, any answer fits and none holds up.“Is housing fair?”

The same concern, asked three ways, and only one works
WordingWhat happens
“How many bus lines run through my neighborhood?”Researchable and bounded, but not arguable: it has one answer and you can find it in a minute. It is a fact, not a question.
“Is public transit unfair?”Arguable, but neither researchable nor bounded: it does not say where, when, or compared with what. Any answer is worth the same, which means none is worth anything.
“Does my neighborhood have worse access to public transit than other neighborhoods of the city, and what would explain the difference?”It works. It allows more than one answer, there are public data to answer it, and it has a place and a comparison.

The test of a good question

If you can imagine two reasonable, informed people answering it differently, you are on track. If the answer seems obvious before you start, change it: research whose result you already know is not research, and the rubric notices.

🔎Where the data are

More public data are available, for free, than people imagine. These are the places to look, ordered by how close they are to your neighborhood.

Public data sources, from local to national
WhereWhat you findHow to get there

U.S. Census BureauPopulation, income, housing, commuting, language spoken at home. With detail by census tract, which is almost block by block.Search for “American Community Survey” and your neighborhood or ZIP code.

Why this table has no links

On purpose. Addresses change, portals are redesigned, and a broken link in a course is worse than none. And there is a better reason: knowing how to find the source is part of what this project assesses.

1Step 1 · Ask

Write your compelling question and, under it, three smaller supporting questions you must answer to get there. Check it against the three conditions: arguable, researchable, bounded.

A comparison you can measure plus a “what would explain it” usually works. Example: “Does my neighborhood have worse access to public transit than other neighborhoods of the city, and what would explain the difference?”

Smaller questions, often with one answer each, that build the big one. Example: How many buses an hour stop in my neighborhood? And in the city as a whole? How long do my neighbors take to get to work?

Two or three lines. You will say this in the first minute of your presentation.

The test of a good question

Read your question to someone and ask them to answer it from memory. If they give a quick, sure answer, the question was not arguable: change it now, not in week 3.

2Step 2 · Find and evaluate sources

Gather at least four sources: two with numbers and two with a human voice. For each one, write down three things before you use it: who produced it, when the data are from, and for what purpose it was published. It is the same card you have seen on every document in this course, and for the same reason: a source with no origin cannot be judged, and one that cannot be judged cannot be used.

Two with numbers and two with a voice is not decoration

An eviction statistic says how many there were and does not say what it is like to be evicted; a family’s testimony says the second and does not prove the first. A project with only numbers convinces no one, and one with only testimony proves nothing. It is what you practiced in Lesson 6.2.

Required, and it earns points (criterion 2).

3Step 3 · Put the evidence together

Build your own table or graph with the data you gathered. Do not copy one from the internet: make yours, even by hand. Write one value per row and the place, group, or year it belongs to, always written the same way (for example, always “My neighborhood” and “City”), so the page can group the rows.

Same unit, or no comparison

Compare only values in the same unit and the same period (Lesson 8.1). A total for your neighborhood next to a percent for the city is not a comparison. If the units differ, divide first: per person, per household, per 1,000 people (Lesson 2.3).

Describe only. The word “because” cannot appear in this paragraph: describing and explaining are two different things (Lesson 6.2).

What do the voices tell you that the numbers cannot, and what do the numbers show that the voices cannot?

4Step 4 · Conclude and propose

The last two steps separate a good project from an excellent one, and both are about not overreaching.

Three conclusions about the same investigation, with the same evidence behind them
ConclusionWhat is wrong with it
“Transit is worse in my neighborhood.”Too vague. Worse than where, measured how? It cannot be checked, so it cannot be discussed either.
“The city deliberately discriminates against my neighborhood in transit.”Too big. Frequency data do not prove intention, and you have no sources on how the decisions were made. It is the absolute of Lesson 8.1.
“Between 2015 and 2025, bus frequency in my neighborhood fell by X%, while in the city as a whole it fell by Y%. The testimony I gathered describes longer trips to work. The data do not show why it was decided this way.”The right size. It says what it measures, against what comparison, with what human voice, and where what it can claim stops.

Answer your question, and cite right away the data point behind it.

Required, and it earns points (criterion 4). Saying it out loud is not a weakness: it shows you understood.

A proposal someone can really carry out

Three conditions of an informed action
ConditionWhat it means
ConcreteIt says exactly what is done. “Raise awareness” is not an action; “present a petition with signatures at the March school board meeting” is.
DirectedIt names whoever has the power to decide it. Everything you learned in Topics 4 and 5 about who does what applies here.
Within reachYou or a small group can start it without permission or a budget. If it needs a federal law, it is not your proposal: it is your wish.

The question that organizes this part

Who has the power to change this? A matter decided by your city council member is not the same as one decided by the school board or by the state. Sending a proposal to someone who cannot grant it is the most common way for nothing to happen.

5Five minutes and two questions

The presentation lasts five minutes and has five parts, about one a minute, in this order: the question and why it matters to you; the sources, where they come from and which one seemed least reliable; the evidence, your graph and what the testimony says; the conclusion, the size of the evidence, and what is still unresolved; the proposal: what, to whom, and when.

One or two sentences per minute. If you go over five minutes when you rehearse, do not talk faster: cut. And if what you cut is “what is still unresolved,” put it back and cut something else.

The two required questions

After the presentation, whoever listens asks you two questions. If you work alone, write them yourself and answer them in writing: these two, whatever the others are.

If someone listens to you

This is what the course asks of the listener: point out one specific strength in the use of evidence and one source worth checking. Not an opinion on the subject, and not general advice. It is the rule of Lesson 7.3: comment on the work, not on the position.

✅How to grade it

Press Make the report, copy the text, and give it to your teacher to grade with the rubric, criterion by criterion. Before you hand it in, grade it yourself with the same rubric, and apply the rule of Lesson 8.1 to your own work: does your conclusion hold up with the evidence you presented?

➡️Coming next

Three ideas to take away. Research whose result you already know is not research, so if your question seems obvious before you start, change it. Numbers and voices need each other: an eviction statistic says how many there were and not what it is like to be evicted. And a proposal goes to whoever has the power to grant it, which is the first thing to find out.

This project is the only work in the course where nobody tells you the right answer in advance, and that is on purpose. Everything else was training for this: reading a source, doubting an average, telling describing from explaining, spotting an absolute, and knowing who decides what.
