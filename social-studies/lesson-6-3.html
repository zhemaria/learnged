Lesson 6.3 · From the 20th century to today: industrialization, wars, and civil rights

When something changes, ask what changed it, and expect more than one answer.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.3 · Multiple causesSSP.10 · Data by decadeSSP.7 · Supported claims

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Relate industrialization and mass immigration to the labor movement and federal regulation.
  • Explain what changed in the international role of the United States between 1918 and 1945.
  • Compare four strategies of the civil rights movement and say what each achieved and what it did not.
  • Read a series of data by decade and propose more than one cause for the same change.

Before you start

Lesson 6.2 ended in 1901, with George Henry White leaving his seat and twenty-eight years of a Congress with no African American ahead. This lesson covers what came next and closes Topic 6. A hundred years do not fit in one lesson, so instead of telling everything, it is organized around a single question, which is also the one the test asks: when something changes, what changed it?

1The machine, the people, and the law

Between the end of Reconstruction and World War I, the United States went from a farming country to the world’s leading industrial power. That required two things in enormous amounts: machines and workers. The machines were built. The workers came by ship.

Immigration to the United States, by decade

Source: yearbooks of the federal immigration service. People admitted, rounded to the nearest hundred thousand.

Almost nine million people in a single decade, 1901 to 1910. And thirty years later, half a million. One thing alone does not explain that curve, and that is the exercise.

Federal immigration and labor laws, 1882–1938
YearLawWhat it did
1882Chinese Exclusion ActBanned the entry of Chinese laborers: the first major federal law to exclude a group by nationality.
1906Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection ActThe first federal regulation of what is sold to eat, passed months after a novel described the Chicago slaughterhouses.
1917Immigration ActImposed a literacy test and drew a zone from which no one was admitted.
1924Immigration Act (Johnson-Reed)Set quotas by national origin based on an old census, sharply cutting entry from southern and eastern Europe.
1935National Labor Relations ActRecognized in federal law the right to form unions and bargain collectively.
1938Fair Labor Standards ActA federal minimum wage, a maximum workweek that reached 40 hours by 1940, and limits on child labor.

The question of this section

The quota law is from 1924, and the collapse in immigration shows up in 1931–1940. In between comes the Great Depression, which began in 1929 and by itself discourages anyone thinking of emigrating to find work. How much of the drop is the law and how much is the crisis? With this graph alone, it cannot be known. You can say that both pushed in the same direction; you cannot divide the weight. Recognizing that is worth more on the test than inventing a percentage.

The other answer: organizing

The same machine that needed workers used them up fast. Twelve-hour days were common, there was no compensation for accidents, and firing someone who complained was legal. The response came by two roads that should not be confused.

Two roads, and what set them off
What it was
From belowUnions, strikes, and collective bargaining. Illegal or nearly so at first; recognized in federal law only in 1935.
From aboveFederal regulation: food inspection, industrial safety, minimum wage. It came when a specific disaster made the situation impossible to defend.
The triggerUsually an event with a name and a date. The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in New York in 1911, with 146 dead, led to dozens of state safety laws within a few years.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The vocabulary of Lesson 6.1 comes back

The fire is the immediate cause of those laws. The conditions that made it possible (locked doors, no inspections, no legal responsibility) are the structural cause, and they had been there for decades. A disaster does not create the problem: it makes it impossible to keep ignoring it.

2The country that stayed the second time

This block of the test is almost always asked the same way: not about battles, but about the role of the United States in the world. And that role changes once, at a moment you can identify.

Two world wars, two different exits
World War I · in and outWorld War II · in and stay
EntryLate, in 1917, after three years of warAfter the attack on Pearl Harbor, December 1941
After the warPresident Wilson proposes a League of Nations; the Senate rejects the treaty, and the country stays out of the organization its own President promoted.The United Nations (1945), with headquarters in New York; the Marshall Plan to rebuild Western Europe (1948); NATO, a permanent military alliance in peacetime (1949).
ResultWin the war and go home, the pattern of the whole 19th century.An international commitment that has lasted eighty years.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

From that came the Cold War: about forty-five years of rivalry with the Soviet Union without direct war between the two powers, but with wars elsewhere (Korea, Vietnam), a nuclear arms race, and a crisis over Cuba in 1962 that came close to nuclear war. It ended between 1989 and 1991, with the fall of the Berlin Wall and the breakup of the Soviet Union.

The sentence that sums up the block

After 1918, the United States won the war and left. After 1945, it won the war and stayed. Everything else (the bases, the alliances, permanent military spending, the interventions) comes from that second decision.

And it had consequences at home

How the wars connect with civil rights
ConnectionWhat happened
1 · The war created jobsMillions of defense jobs, mostly in Northern and Western cities, sped up the second wave of the Great Migration from the South.
2 · The war created leverageIf the country needed everyone producing, excluding workers by race was a production problem as well as an injustice. That is the argument of Executive Order 8802.
3 · The war created a contradictionA segregated army fighting a racist ideology, and Black soldiers coming home decorated to places where they could not vote.
4 · The Cold War added a cold reasonWith two blocs competing for the sympathy of newly independent countries in Africa and Asia, segregation became a foreign-policy problem. The federal government argued exactly that before the Supreme Court in the Brown case.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Careful with what you conclude

That the Cold War gave the federal government a reason of convenience to act is a documented fact. It does not follow that the civil rights movement was a product of foreign policy: the people who organized had been doing it for decades, for their own reasons. Both things are true, and a good answer holds them together without letting one swallow the other.

3Four ways to change a law

This is the central section of the lesson. The civil rights movement did not use one strategy: it used several at the same time, and each one served different purposes. Comparing them is what the test asks.

⚖️ Strategy 1 · Litigation

📜 Document A · Litigation: Brown v. Board of Education

Date
May 17, 1954; unanimous
Author
Chief Justice Earl Warren

We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of “separate but equal” has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.

For twenty years the NAACP took cases about graduate schools to court, where the material inequality was impossible to deny, and narrowed the circle until it attacked the whole doctrine (Lesson 4.3).

What it achieved and what it could not do
What it achievedWhat it could not do
It changed the law of the whole country at once, unanimously.It set no deadline. A second decision (1955) said only “with all deliberate speed,” and ten years later much of the Deep South was still segregated.
It created a precedent to rely on in any lower court.It created no machinery: families had to sue district by district, with money and lawyers.
—It covered only public education: not buses, restaurants, or jobs.

📣 Strategy 2 · Mobilization

📜 Document B · Mobilization: Executive Order 8802

Date
June 25, 1941
Author
President Franklin D. Roosevelt

…there shall be no discrimination in the employment of workers in defense industries or government because of race, creed, color, or national origin…

A. Philip Randolph announced a march on Washington before negotiating anything. Roosevelt met him on June 18 and signed on June 25. The march was called off.

What it achieved and what it could not do
What it achievedWhat it could not do
A result in seven days, without the march ever taking place: the credible threat was enough.The committee it created could only investigate and recommend; it could not fine anyone or cancel contracts.
The first presidential order on race since Reconstruction.One President signs an executive order and another can revoke it: it is not a law (Lesson 4.2).
—After the war, Congress kept the committee from becoming permanent.

🏛️ Strategy 3 · Legislation

📜 Document C · Legislation: the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965

Dates
July 2, 1964, and August 6, 1965
Kind of source
Primary · federal laws. Excerpts.

Title VI, 1964

No person in the United States shall, on the ground of race, color, or national origin, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance.

Section 5, 1965

[A covered State wishing to change a voting rule] may institute an action in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia for a declaratory judgment that such qualification, prerequisite, standard, practice, or procedure does not have the purpose and will not have the effect of denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race or color…

The slowest of the four, and the one that provided real tools. The 1965 law calls itself, literally, an act “to enforce the fifteenth amendment”: it was signed ninety-five years after that amendment was ratified.

What it achieved and what it could not do
What it achievedWhat it could not do
It reversed the burden: before, the person suffering discrimination had to sue; now the state had to ask permission and prove its rule did not discriminate.It took a long time: ninety-five years between the amendment and the law that enforced it.
It tied money to compliance: whoever segregated lost federal funds. That integrated schools that ten years of court decisions had not.A law can be changed, and courts can strike down parts of it: in 2013 the Supreme Court struck down the formula that decided which states were covered by Section 5 (Lesson 5.1).
It covered what Brown did not: businesses open to the public, jobs, federal programs.—

✊ Strategy 4 · Civil disobedience and boycotts

Publicly disobeying a law considered unjust, accepting arrest, and so forcing the conflict into view. Its forms were boycotts of segregated services (the Montgomery bus boycott lasted more than a year, 1955–1956), sit-ins at lunch counters that would not serve Black customers (Greensboro, 1960), and marches. Its distinctive feature is that it needs no one’s permission: not a judge’s, not a President’s, not Congress’s. And its cost is paid by those who practice it: arrests, firings, and violence.

What it achieved and what it could not do
What it achievedWhat it could not do
It put the issue on the front page and on television, which was new and in every home.By itself, it does not change a single comma of a law: someone (a court, a President, Congress) has to turn pressure into text.
It created the political pressure without which the laws of 1964 and 1965 would not have passed Congress.Its personal cost is very high, and it falls on those who can least afford it.
It showed that the economic cost of segregation could fall on those who kept it.—

Why this strategy has no document here

The texts that explain it best, Martin Luther King Jr.’s “Letter from Birmingham Jail” and his speech at the March on Washington, both from 1963, are still under copyright and will be for decades. This course only reproduces documents in the public domain. It is not an omission: it is copyright law at work. Look for them in a library or on the King Center’s website: this section is a summary, and they are the source.

4How to evaluate a strategy

A test answer that analyzes a strategy “with evidence” must recognize both what it achieved and where it stopped. That is not politeness: it is a method, with four questions.

Four questions to evaluate a strategy for social change, applied to Brown
QuestionWhat it looks forApplied to Brown
What exactly did it produce?A document, a law, a decision, a measurable change. Not an impression.A unanimous decision ending “separate but equal” in public education.
Whom does it bind, and with what?The difference between declaring and enforcing.School districts, but only if someone sues them. No deadline and no penalty.
Who pays the cost?Every strategy has a cost, and someone carries it.The families who sued: money, lawyers, years, and reprisals in their own communities.
What stayed out of reach?The stated limit, not the one you assume.Everything that is not public education. The text itself says so.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The mistake that costs the most points

Writing that a strategy “worked” or “failed.” None of the four did only one or the other: each achieved something specific and stopped at a specific point. A strong answer says what it achieved and where it stopped, and supports both with the document.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Two causes for one change (20 minutes). Write a paragraph about the drop in immigration in the 1930s naming both possible causes and saying what evidence would separate them. Check: your paragraph must not give a percentage to either cause.
  • One strategy, four questions (30 minutes). Apply the four questions of section 4 to Executive Order 8802 or to the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Check: each answer cites the document.
  • Before and after (15 minutes). In two sentences, explain the difference between the U.S. role after 1918 and after 1945, with one example from each. Check: League of Nations rejected; UN, Marshall Plan, NATO.

➡️Coming next

This lesson crossed a century around one question: when something changes, what changed it? Immigration fell for two reasons that cannot be separated with one graph. Labor laws came from below and from above, set off by disasters with names and dates. The country won two world wars and chose differently after each one. And civil rights advanced through four strategies that needed each other, none of which simply “worked” or “failed.”

This ends Topic 6. Topic 7 turns to economics and geography: supply and demand, population and migration, and resources, using the same habit of separating what the data show from what explains them.

📘Key words

Industrialization
The shift from farming and handcraft to factory production.
Quota
A fixed limit on the number of immigrants from each country.
Collective bargaining
Negotiation between a union and an employer over wages and conditions.
Cold War
The rivalry between the United States and the Soviet Union, about 1947–1991, without direct war between them.
Litigation
Using lawsuits in court to change the law.
Civil disobedience
Publicly and peacefully breaking a law considered unjust, and accepting the penalty.
Boycott
Refusing to buy or use a service to pressure those who run it.
