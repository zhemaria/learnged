Social studies · Topic 1 — Inquiry, sources, and analytical literacy

Lesson 1.1 · Compelling and supporting questions: the inquiry arc

The test almost never asks for a date. It asks what a document supports. It all starts with knowing how to ask.

⏱️ About 4 hours with practice and homework · take breaks SSP.1 · Inferences from sourcesSSP.2 · Central ideasSSP.7 · Claims and evidence

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Tell apart a compelling question (open, debatable, of lasting importance) from a supporting question that one source can check.
  • Derive from a compelling question the supporting questions you need to answer it.
  • Decide which kind of source would answer each supporting question, and what lies outside what that source can show.
  • Recognize the four dimensions of the C3 inquiry arc in a worked example.

Before you start

Where it all starts. It is worth saying from the first minute, because it changes how you study: the GED® Social Studies test almost never asks for a date. What it does is put a document in front of you (a piece of the Constitution, a map, a graph of unemployment, a speech, a political cartoon) and ask what it supports and what it does not.

And behind each of those documents there was first a question. Someone wanted to know something, asked it in a certain way, and that way decided what evidence they went out to look for. That is why this course does not start with history or government, but with questions.

What you need: a notebook, a local newspaper or news website, and a topic from your community that really matters to you (transit, housing, your children’s school, work). You will choose it at the end and use it in several more lessons.

1The inquiry arc

The C3 Framework is the plan that most social studies teaching in the United States follows today. Its central idea is that investigating is not collecting information: it is a path that goes from a question to a conclusion you can defend. The path has four parts, and it is called an arc because it rises from curiosity and comes down to action.

The four dimensions of the inquiry arc
DimensionWhat it isThe question that guides it
1 · Develop questionsWrite the question that will hold up the whole inquiry, and decide which smaller questions you need to answer it.What do I want to find out, and why does it matter to someone else?
2 · Apply the tools of each disciplineThink about the problem the way a historian, a geographer, an economist, or a specialist in government would.Which discipline shows this problem best?
3 · Evaluate sources and use evidenceGather documents and data, judge whether they can be trusted, and decide what they really support.What does this source prove, and what does it only suggest?
4 · Communicate conclusions and take actionPresent the answer with its evidence, admit the objections, and propose an informed action.What can I claim, and what would it take to convince someone who disagrees?

Example 1 · A complete arc from start to finish

1Develop questions. In a Chicago neighborhood, the bus line on the main street was moved two blocks and stopped passing in front of the clinic. A student decides to look into it. She writes the question that holds everything up: Whom should a neighborhood’s public transit serve first? From it she draws three smaller questions: when the route changed, who made the decision, and how many people used that stop.

2Apply the tools of each discipline. She sees that the problem is about civics (who decides, and with what authority) and about geography (distances, population density, and access).

3Evaluate sources. She gathers the minutes of the public meeting where the change was approved, the old and new route maps, and the rider counts that the transit agency publishes. She notices that the minutes record three opinions in favor and none against, and she asks herself who was not at that meeting.

4Communicate and take action. She writes one page with her conclusion and the evidence for it, and presents it at the next public hearing for the neighborhood.

✓The inquiry did not end in a list of loose facts but in a claim that another person can argue about with evidence. That is exactly what the test will ask you to do with the documents it shows you.

It is an arc, not a staircase

The four dimensions are not four fixed steps. It is normal to go back from the third to the first: when you find an unexpected source, the question usually gets sharper. An arc that never changes usually means the question was too easy.

2Compelling questions and supporting questions

Dimension 1 separates two kinds of questions. A supporting question looks for a fact: it has one answer, one source can check it, and once it is answered, it is done. In what year was Chicago founded? A compelling question looks for an argument: it allows more than one reasonable answer, it needs several sources, and it is still alive after you answer it. Was industrialization a gain or a loss for the workers of Chicago?

Both are needed, and neither is better than the other. What does not work is mixing them up. An inquiry built on a supporting question is over in three minutes. An inquiry that has only a compelling question, with no supporting questions under it, stays an opinion without evidence.

The five-second test

If a quick internet search answers the question and everyone would accept that answer, it is a supporting question. If two informed people can answer it differently and both can defend their answer with evidence, it is a compelling question.

A compelling question has three features at the same time. If one is missing, it is not compelling yet.

The three features of a compelling question
FeatureWhat it meansSign that it is missing
OpenIt is not answered with a fact, a yes, or a no.The answer fits in one word or one number.
DebatableInformed people can hold different answers, with evidence.Everyone would answer the same, or the answer depends only on personal taste.
Of lasting importanceIt touches a problem that still matters outside this one case.It only matters inside the example and cannot be carried to any other situation.

Example 2 · From a weak question to a compelling one

1Starting point: In what year was the Nineteenth Amendment ratified? The five-second test answers it: 1920, and nobody disputes it. It is a perfectly good supporting question, but it cannot hold up an inquiry.

2Look for the tension behind the fact. The amendment said the vote could not be denied because of sex, but many women, especially Black women in the South, still could not vote in practice for decades.

3Rewrite the question around that tension: Is it enough to recognize a right in the law for that right to really exist?

4Check the three features: it is open, it can be debated with evidence, and it still matters today, because you can ask it about any right that exists on paper.

✓The first question is not thrown away. It becomes one of the supporting questions that the new question needs.

Debatable does not mean a matter of taste

“What is the best city in the United States?” allows many answers, but evidence cannot settle any of them: it depends on what each person likes. A compelling question is argued with documents and data, not with personal preferences. If nobody could ever lose the argument by bringing evidence, the question is not ready yet.

3Deriving the supporting questions

A compelling question cannot be answered all at once: it is too big. You answer it in parts, and the parts are the supporting questions. The skill the test checks again and again (even if it never uses that name) is exactly this one: looking at a claim and seeing what you would need to find out to support it.

A well-written supporting question meets one simple condition: you can name the source that answers it before you look for it. If you do not know where you would look for the answer, the question is not well written yet.

Supporting questions and the kind of source that answers each one
Supporting questionSource that answers it
How many people used that bus stop?Rider data published by the transit agency.
Who approved the change, and with what authority?Minutes of the public meeting; the city ordinance.
How did the neighborhood’s population change in ten years?U.S. Census tables for that area.
What did the neighbors say when it was announced?Local newspapers from that date; interviews; letters to the editor.

Example 3 · From a headline to an inquiry

1Headline: “The city closes two South Side schools because of low enrollment.” Compelling question: When a district decides to close a school, what should weigh more, the cost or the students’ access?

2Supporting: How many students were enrolled in each school during the last five years? The district’s enrollment reports answer it.

3Supporting: How far away is the nearest school that will take those students? A map of the district with its scale answers it.

4Supporting: How much does the district save by closing them, and what will that money be used for? The approved budget answers it.

✓The three supporting questions do not answer the compelling one by themselves, but without them any answer would be only an opinion. Notice also that each one names its source before going to look.

The most common mistake

Writing supporting questions that already assume the answer to the compelling one. “Why do school closings hurt poor families?” is not a supporting question: it takes for granted exactly what had to be investigated. A question that only lets in evidence from one side is not a question; it is a conclusion in disguise. The open version would be: “How did the travel time to school change for the students of the closed schools?”

4A document to analyze: letters from 1917

Here are four real letters written in 1917 by people who were thinking of leaving the South. They were sent to the Chicago Defender, a Black newspaper that urged readers to come north, and to the Urban League, which helped newcomers find work and housing. These letters are part of the Great Migration: between about 1916 and 1970, some six million African Americans moved from the rural South to cities in the North and West. Read them thinking about which questions they answer and which ones they do not.

📜 Four letters from migrants, 1917

Document

Authors

About the language of these letters. The people who wrote them had little schooling, and the spelling is irregular. It is shown exactly as it was printed in 1919, because it is evidence too: it tells us something about who wrote and in what conditions. The letters also use the racial words of 1917 (“the Race,” “Colored”), copied as they are.

Letter 1 · Sanford, Florida, April 27, 1917

Dear Sir: I have seen through the Chicago Defender that you and the people of Chicago are helping newcomers. I am asking you for some information about conditions in some small town near Chicago.

There are some families here thinking of moving up, and are desirous of knowing what to expect before leaving. Please state about treatment, work, rent and schools. Please answer at some spare time.

Letter 2 · Mobile, Alabama, April 25, 1917

Sir: I was reading in theat paper about the Colored race and while reading it I seen in it where cars would be here for the 15 of May which is one month from to day. Will you be so kind as to let me know where they are coming to and I will be glad to know because I am a poor woman and have a husband and five children living and three dead.

my husband only get $1.50 a day and pays $7.50 a month for house rent and can hardly feed me and his self and children. I am the mother of 8 children 25 years old and I want to get out of this dog hold because I dont know what I am raising them up for in this place and I want to get to Chicago where I know they will be raised.

Letter 3 · Ellisville, Mississippi, May 1, 1917

Wages here are so low can scarcely live We can buy enough to eat we only buy enough to Keep up alive I mean the greater part of the Race. Women wages are from $1.25 Some time as high, as $2.50. just some time for a whole week.

P. S. You can place my letter in Some of the Defender Colums but done use my name in print, for it might get back down here.

Letter 4 · New Orleans, Louisiana, April 22, 1917

under the head lines in the Chicage Defender of Saturday April 22-17 I red how some of us that goes up north are being treated, there is a few that have gone from this city north, and came back a few weeks, some say they came back on account of being to cold “The others Say they ware to pay so much to get work etc”

I would like to go north, and would rather be in some place, other then Chicago, or near Chicago.

What these letters do not say

All four were written before leaving. None tells what happened after arriving, and Letter 4 even warns that some people came back. A set of departure letters answers very well why people wanted to leave, and it does not answer at all whether life got better. Seeing that limit (what the source proves and what it only suggests) is the skill that Lesson 1.2 works on in depth.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Twenty questions (25 minutes). Write twenty questions about one topic you care about (transit, housing, work, school) without thinking about categories. Then sort them: compelling on one side, supporting on the other. Turn five supporting questions into compelling ones by finding the tension behind each fact. Check: apply the five-second test to every “compelling” question, and make sure you can name the source for every “supporting” one.
  • From a headline to an inquiry (30 minutes). Choose a local news headline about a public decision (a budget, a construction project, a closing). Write one compelling question and three supporting questions, with the kind of source that would answer each one. Check: imagine someone defending the opposite answer. If you cannot imagine any reasonable argument on the other side, rewrite the question.
  • Your own inquiry (40 minutes). Choose a topic from your community that really matters to you. Write a compelling question, three supporting questions, and a paragraph that explains why the first one has no single answer. Keep this page: you will use it again in Lessons 1.2 and 1.3, and it will be the starting point of the final project in Topic 8.

➡️Coming next

This lesson set up the first tool of the course: telling a question about a fact from a question about an argument, and breaking a big question into the small questions that hold it up. It is dimension 1 of the arc, and it is also the habit the test rewards when it asks what a document supports.

But a supporting question without a reliable source is worth nothing. Lesson 1.2 teaches you to question a document before reading it closely: who made it, when, for whom, and for what purpose. Keep the inquiry you wrote for homework: there, we will look for its sources.

📘Key words

Inquiry
An investigation that goes from a question to a conclusion supported by evidence.
C3 Framework
The plan for social studies in the United States (College, Career, and Civic Life); it organizes inquiry into four dimensions.
Compelling question
An open, debatable question of lasting importance that needs several sources.
Supporting question
A specific question with one answer that one source can check.
Evidence
Documents, data, or testimony that support (or weaken) a claim.
Great Migration
The move of about six million African Americans from the rural South to cities in the North and West, about 1916–1970.
Minutes
The official written record of what was said and decided at a meeting.
