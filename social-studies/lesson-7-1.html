And it changes method: here there are fewer documents to interpret and more graphs to read and calculations to do. This lesson starts where all of economics starts: there is not enough for everything.

1There is not enough for everything

All of economics comes from one fact: resources are scarce and wants are not. Scarce does not mean rare, expensive, or small. It means there is not enough for every possible use at the same time. There is a lot of drinking water in the world, and it is still scarce, because it is not where it is needed and moving it costs money. The test takes one line: if using something for one thing prevents using it for another, it is scarce.

From there, with no step in between, comes the concept asked most in this block. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up. It is not what you pay: it is what you stop doing.

A course, counted completely

1What you pay: tuition of $600. It appears on the receipt.

2What you stop doing: 10 hours a week of study that could have been worked at $18 an hour. It appears nowhere.

3The opportunity cost: the $600 plus what you would have earned in those hours ($180 a week). That is what the test asks.

Two rules to take away

First: look for one alternative, the best of the ones given up. Do not add up every option you passed on, only the one you would have chosen if the first did not exist. Second: the opportunity cost can be time, not money, and a decision that costs nothing on the receipt can have a huge opportunity cost.

The test mistake

“I spent Saturday studying. It cost me nothing.” It did: the eight-hour shift you did not work, or the day with your family. Free does not mean it costs nothing. If your answer to an opportunity-cost question is “zero,” you almost certainly looked at the price instead of the alternative.

2Two curves and one crossing

If resources are scarce, they have to be shared out. In a market economy, the price does that, and the price comes from where two curves cross.

The two curves
Demand · what buyers wantSupply · what sellers want
The ruleThe higher the price, the less they want to buy.The higher the price, the more they want to sell.
The shapeIt slopes down from left to right (red in this lesson).It slopes up from left to right (blue in this lesson).

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Where they cross is the equilibrium: the only price at which the quantity people want to buy is exactly the quantity people want to sell. Nothing left over, nothing missing. (The numbers below are chosen so the arithmetic is easy.)

Above the equilibrium price there is a surplus, and sellers lower the price. Below it there is a shortage, and buyers compete and push the price up. That is why the market tends toward the crossing.

The distinction asked most

A change in demand versus a change in quantity demanded
What is saidWhat happens on the graphWhat causes it
Demand changesThe whole curve shifts right or left.Something other than the price: a fashion, more income, a cheaper substitute.
Quantity demanded changesThe curve does not move: you slide along it.The price, and only the price.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Why “the price went up, so demand went down” is wrong

What went down was the quantity demanded. The demand curve stays exactly where it was. And the difference matters: if the curve itself had moved, the equilibrium price would have changed too.

3What happens to the price?

Only four things can happen, and they all fit in this table. It is worth learning, because with it you can solve any question of this kind.

The four possible shifts and their effect on the equilibrium
What happensCurve that movesPriceQuantity
Demand risesDemand → right↑ Rises↑ Rises
Demand fallsDemand → left↓ Falls↓ Falls
Supply risesSupply → right↓ Falls↑ Rises
Supply fallsSupply → left↑ Rises↓ Falls

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Here demand shifted right (more buyers, or a fashion): price and quantity both went up, from $5 and 50 to $6 and 60.

The trick so you don’t memorize four rows

When demand moves, price and quantity change in the same direction. When supply moves, they change in opposite directions. And one question before moving anything: does the event affect the buyer or the seller? A drought, a new machine, a tax on production, or the price of fuel touch the seller: they move supply. A fashion, a higher income, a health warning, or the price of a substitute touch the buyer: they move demand. Almost every mistake in this block comes from skipping that question.

4What it really costs

So far, markets. Now the part you use the same day you leave class. A washing machine costs $600. Look at what each way of paying costs in total.

The total cost of the same $600 purchase, by way of paying (rounded for teaching)
Way of payingHow it worksWhat you end up paying
CashPay $600 today.$600. But you no longer have that $600 for anything else: that is its opportunity cost.
Installments24 payments of $32 a month.$768: $168 in interest, 28% more than the price tag.
Credit card, paying the minimumA card at 24% a year, paying about the minimum each month.Quite a bit more than $768, and the figure grows the longer it takes, because interest is charged every month on what is still owed.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The calculation

1Multiply the payment by the number of payments: 32 × 24 = 768.

2Subtract the cash price: 768 − 600 = 168. That is what the credit costs.

3As a percent: 168 ÷ 600 × 100 = 28%.

✓None of the three options is “the right one.” Cash costs less money and leaves less on hand; the other two buy time in exchange for interest, and sometimes that is worth it. What is always a mistake is not doing the math.

And inflation, which charges you without being noticed

Inflation is a general, sustained rise in prices. Its practical effect is that the same money buys less every year. It is measured with the cost of a basket of typical purchases (example figures):

Inflation from the cost of the same basket
YearCost of the basketInflation since the base year
Base year$850.000.0%
One year later$875.00(875 − 850) ÷ 850 × 100 = 2.9%
Two years later$891.00(891 − 850) ÷ 850 × 100 = 4.8%

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Two things the test does ask about inflation

First: not all prices rise the same. Inside the basket some items rise, some fall, and the total can rise anyway. Second: it hurts most those with a fixed income, like a retiree, because their money loses value with no raise. For someone with a fixed-rate debt, on the other hand, inflation makes the debt cheaper.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your opportunity cost (15 minutes). Choose a decision you made this week and write its opportunity cost, in money or in time. Check: name one alternative, not a list.
  • Which curve? (20 minutes). Find two news items about prices (gasoline, eggs, rent). For each, say whether buyers or sellers were affected, which curve moved, and what happened to price and quantity. Check: demand moves both in the same direction; supply in opposite directions.
  • A real contract (20 minutes). With a real financing offer or bill, compute the total paid, the interest in dollars, and the interest as a percent of the cash price. Check: payment × number of payments − cash price.

📘Key words

Scarcity
Not having enough resources for every possible use at the same time.
Opportunity cost
The value of the best alternative given up.
Demand
How much buyers want to buy at each price.
Supply
How much sellers want to sell at each price.
Equilibrium
The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
Substitute
A product used in place of another (tea for coffee).
Interest
The price of borrowing money.
Inflation
A general and sustained rise in prices.
