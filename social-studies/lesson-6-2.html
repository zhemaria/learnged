Lesson 6.2 · Expansion, slavery, the Civil War, and Reconstruction

Four compromises decided where and postponed whether; three amendments changed the text, and the numbers show what happened.

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain why each new territory turned slavery into a national crisis.
  • Analyze the four legislative and judicial attempts and say what they all had in common.
  • Contrast an official document with the testimony of the person it describes.
  • Evaluate Reconstruction with data and documents, not with a general judgment.

Before you start

Lesson 6.1 ended with an idea: in March 1766 the immediate conflict was solved and the structural one was left alive. This lesson watches the same thing happen four times in a row, with four compromises, and then looks at what happened when the deep question was finally settled by force. It adds a new skill: reading a series of data and proposing what explains it, which is practice SSP.10.

1Not a map: a count

Between 1803 and 1853 the United States more than tripled its territory: the Louisiana Purchase, Florida, Texas, Oregon, half of Mexico, the Gadsden Purchase. On textbook maps this looks like a stain spreading west, and told that way it seems a matter of geography. It was not.

Sooner or later, every territory became a state. And every state, whatever its population, sent two senators.

Why each new territory became a national crisis
Free statesSlave states
PopulationGrowing fast with European immigration, which settled in the NorthSmaller, and part of it with no rights
House of Representatives (by population)Gained seats with every censusLost weight with every census
Senate (two per state)Tied, as long as the number of states was equalThe only place where the South kept the balance

The whole mechanism

As long as the number of free and slave states was equal, the Senate stayed tied and no law against slavery could pass. Admitting one more free state broke the tie for good. That is why no territory was admitted alone, and why an argument about Missouri or Kansas was really an argument about who would govern the country. The test will rarely ask the date a state was admitted; it will give you a map or a table and ask why admission caused a crisis. The answer is always the same count: two senators per state.

2Four compromises and one crack

For forty years, Congress solved that count four times. All four worked. All four failed. It is worth seeing why, because the pattern is the same one you saw in the Declaratory Act of 1766.

Four attempts to settle slavery in the territories by law
AttemptWhat it didHow long it lasted, and why it broke
Missouri Compromise, 1820Missouri enters as a slave state and Maine as a free state, keeping the tie. A line is drawn at latitude 36°30′: no slavery north of it in the Louisiana territory.34 years. It broke when new territories arrived that the line had not foreseen, and a later law repealed it.
Compromise of 1850California enters as a free state; in exchange, a much harsher Fugitive Slave Act makes Northern states help capture people who escaped.4 years. The fugitive law brought the conflict into Northern towns, which until then could look the other way.
Kansas-Nebraska Act, 1854Repeals the 36°30′ line and lets the settlers of each territory decide by vote (“popular sovereignty”).It broke at once. If the question was settled by votes, it paid to bring voters: settlers from both sides arrived, and there was armed violence in Kansas.
Dred Scott v. Sandford, 1857Not a compromise but its end. The Supreme Court (7 to 2) rules that Congress cannot ban slavery in the territories and that a Black person cannot be a citizen.It did not break: it closed the road. If Congress could not legislate on this, no compromise by law was possible anymore.

What the four had in common

None of them decided whether slavery was acceptable: all four decided where. Each one bought time by postponing exactly the same question, and every time new territory arrived, the question came back untouched.

And here the vocabulary of Lesson 6.1 is needed. Lincoln’s election in November 1860 is the immediate cause of secession: it is what set it off, and the dates prove it. But Lincoln did not propose to abolish slavery where it already existed; he proposed to stop it from spreading to the territories. If that was enough to break the Union, it is because the structural cause had gone unresolved for forty years. An answer that only says “Lincoln won” has answered half the question.

3Two versions of the same event

So far, laws and court decisions. But a compromise about “slavery in the territories” is about real people, and in one case we have both versions: that of the man who held her as property, and her own. Harriet Jacobs was born enslaved in Edenton, North Carolina, in 1813. In June 1835, at 21, she left. Dr. James Norcom, who held her as property, published a notice. Twenty-six years later, by then free, she published a book.

📜 Document A · The notice

Document
Runaway notice placed by Dr. James Norcom
Place and date
American Beacon, Norfolk, Virginia, July 4, 1835
Kind of source
Primary · newspaper advertisement. Excerpts; the physical description is summarized in brackets.

$100 REWARD. Will be given for the apprehension and delivery of my Servant Girl HARRIET.

[A description follows: she is 21 years old, about five feet four inches tall, light-skinned; she speaks easily, is a good seamstress, and is used to dressing well.]

As this girl absconded from the plantation of my son without any known cause or provocation, it is probable she designs to transport herself to the North. […]

All persons are hereby forewarned against harboring or entertaining her, or being in any way instrumental in her escape, under the most rigorous penalties of the law. JAMES NORCOM.

Notice what counts as information: age, height, skin, voice, clothes. They are the features that serve to identify a piece of property and recover it. The reason for leaving does not appear, because for the writer there is none.

📜 Document B · What she wrote

Document
Harriet Jacobs, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl, Written by Herself
Place and date
Boston, 1861. Chapters V and XXI.
Kind of source
Primary · autobiography, written 26 years after the events
Note
The first passage refers to sexual harassment.

Chapter V

But I now entered on my fifteenth year—a sad epoch in the life of a slave girl. My master began to whisper foul words in my ear. […] I turned from him with disgust and hatred. But he was my master.

Chapter XXI

The garret was only nine feet long and seven wide. The highest part was three feet high, and sloped down abruptly to the loose board floor. There was no admission for either light or air. […] Southerners have the habit of stopping and talking in the streets, and I heard many conversations not intended to meet my ears.

The first passage answers “without any known cause or provocation.” The second answers “transport herself to the North”: she did not go north. She hid in the garret of her grandmother’s house, in the same town, a few steps from Norcom’s house, and stayed there seven years, hearing through a crack how the neighbors took it for granted that she was already in the free states.

Neither one lies about the checkable facts

This is the hard part of the lesson, and what the test asks most. Norcom does not invent her height or her age: he describes exactly the person he is hunting. Jacobs does not deny leaving or being a seamstress. The difference is not in the facts; it is in what was considered worth writing down. A source can be accurate in everything it says and still leave out the one thing that explains what it describes.

4What was written and what happened

The war ended in 1865 and settled by force what forty years of laws had not. Then came Reconstruction, the part that is most often oversimplified. In five years the Constitution changed more than in the previous seventy-five, with three amendments in a row (Lesson 5.1). Read them slowly and three things appear that the usual summary skips:

Three readings of the Reconstruction amendments
AmendmentWhat the usual summary skips
Thirteenth (1865)It has an exception: slavery and involuntary servitude are banned “except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted.”
Fourteenth (1868)Its Section 2 included its own penalty: a state that denied the vote would lose representation in the House in proportion. It was never applied, not once.
Fifteenth (1870)It does not grant the vote. It forbids denying it for three specific reasons (race, color, previous servitude), and it is aimed at governments. Any other requirement stayed standing.

On paper, then, the question was settled in 1870. Look at what happened.

📜 Document C · Abram Colby testifies

Document
Testimony of Abram Colby before the Joint Select Committee of Congress on conditions in the former Confederate states
Place and date
Taken in Atlanta, October 1871; published in the committee’s report, 1872
Who he was
Born enslaved; a barber; elected to the Georgia legislature in 1868
Note
Describes violence.
Kind of source
Primary · sworn testimony. Excerpts.

On the 29th of October 1869, [the Klansmen] broke my door open, took me out of bed, took me to the woods and whipped me three hours or more and left me for dead. They said to me, “Do you think you will ever vote another damned Radical ticket?” I said, “If there was an election tomorrow, I would vote the Radical ticket.” […]

Question: What is the character of those men who were engaged in whipping you? — Some are first-class men in our town. One is a lawyer, one a doctor, and some are farmers. […]

About two days before they whipped me they offered me $5,000 to go with them and said they would pay me $2,500 in cash if I would let another man go to the legislature in my place. […]

[Asked whether he had campaigned in his county:] No man can make a free speech in my county.

Here is the exact point, and it is not the one it seems

The easy conclusion is that the amendments “didn’t work.” Look more precisely: no official turned Colby away at any registration desk. The Fifteenth forbids the state to deny the vote, and the state did not deny it to Colby: a lawyer and a doctor from his own town stopped him, at night, in the woods. The three amendments are written against governments, and that violence was not carried out by a government. That gap is not a drafting error: it is where the tool reached its limit, and that is why Congress then passed the Enforcement Acts of 1870 and 1871.

The curve, in two series of numbers

The documents say what they say. Now the data, which is practice SSP.10: first describe the curve, then propose what explains it, and do the two separately.

The second series measures something else: not how many were elected, but how many could vote. In 1898 Louisiana adopted a constitution with a poll tax, a literacy test, and a “grandfather clause” that excused from both anyone whose father or grandfather could vote before January 1, 1867.

Registered Black voters in Louisiana

Source: Louisiana voter registration records, as reported by The Historic New Orleans Collection and 64 Parishes.

From 130,344 to 1,342 in eight years. The second bar is barely visible on this scale: it is about 1% of the first. One fact complicates the easy reading: the same measures also cut white registration considerably, because many poor white voters did not qualify under the grandfather clause. That does not change whom the measures were designed against (the debates of the 1898 convention say so openly), but it forces a precise statement: the measure was targeted, and it also had effects its authors did not seek.

Describing is not explaining

“Between 1896 and 1904 Black voter registration in Louisiana fell from 130,344 to 1,342” is a description, and it stands on the graph alone. “It fell because the 1898 constitution imposed requirements from which white voters were excused” is an explanation, and it needs the text of that constitution. The test asks for both, and mixing them up is the most common mistake.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The Senate count (20 minutes). Explain in five sentences why admitting Kansas was a national crisis, using the words House, Senate, and population. Check: your answer must mention two senators per state.
  • Two sources, one event (30 minutes). Make two columns: what Document A records and what Document B records about the same events. Mark the facts both share. Check: the shared facts are the firmest ones.

📘Key words

Missouri Compromise
1820 deal that admitted Missouri (slave) and Maine (free) and drew the 36°30′ line.
Popular sovereignty
Letting a territory’s settlers vote on whether to allow slavery (1854).
Secession
The withdrawal of Southern states from the Union (1860–1861).
Reconstruction
The period after the Civil War (1865–1877) when the South was rebuilt and the Constitution was amended.
Grandfather clause
A rule excusing from voting requirements anyone whose ancestors could vote before 1867.
Description
A statement of what the data show, without saying why.
Explanation
A statement of why the data look that way; it needs evidence beyond the data.
