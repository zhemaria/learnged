When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

Before you start

Lesson 7.1 was about curves and calculations. This one is about people counted: how many there are, how old, where they are, and why they move. And it starts close to home, because the first fact of the lesson can be checked by looking out the window.

1The average that hides the country

Population density is a division: people divided by area. The United States has about 37 people per square kilometer. Japan, about 330. Greenland, less than one. Now do the same calculation for Chicago.

The calculation

1Chicago has about 2.7 million people in about 590 square kilometers.

22,700,000 ÷ 590 ≈ 4,576

✓More than one hundred times the average density of the country it is in.

The lesson of this section

Both figures are correct and neither lies. What happens is that a national average describes no one: it is calculated by spreading the whole population over the whole territory, including the deserts of Nevada and the mountains of Alaska, where almost no one lives. You saw this in Topic 2 with measures of center; here it comes back on a map, with the same warning: before interpreting an average, ask what is being divided by what.

How this shows up on the test

You will get a density map or a table of countries and be asked for a conclusion. The right answer is almost never the one that generalizes. If a country has a low average density and you conclude “it is empty,” you have fallen for it: it may have most of its people in three cities. Egypt is the classic example, with almost all of its population along the banks of a single river, the Nile.

2Reading the future in a pyramid

A population pyramid is a bar graph turned on its side: each bar is an age group, men on the left and women on the right, the youngest at the bottom and the oldest at the top. What matters is not the figure on each bar: it is the shape. And today’s shape tells you what will happen in twenty years, because those at the bottom today will be in the middle then.

What each shape predicts for the next twenty years
ShapeWhat you seeWhat it needs nowWhat it faces in twenty years
Expansive (triangle)Very wide base, narrow top: many children are born and few people reach old age.Schools, vaccines, maternal care, food.A wave of young people looking for their first job. If there is no work, emigration.
Stationary (column)Young and adult groups of similar size: each generation replaces the last.Keeping what it already has.Few sudden changes. The most stable of the three.
Constrictive (urn)Base narrower than the middle: fewer children are born than needed to replace the population.Nursing homes, pensions, health care for older people.Fewer workers supporting more retirees. Usually answered with immigration, a later retirement age, or both.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The whole reasoning in one sentence

Those in this bar today will be in that bar in twenty years. Find the widest bar, move it twenty years up, and ask what those people will need when they get there. With that you can answer any pyramid question without memorizing anything.

Two traps

First: a pyramid shows ages, not quality of life. A wide base does not mean poverty, even though they often go together; saying so is an assumption, not a reading. Second: a notch in the middle of a pyramid almost always has a historical cause. A war, a famine, or a mass emigration shows up as a gap in the matching age group. That notch is history drawn on a population graph, and the test sometimes asks about exactly that.

3Why people leave and why they arrive

Every migration is explained with two lists: what pushes people to leave a place and what pulls them toward another. They are called push factors and pull factors.

Push and pull factors
Push · what drives people outPull · what draws people in
Unemployment, low wages, povertyAvailable jobs and better wages
War, persecution, violencePolitical stability and safety
Lack of basic services or freedomsEducation and health care
Droughts, earthquakes, hurricanesFamily already there

They almost never act alone. People who leave usually have several push and several pull factors at once, so the interesting question is not which ones exist, but which weighed more. That question is not answered with a list: it is answered with a source.

You already read these letters in Lesson 1.1, to learn how to read a primary source. They come back here for another reason: they are a migration documented by the people who lived it. They are part of the Great Migration, the move of about six million African Americans from the rural South to the cities of the North and West between 1916 and 1970, the same population that later made possible the election of Oscar De Priest in Chicago (Lesson 6.2).

About the language of these letters. The people who wrote them had little schooling, and the spelling is irregular. It is shown exactly as it was printed in 1919, because it is evidence too: it tells us something about who wrote and in what conditions. The letters also use the racial words of 1917 (“the Race,” “Colored”), copied as they are.

Letter 1 · Sanford, Florida, April 27, 1917

Dear Sir: I have seen through the Chicago Defender that you and the people of Chicago are helping newcomers. I am asking you for some information about conditions in some small town near Chicago.

There are some families here thinking of moving up, and are desirous of knowing what to expect before leaving. Please state about treatment, work, rent and schools. Please answer at some spare time.

Letter 2 · Mobile, Alabama, April 25, 1917

Sir: I was reading in theat paper about the Colored race and while reading it I seen in it where cars would be here for the 15 of May which is one month from to day. Will you be so kind as to let me know where they are coming to and I will be glad to know because I am a poor woman and have a husband and five children living and three dead.

my husband only get $1.50 a day and pays $7.50 a month for house rent and can hardly feed me and his self and children. I am the mother of 8 children 25 years old and I want to get out of this dog hold because I dont know what I am raising them up for in this place and I want to get to Chicago where I know they will be raised.

Letter 3 · Ellisville, Mississippi, May 1, 1917

Wages here are so low can scarcely live We can buy enough to eat we only buy enough to Keep up alive I mean the greater part of the Race. Women wages are from $1.25 Some time as high, as $2.50. just some time for a whole week.

P. S. You can place my letter in Some of the Defender Colums but done use my name in print, for it might get back down here.

Letter 4 · New Orleans, Louisiana, April 22, 1917

under the head lines in the Chicage Defender of Saturday April 22-17 I red how some of us that goes up north are being treated, there is a few that have gone from this city north, and came back a few weeks, some say they came back on account of being to cold “The others Say they ware to pay so much to get work etc”

I would like to go north, and would rather be in some place, other then Chicago, or near Chicago.

What to do with them, and it is not summarizing

For each letter, write in two columns the push factors and the pull factors the writer mentions. Only the ones in the text: if a letter does not say why the person wants to leave, that box stays empty, and that is information too. After all four, see which factor repeats most. That is the weight, measured with the source and not with an impression.

Voluntary and forced, and why the difference matters

The two main kinds of migration
KindWhat defines itWhy the label matters
VoluntaryThe person decides to move in search of better opportunities. Pull factors dominate.It falls under each country’s ordinary immigration laws.
ForcedThe person has no reasonable alternative: war, persecution, or disaster. Push factors dominate.Someone who crosses a border fleeing persecution is a refugee, with protections under international law that voluntary migrants do not have. Someone forced to move inside their own country is an internally displaced person, who does not have that same status.

The line is not always clear, and that is test material

Is someone who leaves because there is no work migrating voluntarily? What if there has been no work in that region for ten years? What if a drought destroyed the harvest? These cases are seriously debated, partly because specific rights depend on the label. The test will not ask for your opinion: it will ask you to identify which factors are in the case and what kind they are.

4People are moving to cities

Urbanization is the rise in the share of the population that lives in cities. It is one of the biggest changes of recent history and it is still going on: about half of humanity now lives in cities (the exact figure depends on how “city” is defined), and in the United States the share is about 80% (2020 Census).

Common consequences of rapid urbanization, in both directions
In the city that receives peopleIn the countryside that empties
Pressure on housing: more demand with the same supply, and you know from Lesson 7.1 what happens to the price.Fewer farm workers, often answered with more machines.
More demand for transportation, water, sewers, and schools.Rural schools and hospitals that close for lack of users.
More economic activity and more job opportunities in one place.An aging population, because those who leave are usually young.

The last row connects the whole lesson

If mostly young people leave a region, that region’s pyramid becomes constrictive, and the pyramid of the city that receives them widens in the middle. Migration and pyramids are the same phenomenon seen two ways.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your county (20 minutes). Find the population and area of your county and of your state, and compute both densities. Check: they will be very different; explain why in one sentence.
  • The letters, in two columns (30 minutes). For each 1917 letter, list the push and pull factors the writer mentions. Check: leave a box empty if the letter does not say it.
  • A real pyramid (20 minutes). Find the population pyramid of a country you know (the Census Bureau publishes them). Say which shape it has and what it predicts for twenty years from now. Check: move the widest bar twenty years up.

📘Key words

Population density
People per unit of area (people per km²).
Population pyramid
A sideways bar graph of a population by age and sex.
Push factor
A condition that drives people to leave a place.
Pull factor
A condition that draws people to a place.
Refugee
A person who crosses a border fleeing persecution, war, or violence.
Internally displaced person
A person forced to move within their own country.
Urbanization
The rise in the share of people who live in cities.
