Lesson 5.2 · The election process: parties, campaigns, and the Electoral College

You do not vote for the President: you vote for electors. And a debate built on values is not settled with data.

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain how the Electoral College works and why the popular and electoral votes can split.

Before you start

The last lesson ended with an idea: a written right and an exercised right are not the same. Here we go into the exercise. And we enter ground where there is real disagreement: the Electoral College has been argued over for more than a century, and that argument is not settled with data. This course does not take a side; section 3 explains why and sets out both positions with the same care.

1The path of a presidential election

From the day someone announces a candidacy to the day of taking office, more than a year goes by, in five stages.

The five stages of a presidential election
StageWhat happens
1 · Primaries and caucusesEach party chooses its candidate, state by state. The parties do not compete with each other yet: the hopefuls inside each party do.
2 · National conventionThe party officially names its candidate and approves its platform.
3 · General campaignNow the parties compete, with debates and advertising.
4 · Election Day (November)Voters choose, in practice, their state’s electors.
5 · Electoral College and countElectors vote in December, Congress counts the votes in January, and the winner takes office on January 20.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

A detail that surprises almost everyone

When you mark your ballot for President, technically you do not vote for the candidate: you vote for a slate of electors pledged to that candidate. That detail, which looks like a technicality, is the key to section 2.

Why two parties?

The Constitution does not mention political parties. The two-party system comes from the rules: in most U.S. elections one winner takes the whole seat, so a third party that wins 20% of the vote everywhere can end up with no seats at all. Third parties still matter: they raise issues that the big parties later adopt, and they can change who wins. Theodore Roosevelt’s Progressive Party took 27% of the vote in 1912; Ross Perot took about 19% in 1992. Neither won the presidency.

2How the Electoral College works

There are 538 electoral votes, and it takes 270 (more than half) to win. The number comes from a simple sum:

Where 538 comes from

1435 representatives (one for each House seat)

2+ 100 senators (two per state)

3+ 3 for the District of Columbia (Twenty-third Amendment, 1961)

✓= 538. Each state gets as many electoral votes as it has members of Congress. Illinois: 17 representatives + 2 senators = 19.

The three rules that produce all the effects
RuleConsequence
Each state adds its two senators to its representativesNo state has fewer than three electoral votes, however small. A small state has more weight per person than a large one: in Wyoming there are about 190,000 people per electoral vote; in California, about 730,000.
Almost every state gives all its electoral votes to whoever wins that stateWinning a state by a little or by a lot gives the same result. The loser’s votes in that state add nothing.
Only 270 are needed, wherever they come fromCampaigns focus on the few states where the result is close (“swing states”).

Two exceptions worth knowing

Maine and Nebraska do not use winner-take-all: they give some electoral votes by congressional district. They prove that the rule is not in the Constitution: each state decides how to give its electors. Changing the rule would not need an amendment; a state law would be enough.

Why the popular vote and the electoral vote can split

An example with made-up numbers, to see the mechanism

AState A, 10 electoral votes: candidate X wins 5,100,000 to 4,900,000. X takes all 10.

BState B, 10 electoral votes: candidate Y wins 9,000,000 to 1,000,000. Y takes all 10.

=Electoral votes: a tie, 10 to 10. Popular vote: Y has 4,900,000 + 9,000,000 = 13,900,000; X has 5,100,000 + 1,000,000 = 6,100,000.

✓Y’s extra votes in state B count for nothing. That is not a quirk of the example: it is how the system is built.

In U.S. history, the candidate with the most popular votes has lost the presidency in 1876, 1888, 2000, and 2016, plus the special case of 1824, which the House of Representatives decided because no one had a majority of electoral votes.

3The debate over the Electoral College

Here the course stops and changes register. Everything above is checkable fact. What follows is an open debate in which informed people, in good faith, hold opposite positions.

Why the course does not take a side

Not out of caution: because the disagreement is not settled by data. It rests on a question that comes before the data: what should a presidential election represent, people or states? Whoever answers “people” reaches one conclusion; whoever answers “states”, the opposite. Both answers can be defended, and choosing between them is a values decision that belongs to you. What this course owes you is to know both arguments well enough to state them without distorting them.

The two sides of the debate
Arguments for keeping itArguments for changing it
It protects the weight of small states. It matches the deal of 1787, in which the Senate already gave two seats to every state, whatever its size.Votes do not weigh the same. Because of the minimum of three electors, a vote in a small state has more electoral weight than one in a large state.
It forces broad geographic support. A candidate cannot win by piling up votes only in the most populated areas.It can elect the candidate with fewer votes. It has happened several times; for those who believe the President represents people, that is hard to justify.
It contains recount disputes. A close result is settled inside one state, not with a national recount.Millions of votes count for nothing. With winner-take-all, a vote for the loser in a state adds no electoral vote.
It reinforces federalism. The states make the choice, consistent with the rest of the constitutional design.It concentrates campaigns in a few states. States whose result is known in advance get little attention, few promises, and little turnout effort.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Notice what almost everyone misses

The first argument on each side is about the same fact: small states weigh more per person. One side calls it protecting the federal deal; the other calls it unequal votes. They do not argue about whether the fact exists: they argue about whether it should. Seeing that is exactly the skill of Lesson 1.3.

There are proposals for change of different reach. Some would need an amendment; others would not. The best known is the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact: states that join promise to give their electors to the winner of the national popular vote, but only once states with at least 270 electoral votes have joined. In April 2026 Virginia joined, bringing the total to 18 states plus D.C., with 222 electoral votes. The course mentions it so you know the debate is alive, without recommending anything.

4Who votes, and what can be said from that

Turnout is not spread evenly across groups, and the pattern repeats election after election. These are real figures for the 2024 presidential election: the percent of citizens of voting age who said they voted.

By age

By education

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey, Voting and Registration Supplement, November 2024.

The pattern is steady: the older the group, and the more schooling, the higher the turnout. In 2024 the oldest group voted at a rate 27 percentage points higher than the youngest.

Here you must apply Lesson 2.1, with discipline

These graphs show correlations. They do not say that studying makes people vote, or that age causes turnout. Remember the third explanation of any correlation: there may be other factors pushing education, income, and the habit of voting at the same time, such as stable housing, free time, or how easy it is to register. The graph cannot choose among those explanations.

A wrong reading: “Young people don’t vote because they don’t care.” A correct reading: “Turnout among 18- to 24-year-olds is lower; the causes may be several, and this graph does not tell them apart.”

5Campaign propaganda

Here Lesson 1.3 comes back, now in an ad. The techniques are the same ones the two weeklies of 1905 used, with a bigger budget.

Five common persuasive techniques in campaigns
TechniqueHow to recognize it
Fear appealDescribes what will happen if the other side wins, in vague and alarming terms.
Plain folksPresents the candidate as one of us, so that his views seem like common sense.
TestimonialA respected or likable person endorses the candidate, without giving reasons.
Loaded wordsWords that judge while seeming to describe (signal 1 of Lesson 1.3).
Selective contrastCompares the best of one side with the worst of the other; both true, both chosen.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your state’s electors (15 minutes). Find how many representatives your state has and add its two senators. Check: for Illinois, 17 + 2 = 19.
  • Both sides, fairly (30 minutes). Write one paragraph with the best argument for keeping the Electoral College and one with the best argument for changing it, as their defenders would say it. Check: a person who holds each view should be able to sign your paragraph.
  • A real ad (20 minutes). Choose a political ad from any party. Name its main technique and write what concrete proposal, if any, it contains. Check: a proposal can be checked later; a feeling cannot.

📘Key words

Primary election
An election in which a party’s voters choose its candidate.
Caucus
A meeting of party members to choose a candidate, used in some states instead of a primary.
Elector
A person chosen in each state to cast that state’s electoral votes.
Electoral College
The 538 electors who formally elect the President and Vice President.
Winner-take-all
The rule by which a state gives all its electors to whoever wins that state.
Swing state
A state where the result is close and could go either way.
Turnout
The share of eligible people who actually vote.
Propaganda
Messages designed to persuade, often by emotion rather than evidence.
