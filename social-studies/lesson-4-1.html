Lesson 4.1 · The legislative branch: how a bill becomes a law

Two chambers built to move at different speeds, and a path with more side doors than steps.

🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Compare the House and the Senate: size, term, who they represent, and their special powers.
  • Explain impeachment: the House accuses, the Senate judges.
  • Put in order the steps of a bill and name the points where it can die, including the committee and the filibuster.
  • Follow a real law, the Civil Rights Act of 1964, through those steps.

Before you start

Topic 3 built the frame: where authority comes from, how power is divided, and how the Union and the states share it. Topic 4 goes inside each of the three branches, in the order of the Constitution: Article I (Congress), Article II (the President), and Article III (the courts). This lesson starts with the branch that makes the laws, and with the question the test asks again and again: how does a bill really become a law, and where does it usually die?

1Two chambers that are not alike

Congress has two chambers because in 1787 the founders had to settle a fight between large and small states. The solution (called the Great Compromise) was not to choose: one chamber represents the population, and the other represents the states.

The House of Representatives and the Senate, compared
House of RepresentativesSenate
Members435, divided among the states by population100, two per state, whatever its size
TermTwo years; the whole House is elected every two yearsSix years; one-third is elected every two years

Only this chamber can…impeach (bring charges against) an officialapprove treaties, by two-thirds
Only this chamber can…choose the President if no one wins a majority in the Electoral Collegehold the impeachment trial, and remove by two-thirds

How to remember impeachment

The House accuses; the Senate judges. It is like charges and a trial: two separate acts, in two separate rooms. Being impeached does not remove anyone from office; it only opens the trial. This is one of the most common mix-ups on the test and in the news.

Why the terms are different

Two years make a representative think about the next election almost all the time, which keeps him close to the mood of his district. Six years give a senator room to cast an unpopular vote and survive. It is a design for the two chambers to react at different speeds to the same pressure.

2The path, and the side doors

The diagram usually taught shows a staircase going up. This one also shows the side doors, which is where most bills leave.

The committee decides almost everything

Look at the left side. There are several exits where a bill dies before it even reaches the President, and the first one is the deadliest: if the committee does not act, there is no debate, no vote, and no news. A committee can simply leave a bill off its schedule, and that kills it without anyone voting against it. It is a huge and hard-to-see power: it never shows up in any vote count, because the vote never happens.

At the end of the path is the veto, which is not the last word. If the President vetoes, the bill goes back to Congress, and if both chambers gather two-thirds, it becomes law without the President’s signature. That is the checks and balances of Lesson 3.2 at work: the executive can stop the legislature, and the legislature can go over it with a very large majority.

One more door, only in the Senate: the filibuster. A group of senators can keep talking to block a vote. To end the debate (cloture), 60 of the 100 senators must agree today; in 1964 the rule was two-thirds of those voting. So in the Senate, a bill often needs 60 votes, not just a majority, to come to a vote at all.

3Follow a real bill: the Civil Rights Act of 1964

The diagram is clean. Reality is not. This is the path of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which banned discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in jobs and public places, laid over the same steps, with dates and votes checked against official records.

What this path teaches

Thirteen months from the request to the signature. Two committees that could have buried it: one was skipped, and the other gave in to the threat of being skipped. A two-month filibuster that was broken only by gathering votes from both parties. And not once was there a veto: the danger was always inside Congress, not between Congress and the President.

A note about the numbers

Sources give the length of the filibuster as 54, 57, 60, or 75 days, depending on whether they count working days, calendar days, or the whole debate on the bill. Here we say “about sixty days,” following the Senate’s own history office. That such a specific number changes with who counts it is exactly what Topic 2 taught: before you quote a number, know what it measures.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Your members of Congress (20 minutes). Find who represents you in the House and your two senators (house.gov and senate.gov, “Find your representative”). Write their names, their parties, and when each one is next up for election. Check: your representative is up every two years; your senators, in different years.
  • Follow a bill today (30 minutes). At congress.gov, find a bill on a topic you care about and write which step it has reached and what its next door is. Check: most bills you find will still be “in committee”: that is the normal result, not a mistake.
  • The two speeds (15 minutes). Write a paragraph explaining why the founders gave representatives two-year terms and senators six-year terms. Check: your paragraph should say what each length makes easier.

📘Key words

Bill
A proposed law, before it is passed and signed.
Committee
A small group of members that studies bills on one subject and decides which ones move forward.
Veto / override
The President’s refusal to sign; Congress can override it with two-thirds of each chamber.
Impeachment
The House formally charges an official; the Senate holds the trial.
Filibuster / cloture
Blocking a Senate vote with endless debate / the vote that ends it (60 senators today).
Great Compromise
The 1787 deal: the House by population, the Senate with two per state.
Civil Rights Act of 1964
The law that banned discrimination by race, color, religion, sex, or national origin in jobs and public places.
