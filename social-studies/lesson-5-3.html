Lesson 5.3 · Taxes, the budget, and fiscal policy

A tax is measured against income, not the purchase; and a smaller deficit still makes the debt grow.

Two things to notice in that table

First: the 6.2% and the 1.45% do not go to the general fund but to two specific programs, which is why they appear with their names. Second: the Illinois line never travels to Washington. That is the federalism of Lesson 3.3, showing up on a pay stub.

2Progressive, regressive, proportional

The three words describe what percent of a person’s income the tax takes as income goes up. That is the key almost everyone misses: it is measured against income, not against the purchase.

The three shapes of a tax
TypeAs income rises, the percent paid…Example
Progressivegoes upThe federal income tax, with its brackets
Proportional (flat)stays the sameIllinois’s 4.95% income tax
Regressivegoes downThe sales tax

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Why the sales tax is regressive if the rate is the same for everyone

The same rate, two different burdens (10% sales tax, monthly)

APerson A earns $2,000 and spends $1,600 on taxed goods. Tax: $160. That is 160 ÷ 2,000 = 8% of her income.

BPerson B earns $10,000 and spends $4,000 on taxed goods. Tax: $400, more dollars. But that is 400 ÷ 10,000 = 4% of his income.

✓Same rate, and A’s burden is twice B’s. People who earn less spend a larger share of what they earn; people who earn more save or invest part of it, and that part is not taxed at the register.

The subtle case: Social Security

Its rate is 6.2% for everyone, which looks proportional. But it is paid only up to a wage cap: $184,500 in 2026. Above that, nothing more is paid. Someone who earns $50,000 pays $3,100, which is 6.2% of income. Someone who earns $500,000 pays the maximum, $11,439, which is about 2.3% of income. The printed rate is the same; the effective rate falls. That is why it is classified as regressive, and why you must always look at the effective rate on income, not the printed one. (Medicare, by contrast, has no cap: 1.45% on every dollar, plus an extra 0.9% above $200,000.)

3Deficit and debt are not the same

They are mixed up in the news every day, and the difference is simple:

Deficit and debt, compared
DeficitDebt
What it measuresHow much more was spent than collected in one yearHow much is owed in total, added up
PeriodOne fiscal yearAll of history up to today
At home, it is like…what you overspent this monththe balance on your credit card
Real figure (November 2026)$2.0 trillion in fiscal year 2026 (CBO)Passed $40 trillion in August 2026 (Treasury)

Ten years of deficits, and the debt they leave behind (made-up numbers; what matters is the shape):

Deficit each year

Total debt

The deficit bars go down in five of the ten years. The debt line does not go down even once. That is the whole difference: as long as there is a deficit, however small, the debt keeps growing.

The sentence that clears up most headlines

“The deficit went down” and “the debt went down” do not mean the same thing, and the first does not imply the second. A smaller deficit still adds to the debt; for the debt to go down, the government has to spend less than it collects, which is a surplus. And this connects with section 1: interest on the debt is what all the earlier deficits cost together. The larger the debt, the larger that part of the budget that buys no service.

4Fiscal policy: speed up or slow down

The government can use taxes and spending to influence the economy. There are only two directions. But first, it helps to know what is being acted on: the economy does not grow in a straight line, but in cycles.

The two directions of fiscal policy
Expansionary · speed upContractionary · slow down
What it doesSpend more, cut taxes, or bothSpend less, raise taxes, or both
When it is usedWhen the economy cools: high unemployment, little activityWhen the economy overheats and prices rise fast
How it worksPuts more money in circulation, which supports spending and jobsTakes money out of circulation and cools demand
Its riskA bigger deficit, and it can push prices upIt can brake too hard and cause unemployment

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

Here there is real disagreement, and the course does not settle it

How much to use each policy, how strongly, and when, is argued among economists and among parties, and that argument mixes technical judgments with judgments about what a government should do. What the test asks, and what this lesson asks, is that you recognize which direction a measure pushes: does it put money into circulation or take it out? That is description, and it can be done without taking a side.

📘Key words

Progressive tax
A tax that takes a higher percent of income as income rises.
Proportional (flat) tax
A tax that takes the same percent of every income.
Regressive tax
A tax that takes a lower percent of income as income rises.
Effective rate
The tax paid as a percent of total income.
Deficit
How much more the government spends than it collects in one year.
Surplus
How much more the government collects than it spends in one year.
Debt
The total the government owes, the sum of past deficits minus surpluses.
Fiscal policy
Using taxes and government spending to influence the economy.
