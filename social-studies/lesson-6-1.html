Before you start

Topics 3, 4, and 5 were about how government works: what the founding documents say, how power is divided, and where public money comes from. Topic 6 changes the question: not how it works, but why it came to be that way. This first lesson goes back to the years before and after 1776 and uses them to train one skill above all: telling what came after from what came because of.

1After is not because of

Topics 3 to 5 were about how government works. Topic 6 changes the question: why it came to be that way. And here a problem appears that is not about history but about reasoning, and the test measures it directly (practice SSP.3): the difference between what came after and what came because of.

The mistake has had a name for twenty centuries. The Romans called it post hoc, ergo propter hoc: “after this, therefore because of this.” It means taking it as proven that A caused B because B happened after A. You drink tea and the next day your cold is gone. Did the tea cure it? Maybe; or maybe the cold would have lasted exactly that long anyway. The order of events is not enough. Three questions are needed, and they work the same for the tea and for the Revolution:

Three questions before saying “A caused B”
QuestionWhat it checks
1 · Is there a mechanism?I can explain how A produces B, step by step, with no jumps. If I can’t, I have a coincidence.
2 · Does a source say so?Someone at the time connects the two in writing. If only I see it, two hundred years later, it is my hypothesis.
3 · Would B have happened without A?If B was going to happen anyway, A is not the cause. At most, it is the moment it happened.

When a cause survives those three questions, you still have to say what kind it is. The test tells three kinds apart, and mixing them up is the second most common mistake.

Three kinds of cause, with the example of the Revolution
KindWhat it isExample
PreconditionThe ground. It sets nothing off by itself, but without it nothing else would have been possible.The colonies had governed themselves with their own assemblies for a century and a half.
Structural (long-term) causeA deep conflict that lasts for years, is not resolved, and stays ready to flare up again.Who has the power to tax the colonies.
Immediate cause (trigger)The specific event that sets off the reaction. It makes the headlines, and it explains least on its own.The Boston Tea Party, December 1773.

The practical rule

An immediate cause explains when something happened. A structural cause explains why it happened. If an answer on the test names only the immediate cause, it has answered half the question.

21765–1766: what was resolved and what was not

The best place to see the difference between the two kinds of cause is one specific year and the answer that came five months later.

In March 1765 the British Parliament passed the Stamp Act. It taxed almost every paper used in the colonies for legal and business purposes: newspapers, contracts, licenses, deeds, even playing cards. It was the first internal tax London collected directly, without going through the colonial assemblies. It came after a very long war against France (1754–1763) that had left Britain with an enormous debt, and the logic in London was simple: the war defended the colonies, so the colonies should pay their share.

The reaction came in two kinds, and they should never be confused. In the streets, the Sons of Liberty burned effigies and attacked tax collectors’ houses until almost none dared to do the job. In a meeting hall in New York, in October, delegates from nine colonies did something very different.

📜 Document A · The colonies answer the Stamp Act

Document
Declaration of Rights and Grievances, Stamp Act Congress
Place and date
New York, October 19, 1765
Authors
Delegates from nine of the thirteen colonies
Kind of source
Primary · political declaration. Fourteen points; three are quoted.

1st. That his majesty's subjects in these colonies, owe the same allegiance to the crown of Great Britain that is owing from his subjects born within the realm, and all due subordination to that august body, the parliament of Great Britain.

3d. That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.

4th. That the people of these colonies are not, and from their local circumstances cannot be, represented in the House of Commons in Great-Britain.

Read them in that order and a contradiction appears. The first grants Parliament “all due subordination.” The third and fourth take away its power to tax. The document does not say where the line between the two lies, because it does not know: no one did. Notice also what it does not say. It does not ask for independence or mention it. Anyone who reads an independence movement here is putting 1776 inside 1765.

On March 18, 1766, Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. In the colonies there were bells, bonfires, and toasts to the king. That same day, the same Parliament passed a second, much shorter law that almost no one celebrated, because almost no one read it.

📜 Document B · Parliament answers back

Document
The Declaratory Act (American Colonies Act)
Place and date
Parliament of Great Britain, Westminster, March 18, 1766: the same day the Stamp Act was repealed
Kind of source
Primary · law

…That the said colonies and plantations in America have been, are, and of right ought to be, subordinate unto, and dependent upon the imperial crown and parliament of Great Britain; and that the King's majesty, by and with the advice and consent of the lords spiritual and temporal, and commons of Great Britain, in parliament assembled, had, hath, and of right ought to have, full power and authority to make laws and statutes of sufficient force and validity to bind the colonies and people of America, subjects of the crown of Great Britain, in all cases whatsoever.

II. …That all resolutions, votes, orders, and proceedings, in any of the said colonies or plantations, whereby the power and authority of the parliament of Great Britain, to make laws and statutes as aforesaid, is denied, or drawn into question, are, and are hereby declared to be, utterly null and void to all intents and purposes whatsoever.

Section II cancels Document A in advance, in writing, and anything like it.

What happened in March 1766, stated precisely
What was resolvedWhat was left untouched
The tax was withdrawn; no one would pay for stamps.The power to tax, reaffirmed in full and in writing.
The celebrations were sincere: the immediate conflict really was over.The power to make laws “in all cases whatsoever,” with no exception.
Loyal petition seemed to work, which strengthened the moderates.The deep dispute, unresolved and ready to flare up as soon as money was needed again.

The whole lesson in one pair of dates

The immediate cause was resolved and the structural cause was left alive. That is why 1766 is not a happy ending: it is a truce over a problem nobody touched. And that is why, when taxes came back in 1767 and 1773, the conflict did not have to start over. It was waiting.

Careful with the easy conclusion

It is tempting to say the Declaratory Act “caused” the Revolution. Apply question 3: between 1766 and 1775 there are ten years, more tax laws, a riot, a military occupation, and a war that began without anyone declaring it. A structural cause makes an outcome possible; it does not produce it alone. Saying “it caused it” skips ten years of decisions that could have gone another way.

What a timeline can do, and what it cannot

It tells you what existed at each moment. It does not tell you what came from what. If you list these events by date, you get one order. If you list them by cause, some pairs fall apart, and some causes jump over ten years while neighboring events have nothing to do with each other. The two lists do not match, and that mismatch is the content of this lesson.

4Why the Articles failed

Once the war was won, there was a country to govern. The first attempt was the Articles of Confederation, drafted in 1777 and ratified in 1781, and they were weak on purpose: people who had just broken with a distant, powerful government were not going to build another one. The Articles created “a firm league of friendship” among thirteen sovereign states, with a one-chamber Congress, one vote per state, and very little else.

In 1786 that stopped being a theoretical problem. In western Massachusetts, farmers in debt (many of them war veterans who had not been paid) closed by force the courts that were seizing their farms. The movement took the name of one of its leaders, Daniel Shays. The national government could do nothing: it had no money, no army of its own, and no authority to enter a state.

📜 Document C · Washington writes to Knox

Document
Letter from George Washington to Henry Knox
Place and date
Mount Vernon, Virginia, December 26, 1786
Context
A private letter. Washington had been retired from public office for three years. Knox had written to him about the uprising in Massachusetts.
Kind of source
Primary · private letter (Founders Online, National Archives). Excerpts.

In this, as in most other matter[s], we are too slow. When this spirit first dawned, probably it migh[t] easily have been checked; but it is scarcely within the reach of human ken, at this moment, to say when—where—or how it will end. There are combustibles in every State, which a spark may set fire to. […]

[…] apropos, what prevented the Eastern states from attending the September meeting at Annapolis? […] What are the prevailing sentiments of the one now proposed to be held at Philadelphia, in May next? […]

If the powers are inadequate amend or alter them, but do not let us sink into the lowest state of humiliation & contempt, and become a byword in all the earth. […]

I ask this question for information, I know nothing of the facts.

Three different things in a few lines. The first paragraph is alarm. The third is a political program: if the powers are not enough, change them. And the last sentence is the most valuable thing in the document. Washington writes with indignation about the rebels, and at the same time admits in writing that he does not know what happened: what he believes, Knox told him, and Knox is not neutral either. When a source states its own limit, take it at its word: this letter proves very well what political leaders feared in December 1786, and almost nothing about what the Massachusetts farmers did or wanted. For that, you would need their own sources.

The middle paragraph is a chronological trap

Many textbooks say Shays’ Rebellion led to the Philadelphia Convention. Check the dates with this letter in front of you. The call for Philadelphia came out of the Annapolis meeting in September 1786. The rebellion began closing courts in late August, and its most serious moment, the attack on the Springfield arsenal, was in January 1787, a month after this letter. Washington asks about Annapolis in the past tense and about Philadelphia in the future. So the honest statement is not “the rebellion caused the Convention.” It is this: the call already existed, and the rebellion gave it urgency, attendance, and arguments.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Two lists (30 minutes). Copy the timeline of section 3 in order of date. Then draw arrows only where the first event explains the second, and test each arrow with the three questions. Check: you should end up with fewer arrows than pairs.
  • Immediate and structural (20 minutes). Choose a recent news event (a strike, a law, a protest). Write its immediate cause and its structural cause in one sentence each. Check: the immediate cause says when; the structural cause says why.
  • The limit of a source (15 minutes). Write two things Document C proves and two things it does not. Check: everything it proves is about Washington.
