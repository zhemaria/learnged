Before you start

Lesson 2.2 taught you that a single number never describes a set of data, and that choosing between the mean and the median is already a one-sided decision. This lesson closes the topic with the three calculations that appear most on the Social Studies test: the percent, the scale, and the density. All three are simple divisions, and all three are missed for the same reason: dividing by the wrong number.

A note on map scales: the map work here is the same as similar figures and scale drawings in Math Level 3. If you already did that, you are not learning anything new: you are using it on a map.

1Percent change and the base

There is only one formula, and it is worth writing the same way every time:

percent change = ( new value − old value ) ÷ old value × 100

The highlighted part is the only thing people get wrong. You divide by the value you start from: not by the value you end at, not by the difference, and not by the sum of the two.

Why the base changes everything

1An item costs $80. It goes up 25%: 80 × 1.25 = $100.

2The next month it goes down 25%: 100 × 0.75 = $75.

3It does not go back to $80. The increase was figured on 80 and the decrease on 100: the same percent of different bases gives different amounts.

✓To go back from 100 to 80 you would need a 20% decrease, not 25%.

The question that prevents the mistake

Before you divide, ask out loud: a percent of what? The answer to that question is what you divide by.

2Percentage points are not percents

This difference appears on the test and in any economic news, and mixing it up changes how big the headline sounds. If unemployment goes from 4% to 6%:

You can sayCalculationResult
It rose 2 percentage points6 − 42 points

Both sentences describe exactly the same fact, and both are correct. But “it rose 2 points” sounds small, and “it rose 50%” sounds big.

And Lesson 1.3 comes back

Someone who wants to make the problem look small will say two points. Someone who wants to alarm will say fifty percent. Neither one lies. They chose, just like the person who chooses between the mean and the median. The defense is the same: ask why that form and not the other.

3How much did it really go up?

This is the economic calculation the test asks most often, and the one most often missed, because the intuitive answer is wrong. Over ten years, a salary goes from $32,000 to $41,600 a year. In the same period, a price index (like the Consumer Price Index, which measures what the same basket of goods costs) goes from 100 to 128.

How much did it really go up?

1Salary: (41,600 − 32,000) ÷ 32,000 = 0.30 → 30%.

2Prices: (128 − 100) ÷ 100 = 0.28 → 28%.

✗The temptation: subtract, 30 − 28 = 2%. Wrong, even though it is close.

3Correct: bring the final salary back to starting-year dollars: 41,600 ÷ 1.28 = 32,500. Then (32,500 − 32,000) ÷ 32,000 = 1.56%.

✓Buying power went up 1.56%, not 2%. The two percents were figured on different bases (one on a salary, the other on a price index), so they cannot be subtracted, just as you cannot subtract meters from pounds. Bring both amounts to the same unit: dollars of the starting year.

4The scale of a map

A map scale of 1 : 50,000 means that one unit on the map stands for 50,000 of the same units on the ground. One inch on paper is 50,000 inches on the ground; one centimeter is 50,000 centimeters. The scale has no units because it works with any unit, as long as both sides use the same one.

Two ways to give the same scale. The numeric scale (1 : 50,000) is a ratio. The graphic scale is the bar at the bottom, and it has an advantage: if the map is enlarged or shrunk when printed, the bar stretches with it and is still correct, while the ratio is not.

From centimeters on the map to kilometers on the ground

1On the printed map, measure the distance from the school to the clinic with a ruler: 8 cm.

2Multiply by the scale: 8 × 50,000 = 400,000 cm on the ground.

3Change to the unit the question asks for. 1 km = 100,000 cm, so 400,000 ÷ 100,000 = 4 km.

✓The mistake in this section is always about units, never about multiplying. Memorize one equivalence: 1 km = 100,000 cm. (In inches: 1 mile = 63,360 inches.)

5Population density

Density is a division and nothing more:

density = population ÷ area → people per km² (or per square mile)

The interesting part is not the arithmetic. It is that the most populated area is almost never the densest. Illustrative data, chosen so that the order flips:

Area C has almost three times as many people as B and is the least dense of the three. B has the fewest people and is the densest. Sorting by population and sorting by density give opposite lists.

What a density does not say

A density is a mean, and it has the same problem we saw in Lesson 2.2: it shares out equally what is not shared equally. An area with 200 people per km² could have everyone crowded into one city and the rest empty. The number cannot tell those two cases apart.

Remember Agnes Holbrook’s maps from Lesson 1.2. She colored each lot whole, “whether occupied by one person or one hundred,” and warned in writing that her maps give no clue to density. A document that looks like it measures density, by an author who says it does not: the same skill of Lesson 1.2 (what a source supports and what it does not), now with a number.

7Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • Ten percent changes (25 minutes). With the calculator, always grouping the subtraction in parentheses: from 40 to 52; from 80 to 60; from 12 to 15; from 250 to 200; from 1,200 to 1,500. Then, for each one, find the change needed to go back. Check: +30%, −25%, +25%, −20%, +25%; and to go back: −23.08%, +33.33%, −20%, +25%, −20%. They never match.
  • How much did it really go up? (25 minutes) A salary goes from $25,000 to $30,000 while prices go from 100 to 120. Calculate the change in the salary, in prices, and in buying power. Then write two lines explaining it to someone who thinks you can just subtract. Check: 20%, 20%, and 0%. Subtracting happens to work here, but try it with 32,000 → 41,600 and prices 100 → 128, and it fails.
  • Your city to scale (30 minutes). Get a printed map with a scale. Measure three distances and convert them to real kilometers or miles. Then look up the population and area of your city or county and calculate its density. Check: if one distance comes out a thousand times too big or too small, the problem is the unit conversion.

The three calculations of this lesson are simple divisions, and all three are missed for the same reason: dividing by the wrong number. The percent is divided by the starting value. The scale is converted, at the end, to the unit the question asks for. The density is divided by the area, and by nothing else.

📘Key words

Percent change
(new − old) ÷ old × 100.
Percentage points
The plain difference between two percents (6% − 4% = 2 points).
Price index (CPI)
A number that tracks what the same basket of goods costs over time.
Buying power
What money can actually buy, once price changes are taken into account.
Map scale
The ratio between a distance on the map and the real distance (1 : 50,000).
Population density
Population ÷ area; people per km² or per square mile.
Rate
An amount per unit of something else: per hour, per 100 people, per km².
