🎯What you will learn

When you finish this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Relate the uneven distribution of resources to trade patterns and to tension between countries.
  • Interpret a thematic map, knowing what it shows and what it leaves out.
  • Evaluate two opposed positions on an environmental policy by identifying each one’s evidence.
  • Defend a position you disagree with, and recognize a strong point of the other side.

Before you start

Lesson 7.1 was about markets and 7.2 about people. This one closes the topic with what lies under both: the land and what is taken from it. And it ends with the hardest exercise of the course, which is not about content but about character: defending well a position you do not share.

1Resources are not where people are

Geography spread resources around without asking anyone. Copper is where it is, so is cobalt, and fresh water does not fall equally everywhere. Almost all world trade, and many tensions between countries, come from that distribution. The chain has three links; see it whole before looking at any map.

Four concentrated resources and the dependence they create
ResourceWhere it is concentratedWhat it is needed for
OilA very large share of reserves is in a handful of countries, many of them in the Middle East.Transportation, plastics, fertilizers. Almost everything.
CopperChile is the world’s largest producer; Peru is also near the top.Electrical wiring, motors, construction.
CobaltMost of world production comes from the Democratic Republic of the Congo.Batteries for phones and electric vehicles.
Rare earth elementsMining, and above all processing, is highly concentrated in China.Magnets, electronics, wind turbines, defense.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The rare-earth row is the most instructive

The bottleneck is not in the mine but in the processing. A country can have the ore and still depend on another country to turn it into something useful. When the test asks about resource dependence, the answer is rarely just “who has it”: it is usually “who controls some step of the chain.”

A warning for the whole block

Having a valuable resource does not guarantee prosperity. There are countries with enormous reserves and poor populations, and countries with almost nothing underground that are among the richest in the world. If you write “this country is rich because it has oil,” you have skipped several links: who owns the extraction, where the profit goes, and what is done with it are different questions, and they are the ones that decide.

2How to read a thematic map

A thematic map does not show the land: it shows a piece of data spread over the land: population density, wheat production, emissions per country, access to drinking water. It appears on the test almost every time.

Four steps to read a thematic map, in this order
StepWhy
1 · Read the title firstIt says what is being measured. Without it, the colors mean nothing.
2 · Then read the legendIt says what each color means and, above all, in what units. A map of total emissions is not a map of emissions per person.
3 · Only then look at the mapAlmost every mistake comes from looking at it first.
4 · Ask what it does not showA map by country paints each country one color, and inside a country the differences can be huge. It is the problem of the average from Lesson 7.2.

The classic trap: totals versus per person (made-up countries)

ACountry A emits 900 million tons of CO₂ a year and has 300 million people: 900 ÷ 300 = 3 tons per person.

BCountry B emits 150 million tons and has 10 million people: 150 ÷ 10 = 15 tons per person.

✓On a map of totals, A is the darkest; on a map per person, B is. Both maps are true. If a question gives you a map and a statement, first check that the statement uses the same units as the map.

Political borders and cultural borders

Two ways to divide the same land
Political borderCultural border
A line agreed on or imposed; it appears on maps.A zone of transition where language, religion, or customs change; it appears on no official map.
It can be drawn as a straight line on a desk, as many are in Africa and in the western United States.It follows rivers, mountains, old routes, and centuries of contact.
It changes with wars and treaties, on specific dates.It changes slowly, with no date, and sometimes not at all even when politics changes.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

When the two do not match, there is conflict or there is mixing, and sometimes both. A political border drawn through a culturally uniform region splits it in two; one drawn around several different regions makes them live together. Neither dooms anything by itself, but it explains many tensions that otherwise seem inexplicable.

3Two positions on the same policy

Now the skill of evaluating claims (practices SSP.5 and SSP.7). For that you need two texts that truly disagree, written by people who took the question seriously. This is one: a divided Supreme Court decision from 2007 on whether the federal environmental agency (the EPA, Lesson 4.2) can and must regulate the greenhouse gases that cars emit. One side won 5 to 4, and both sides were written down.

📜 Document A · The majority

Case
Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency, 549 U.S. 497
Date
April 2, 2007 · decided 5 to 4
Author
Justice John Paul Stevens, for the majority
Kind of source
Primary · court opinion. Excerpts.

A well-documented rise in global temperatures has coincided with a significant increase in the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere. Respected scientists believe the two trends are related. […]

Because greenhouse gases fit well within the Clean Air Act’s capacious definition of “air pollutant,” we hold that EPA has the statutory authority to regulate the emission of such gases from new motor vehicles. […]

We hold only that EPA must ground its reasons for action or inaction in the statute.

Notice the last sentence, because it is almost always told wrong

The Court did not order the EPA to regulate. It ordered it to decide according to the law and to explain its decision.

📜 Document B · The dissent

Case
The same case and the same day
Author
Chief Justice John Roberts, dissenting, for four justices
Kind of source
Primary · dissenting opinion. Excerpts.

Global warming may be a “crisis,” even “the most pressing environmental problem of our time.” […] It is not a problem, however, that has escaped the attention of policymakers in the Executive and Legislative Branches of our Government, who continue to consider regulatory, legislative, and treaty-based means of addressing global climate change.

Apparently dissatisfied with the pace of progress on this issue in the elected branches, petitioners have come to the courts claiming broad-ranging injury, and attempting to tie that injury to the Government’s alleged failure to comply with a rather narrow statutory provision. I would reject these challenges as nonjusticiable. Such a conclusion involves no judgment on whether global warming exists, what causes it, or the extent of the problem.

Read the last sentence again: the whole section depends on it

It is very tempting to sum up this 5-to-4 as “some believe in climate change and some do not.” The text says otherwise, in writing. The dissent calls it a possible “crisis” and says expressly that it makes no judgment on whether it exists. What they argue about is who decides: whether a court can make an agency act on a harm that affects the whole world, or whether that belongs to the elected branches.

What has happened since, as of November 2026

After the decision, in 2009, the EPA issued an “endangerment finding” that greenhouse gases threaten public health, and used it to regulate vehicle emissions. On February 12, 2026, the EPA rescinded that finding for vehicles and repealed the standards based on it, arguing mainly that the Clean Air Act does not give it that authority; the rule took effect on April 20, 2026. Health and environmental groups and a coalition of states sued, and the cases are pending; they may reach the Supreme Court again. The issue is a live political dispute. What this lesson asks is not your position on it, but that you can read the two arguments of 2007 and recognize them when they reappear, because they are reappearing now.

4Defending the position that is not yours

Here is the exercise that closes Topic 7: take the position you disagree with and build its best version. It is not a game of rhetoric. It is the only way to know you understood the issue, because a position you know only in its weakest form has not been understood: it has been caricatured. And it comes up on the test: you will get two texts and be asked what each one holds, not which you like better.

Four rules for a debate where evidence is required
RuleWhat it means in practice
Only the evidence in the textEvery claim must be something you can point to in the document. What you know from elsewhere, even if true, does not count today.
The best version, not the easiestDefend the strongest argument of your side, not the silliest. If your summary of the other side sounds foolish, it is not a summary: it is a caricature.
One strong point of the other sideBefore finishing, say one thing the other side is right about. If you cannot find one, go back to the text: you have not read enough.
Separate the real disagreementName what the two texts agree on and what they do not. They almost always agree on more than it seems, and the real disagreement is narrower and more interesting.

The course will not tell you who is right

This lesson teaches you to read both texts, say what each one holds and with what, and recognize a strong point of the other side. What you think afterward, with the texts in front of you, is your business, and that is exactly the difference between studying a topic and being told about it.

6Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • One product, one chain (20 minutes). Choose an object you use every day (a phone, a car, a cable). Name one resource it needs and draw the three-link chain for it. Check: the third link names who depends on whom.
  • Two maps (20 minutes). Find a map of CO₂ emissions by country and another per person. Name one country that changes place between them. Check: read the title and the units first.
  • The other side (30 minutes). Write the best version of the 2007 position you agree with less, using only Documents A and B. End with one point the other side gets right. Check: someone who holds that view should be able to sign your paragraph.

📘Key words

Natural resource
Something taken from nature that people use: water, minerals, oil, land.
Dependence
Needing another country for a resource or a step of its processing.
Thematic map
A map that shows one piece of data spread over a territory.
Per capita
Per person: a total divided by the population.
Political border
A line agreed on or imposed between states.
Cultural border
A zone where language, religion, or customs change.
Sustainability
Using resources so that future generations can also meet their needs.
