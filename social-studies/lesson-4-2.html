Lesson 4.2 · The executive branch: the presidency, the cabinet, and the bureaucracy

Every presidential power comes with a brake attached, and the news calls three different things “a rule.”

Before you start

Lesson 4.1 followed a bill through Congress and stopped at the President’s desk: sign or veto. This lesson sits at that desk. It asks what else the President can do, what each power needs from the other branches, and what happens to a law once it is signed: who actually carries it out.

1What a President can do, and who he must count on

Article II is remarkably short compared with Article I. Almost no presidential power comes alone: almost every one comes with another branch attached to it.

Presidential powers and the limit attached to each one
PowerLimit
Sign or veto bills passed by CongressCongress can override a veto with two-thirds of each chamber
Appoint judges, ambassadors, and top officialsThe Senate must confirm them
Negotiate treaties with other countriesThe Senate must approve them by two-thirds, or they do not take effect
Command the armed forces as commander in chiefOnly Congress can declare war and approve the money
Grant pardons for federal crimesNot for state crimes, and not in cases of impeachment
Run the government and issue executive ordersOnly within the authority the Constitution or a law already gives; the courts can strike them down

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The pattern to see

Read the table by columns. The left looks like enormous power; the right trims it on every line. That is what Lesson 3.2 called checks and balances, now visible power by power. None of the six is used without counting on someone else.

The biggest limit: impeachment

The House accuses and the Senate judges, as you saw in Lesson 4.1; removing a President takes two-thirds of the Senate. The facts, in order: the House has impeached three Presidents (Andrew Johnson in 1868, Bill Clinton in 1998, and Donald Trump in 2019 and again in 2021). Richard Nixon resigned in 1974 before the House voted. The Senate has never convicted a President.

What that record teaches

Not that the mechanism does not work, but how it is set. Two-thirds of the Senate is a majority that is almost never reached without support from both parties, and that was on purpose: the founders wanted removing an elected President to be possible, but not easy or partisan. Whether the bar is too high or too low is a fair political question, and people defend both views. What this lesson asks is that you know where the bar is and why it was put there.

2Law, executive order, and regulation

This is the most common confusion of the whole topic, and the test checks it. The news does not help: it calls all three a “measure” or a “rule.” They are told apart by a single question: who made it?

The three tools, by who makes them and how hard they are to undo
ToolWho makes itHow it is undone
LawCongress passes it and the President signs itOnly with another law, which goes through the whole path of Lesson 4.1 again. Hard.
Executive orderThe President aloneThe next President can cancel it with another order. One signature. Easy.
Regulation (rule)An agency, within the authority a law gave itThe agency must go through the process again, with a public proposal and a comment period. Months or years. In between.

↔ Slide the table sideways to see all the columns.

The rule that settles almost every case

The more people it took to make something, the more people it takes to undo it. A law needed two chambers and a signature; an executive order needed one signature. That is why the executive order is a President’s fast tool, and the one most exposed to the next President.

None of the three can invent its own authority

An executive order creates no new powers: it only directs the use of powers the President already has under the Constitution or a law. A regulation cannot either: it always rests on a law that allowed that agency to regulate that subject. When one goes beyond that, courts can strike it down, which is the subject of Lesson 4.3. A current example: Congress created the Department of Education by law in 1979. In 2025 and 2026 the administration moved many of its programs to other departments and asked Congress to close it; because a law created it, only another law can abolish it.

About Executive Order 9066

In 1942 President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed it alone, and it led to the forced removal and imprisonment of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of them U.S. citizens. In 1988 Congress passed a law (the Civil Liberties Act) that apologized and paid compensation. It is here because a lesson about what a President can do with one signature would be incomplete without it.

3Who really carries out the laws

A law that passes does not carry itself out. Someone has to inspect, approve, pay, measure, and fine. That someone is the bureaucracy (the federal agencies), where the vast majority of federal employees work.

Fifteen departments make up the cabinet; the President names their heads (called secretaries, except the Attorney General, who leads Justice) and the Senate confirms them. Apart from them are the independent agencies, which write regulations within the authority a law gave them.

How to find the agency for a problem without memorizing the list

Ask what the problem is about and look for that word in the name. A medicine or a food? Health. An accident at work? Labor. A polluted river? Environment (EPA). A highway? Transportation. The test does not ask for the list by heart: it asks you to match a problem with whoever handles it.

5Homework

This is independent practice. Plan for about one hour, with your notebook open.

  • The practice page. Open the practice page for this lesson and do each activity. The exercises come in a new order every time. Keep going until you get 8 out of 10 right on the first try. The score under each activity keeps count for you.
  • The columns of power (20 minutes). Copy the table of section 1 with the right column covered. Write the limit of each power from memory. Check: every limit names another branch or level of government.
  • Three measures in the news (30 minutes). Find three government measures in this week’s news and classify each one as a law, an executive order, or a regulation, saying who made it. Check: if the story says “the agency proposed” or “public comments,” it is a regulation.
  • Your agencies (15 minutes). Write five problems from your daily life (a recall on a car seat, a dangerous workplace, a passport, a student loan, a polluted lot) and name the federal department or agency that handles each one. Check: look for the key word in the agency’s name.

📘Key words

Commander in chief
The President’s role as head of the armed forces.
Pardon
Forgiving a federal crime; the President cannot pardon state crimes or impeachment.
Executive order
A directive signed by the President alone, within powers he already has.
Regulation
A detailed rule written by an agency under the authority of a law, after public comments.
Cabinet
The heads of the 15 executive departments.
Bureaucracy
The departments and agencies that carry out the laws.
Independent agency
A federal agency outside the cabinet departments, such as the EPA or the FCC.
